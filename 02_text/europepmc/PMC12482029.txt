ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfertility-related stress adversely affects marital intimacy, decreases self-esteem, and decreases the rate of sexual contact. In consideration of the psychological distress and marital issues confronted by individuals with infertility, developing strategies that foster an intimate marital life is essential. This study reviewed counselling approaches and their impact on marital intimacy among women and couples experiencing infertility.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020. The search of studies was performed through databases such as Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, PubMed, PsycINFO and the Google Scholar search engine without time limitations until 16th July, 2025, using MeSH keywords of ((infertility OR infertile OR subfertile OR sterility OR subfertility OR childlessness)AND (“counselling” OR “psychosocial intervention” OR “psychological intervention” OR psychological OR “couples therapy” OR therapy OR psychotherapy) AND (“Marital intimacy” OR intimacy)). All randomized controlled trials and quasiexperimental studies regarding the effects of infertility counselling interventions on the marital intimacy of women and couples experiencing infertility were assessed by two independent researchers for quality via the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist.ResultsOut of 1649 studies retrieved from physical and electronic resources, 14 studies including a total of 550 patients were systematically reviewed in this study. The results revealed that various types of infertility counselling interventions, including self-regulatory couple therapy, group therapy on the basis of quality of life, relationship enrichment training, group counselling on acceptance and commitment therapy, psychological intervention, positive couple therapy program, reality therapy, Gottman couple-centered counselling, emotion-focused couple therapy and integrated behavioral approach improve marital intimacy.ConclusionDespite the positive effect of counselling interventions on marital intimacy in infertile couples, owing to the inability to conduct a meta-analysis, there is a need for more robust clinical trials to provide sufficient clinical evidence in this field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03430-4.Keywords: Infertility, Counselling, Marital intimacy, Systematic review

BODY
Introduction

Infertility is a medical condition characterized by the inability to conceive pregnancy after at least one year of regular unprotected intercourse [1]. It can be described as a significant life crisis [2]. Infertility is associated with several adverse psychological consequences. It also leads to sexual dysfunction, decreased intimacy, concerns about divorce and separation, and ultimately lower marital satisfaction and quality of life in infertile couples [3]. In addition, the negative psychological and physiological effects of diagnosis and treatment of infertility can have a detrimental effect on marital relationships [4]. One significant aspect that is influenced by the infertility of either of the spouses is marital intimacy [5].

Intimacy refers to a sense of closeness, similarity, and romantic or emotional connection with another person. It requires deep mutual understanding, the sharing of thoughts and feelings, and serves as evidence of friendship and shared bonds between individuals [6]. Intimacy between spouses is a central and essential aspect of marriage relationships [7]. It is considered a crucial element in the overall quality of marital relationships. This element is closely tied to the level of closeness between partners, mutual respect, affection, and sexual desire [8]. Couples with high levels of intimacy might have a greater ability to deal with issues and transitions in their relationships, thus leading to greater marital satisfaction [9]. The lack or inadequacy of marital intimacy is an indicator of distress in marital relationships [7]. On the other hand, a lack of intimacy in marriage inflicts uneasy consequences such as dissatisfaction with relationships, the disappearance of love and affection, and the absence of proper understanding in the relationships between spouses [10]. In fact, intimacy constitutes one of the central bases of an individual’s life satisfaction and dissatisfaction with life because it strengthens the couple’s commitment to marital relationships and is positively associated with marital compatibility [11].

Marital intimacy is essential for infertile couples [12]. Psychological stress related to infertility disrupts marital intimacy, lowers self-esteem, and lowers the frequency of sexual activity [13]. Available evidence has shown that levels of marital intimacy among fertile couples are significantly greater than those among couples who remain infertile for any reason [14]. Considering the psychological problems and marital issues they face, infertile couples need strategies to help them have a higher level of mental health and to have a married lifestyle with greater intimacy [15].

Several studies have examined the impact of counselling intervention methods on marital intimacy among fertile couples [5, 10, 11, 14–22]. In a clinical trial conducted by Masoumi et al. (2017), a seven-session marital relationship enrichment program was implemented on 50 infertile couples. The findings revealed a significant increase in the average score of marital intimacy in the intervention group [17]. In another study conducted by Koleshtajani et al. (2022), which included eight reality therapy counselling sessions for infertile couples, the intimacy of the couples improved after intervention [14].

While previous systematic reviews have extensively investigated the impact of counselling interventions on various marital outcomes in infertile couples—such as marital satisfaction, mental health, and pregnancy rates [23, 24]—a critical gap remains in the literature regarding marital intimacy. Specifically, no study has yet comprehensively evaluated the effectiveness of infertility counselling interventions on marital intimacy, a key part of a healthy relationship. Given the unique emotional and psychological challenges these couples endure, intimacy often becomes vulnerable, yet its systematic assessment has been overlooked. This review therefore addresses an urgent need by synthesizing evidence on how counselling interventions may preserve or enhance marital intimacy in women and couples experiencing infertility, offering clinicians and researchers clinical insights to bridge this gap.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (Ethical Code: [IR.MUMS.NURSE.REC.1402.074]). While ethical approval is generally not mandatory for systematic reviews, the review committee granted an ethics code for this study to ensure compliance with local research governance policies. While the study protocol was neither published in a journal nor registered in PROSPERO, all the research procedures adhered strictly to institutional ethical standards and applicable research guidelines.

Data sources and search strategy

A widespread search of online databases, including Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, PubMed, PsycINFO and Google Scholar search engines, as well as gray literature (conference abstracts), was performed without time limits by two independent researchers until 16th July, 2025, using the following MESH keywords: ((infertility OR infertile OR subfertile OR sterility OR subfertility OR childlessness) AND (counselling OR “psychosocial intervention” OR “psychological intervention” OR psychological OR “couples therapy” OR therapy OR psychotherapy) AND (“marital intimacy” OR intimacy)). A manual review of pertinent studies was conducted to identify other trials that the electronic search had not detected. The search strategy employed across the databases is detailed in Table 1. All the articles were read by two reviewers (MS, MM), and disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third senior reviewer (RLR). EndNote reference management software version 8 was used for the database search and data selection.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The PICO model, which includes patient, intervention, comparison, and outcome data, was utilized to outline the components of the clinical evidence. For this purpose, experimental and quasi-experimental studies with the following characteristics, whose full texts were accessible, were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis: P) the population included women or couples experiencing infertility (infertility was defined as the failure to achieve a clinical pregnancy after 12 months or more having regular unprotected sexual intercourse); I) the intervention involved various modes of counselling, including individual, couple, and group counselling, aiming to enhance marital intimacy; C) the control group consisted of infertile patients who received just routine care; and O) the outcome measures focused on the assessment of marital intimacy.

The exclusion criteria included noninterventional studies, review articles, commentaries, letters to the editors, conference abstracts/ proceedings and studies whose full texts were not available.

Data extraction

The main characteristics of the studies included in this systematic review were as follows: author’s last name, year, and country of the population sample; study design; sample size; intervention characteristics; intervention format; number of sessions; intervention type and intervention duration (weeks); effectiveness of interventions according to the outcome measure (marital intimacy); and the tool through which they were measured. Data were extracted from each study by two independent reviewers (MS and MM), and differences between the investigators were settled by discussion with a third senior reviewer (RLR).

Notably, to handle missing data, a standard procedure was implemented. For studies where only the abstract was available or the full text could not be retrieved through our search channels, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors twice via email to request the full manuscript or the necessary data. If we did not receive a response within two weeks, the study was excluded from the final analysis due to insufficient data for quality assessment and data extraction. This is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram. Additionally, when key data (such as primary outcomes, baseline characteristics, or methodological details) were not reported in the included studies, the corresponding authors were similarly contacted via email to request the missing data. Additionally, in the data extraction sheets and subsequent tables (studies characteristics and quality assessment Tables), any missing data that could not be obtained from the study authors were clearly labelled ‘Not reported’ or ‘description not available’ to indicate their unavailability. This approach was taken to ensure transparency and minimize potential reporting bias.

Quality assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist was used with two different versions of experimental and quasi-experimental studies to determine the quality of the articles. The checklist had 13 items used to assess the quality of the experimental studies. The evaluation checklist of the quasi-experimental studies included 9 items. All the responses were marked as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear” or “Not applicable”. for each item. After the assessment, the studies are rated as high, medium or low if they score more than 75%, between 50% and 75%, or less than 50% of the total points, respectively [25]. Because a thorough qualitative synthesis was conducted, no studies were excluded on the basis of the quality assessment score. Two researchers (MS and MM) independently assessed the quality of the studies. Our quality assessment process therefore explicitly evaluated whether the authors of each primary study identified potential confounders and employed appropriate methods to control for them (e.g., through study design, matching, or statistical adjustment).

Data synthesis

Owing to clinical heterogeneity in interventions (various counselling approaches, different follow-up periods, diverse measurement tools, and variability in intervention durations and session numbers), we could not perform a meta-analysis. Instead, we synthesized findings narratively and categorized studies by type of counselling approach. To synthesize the data, following the tabulation of the extracted data, a qualitative synthesis using a narrative description of the extracted data was performed for systematic review. Notably, ethical considerations in conducting a systematic review were also considered.

Results

Search results

To compile the present study, 1649 articles were first identified through searching databases according to the study question; among them, 87 duplicated articles were removed, and 1562 records were screened for further review. A total of 1519 articles were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria; 43 full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility. Among the remaining articles, 24 studies were excluded for having irrelevant outcomes or interventions, 3 articles were excluded because they were reviews, and two articles were excluded because their full-text did not have access. Finally, 14 articles were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

Table 2 provides information on selected characteristics of the 14 studies included in the systematic review. All these studies were published between 2013 and 2025. Among the total number of studies, 13 were carried out in Iran, and 1 was carried out in South Korea. In summary, these studies included 11 types of counselling interventions for infertile patients in the field of improving marital intimacy, including self-regulatory couple therapy, group therapy based on quality of life, relationship enrichment training, group counselling based on acceptance and commitment therapy, psychological intervention, positive couple therapy program, reality therapy, gottman couple-centered counselling based on GATHER principles, emotion-focused therapy, integrated behavioral approach and a kind of infertility counselling. Among the 14 studies, eight used couple counselling, and sex studies employed a group infertility counselling approach. The total sample size was 550 patients, with a range from 15 to 70 patients per study.

Among the 14 reviewed studies, 11 studies were clinical trials, and only three studies were quasi-experimental studies; these studies were of lower quality than clinical trials because of the lack of random assignment or the lack of a control group. The findings of this systematic review led to the classification of data related to marital intimacy into two categories: group and couple counselling interventions. Group counselling interventions

Group counselling, as seen in the articles under review, was extremely prevalent, whereby intragroup treatment modalities, positive intermember interactions, and interventions from a well-educated and informed leader to effect changes in nonadaptive feelings, thoughts, and behavior were used. The optimum number of group members was eight to 12. The therapists most often run sessions weekly. Sessions were held for two hours, but the consultant had to implement a cut-off. In infertility clinical counselling, group counselling is initiated by individuals and couples who wish to see other cases with the same problems as theirs [31]. The groups used for marital intimacy in infertile men and women were as follows: group therapy on the basis of quality of life, group counselling on the basis of acceptance and commitment therapy, psychological intervention, and reality therapy sessions on the basis of Glasser’s choice theory.

Group therapy on the basis of quality of life Studies have shown that the negative psychological effects of infertility are greater for women than for men. Women may have problems with their marital intimacy. One of the treatment approaches that can affect this component is group therapy on the basis of quality of life, which is a new treatment in the field of positive psychology. In this model, quality of life is enhanced by creating cognitive‒behavioral changes in five main concepts, including living conditions, attitudes, pleasure criteria, values, and overall satisfaction. In the study of Adl et al. (2016), which focused on marital intimacy in infertile women, women in the intervention group were treated with a group psychotherapy approach on the basis of their quality of life in weekly 90-minute sessions for 5 weeks. The Bagarozzi Marital Intimacy Scale was completed before, after and one month after the completion of the sessions. This study revealed that quality of life program group therapy improved marital intimacy [11].

Group counselling based on acceptance and commitment therapy is another group counselling approach that has been used to improve the marital intimacy of infertile patients. The acceptance and commitment therapy approach to psychological intervention can be defined in terms of six normal psychological processes that revolve around a single core concept. They include experiential avoidance/acceptance; cognitive fusion/defusion; attentional rigidity to the past and future/being present; conceptualized self/noticing self; unclear, compliant, or avoidant motives/values; and inaction, impulsivity, or avoidant persistence/committed action [32]. Naghavi et al. (2020) applied acceptance and commitment therapy for 15 sessions of 1.5 h and then evaluated marital intimacy via the Bagarozzi Marital Intimacy Questionnaire (2001) in both the intervention and control groups. The results showed that group counselling based on acceptance and commitment therapy can be used as an effective psychological intervention for the psychological flexibility and marital intimacy of infertile women [15].

Psychological intervention is another group counselling approach that has been used in this field. Kim et al. (2020), in their research on infertile women undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF), adopted a programme of psychological and physical components once weekly for six weeks and structured a session for 4 h. Participants had opportunities to convey the various emotions they experienced during the psychological diagnosis and treatment of infertility. The individuals were capable of legitimizing their negative emotional experiences faced in personal and relational spheres, such as uncertainty and anxiety, while at the same time recognizing their irrational beliefs and cognitive distortions. This session also provided individuals with the ability to search for and express gratitude and effectively communicate about marital intimacy and sexual relationships, thus enabling a process of physical and mental reconstruction through self-reflection. Through guided imagery and musical activities such as listening, singing, and expressing, physical elements helped them in their emotional sharing, comfort, and physical relaxation. The results revealed that psychological intervention in the marital and personal dimensions can strengthen marital intimacy [19].

Reality therapy and emotion-focused therapy are other group approaches that have been utilized. Reality therapy focuses on what is occurring in the present and considers cultural diversity, assisting an individual in taking responsibility and the right and wrong things in life to resolve problems [33]. Emotionally focused therapy, developed by Leslie Greenberg and colleagues, is a humanistic treatment approach that prioritizes emotional processing and transformation as the primary mechanism of therapeutic change [34]. Jamshidi Koleshtajani et al. (2020), in a study on marital intimacy among infertile women, used reality therapy as eight 90-minute sessions with subsequent sessions for training identifying the five basic needs, desired world and wishes, general behaviors and creativities, couple conflict resolution, destructive behaviors, and intimacy methods. Vanden Broucke and Hanss Bertommen’s (1995) intimacy questionnaire was completed before and 40 days after intervention. These findings indicated that reality therapy can be utilized as a useful intervention to ameliorate the marital intimacy of infertile women [14]. Habibollahi Najafabadi et al. (2023) examined the comparative effectiveness of an emotion-oriented approach and reality therapy in enhancing marital adjustment, marital intimacy, and sexual function among infertile women in Isfahan, Iran. Using a quasi-experimental design with pretest/posttest measures, 45 participants were randomly assigned to three groups: an emotion-oriented approach (8 weekly 90-minute sessions), reality therapy (60 weekly 80-minute sessions), and a control group. The results demonstrated that, compared with the control group, both interventions significantly improved marital intimacy (p < 0.001), with emotional-focused therapy showing superior effectiveness (p < 0.001) [27]. This experimental study by Rahmanian et al. (2023) examined the effects of emotion-focused therapy delivered in eight 90-minute sessions on 34 infertile women in Tehran (17 receiving emotion-focused therapy, 17 controls). Using a pretest/posttest design with validated questionnaires, the results demonstrated that emotion-focused therapy significantly reduced infertility stigma and negative perceived stress while improving marital intimacy [26].

Couple counselling interventions

Couple counselling is employed to enhance marital intimacy in the lives of infertile men and women through different treatment modalities, such as self-regulation couple therapy, relationship enrichment, positive couple therapy, Gottman couple-centered counselling according to GATHER principles, and emotion-focused couple therapy.

Self-Regulating Couple Therapy is one of the counselling approaches used in studies to improve marital intimacy. Self-regulating couple therapy In a clinical trial performed by Kazemian et al. (2013), in 20 infertile couples, self-regulating couple therapy in 8 sessions of 90 min per week enhanced the marital intimacy of infertile women in the experimental group in comparison with the control group (P < 0.001) [16].

Relationship enrichment is another couple counselling intervention that is used to increase marital intimacy among infertile couples. The Enrichment program aims to strengthen interpersonal relationships among couples and could determine the factors and conditions under which marital satisfaction and compatibility can be predicted after marriage. Masoumi et al. (2017) employed a couples’ relationship enrichment model, which consisted of seven 90-minute sessions as a group with the participation of couples (men and women together) twice weekly and one session focusing on sexual relationship promotion separately for men and women. Marital intimacy questionnaires were completed by both groups before the intervention, immediately after the end of the training sessions and 8 weeks after the intervention. The mean marital intimacy score immediately after (P = 0.04) and eight weeks following the intervention (p < 0.001) significantly improved [17]. Poorheidari et al. (2016) used a marital relationship enrichment program comprising seven 90-minute training sessions for 36 infertile couples with domestic violence who were referred to the infertility center. The data were gathered through a marital intimacy questionnaire, which was filled out by both groups prior to and immediately and two months postintervention. The results indicated that relationship enrichment enhanced marital intimacy [18].

Positive couple therapy and integrative behavioral couple therapy were also among the other group couple therapy methods used in the studies. This is a useful method to help partners work through their marriage issues. This approach is part of couple therapy and is effective in resolving problems between couples [35]. Positive psychology focuses on three areas of human experience to help people define the field and orientation of positive psychology: the mental level, the individual level and the social level [36]. Integrative behavioural couple therapy is an evidence-based approach to couples therapy that combines traditional behavioral techniques with emotional acceptance strategies developed by Andrew Christensen [37]. In 2021, Vahidi Farashah et al. conducted a quasi-experimental study to compare the efficacy of positive couple therapy and integrative behavioral couple therapy for empathy and marital intimacy among 45 infertile couples in Shiraz, Iran. The participants were randomly assigned to three groups (two intervention groups, one control), with each therapy delivered in eight 90-minute weekly sessions. Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that both interventions significantly improved empathy (F = 116.81, p < 0.001) and intimacy (F = 107.92, p < 0.001), although positive psychotherapy had greater effects (p < 0.01) [22].

Gottman’s approach based on GATHER principles involves couples therapy with the purpose of helping partners achieve a deep understanding of cognition, awareness, and empathy [38]. This approach aims to help bring about changes in their cognitions, perceptions, and behaviors [39], with a main emphasis on helping couples rebuild and strengthen friendly relationships between them. This method ultimately creates true intimacy and encourages interpersonal growth [38]. In the study of Hosseinpoor et al. (2022), 60 couples facing infertility attended a specialized center for assistance. They participated in eight sessions of counselling focused on couples, using GATHER principles as a guide. Researchers measured couples’ closeness via the intimacy scale by Walker & Thompson, who collected data before the counselling started and again four weeks after it finished. The study revealed that this counselling significantly improved the intimacy between the couple [20].

Emotionally focused couple therapy is another approach that is used in infertile couples. Sadri et al. (2017) conducted a study involving 18 couples struggling with infertility. These couples were divided into two groups. The intervention group attended 10 weekly sessions, each lasting 90 min, focused on emotionally focused couple therapy. To measure intimacy levels, the researchers used the Walker & Thompson intimacy scale both before and after the therapy. These findings indicate that this type of therapy is effective in improving the marital relationships of couples dealing with infertility [30]. In the study by Asgari et al. (2025), the effectiveness of emotion-focused couple therapy on body image and marital intimacy in infertile women was investigated through a quasi-experimental pretest/posttest design with a control group. The sample consisted of 30 infertile women (15 per group) referred to specialists in Ahvaz, Iran, selected via purposive sampling. Participants in the experimental group received nine 90-minute weekly emotionally focused couple therapy sessions, while assessments were conducted via the Personal Assessment of Intimacy in Relationships (Schaefer and Olson, 1981, PAIR) inventory. The MANCOVA results revealed that emotionally focused couple therapy significantly improved marital intimacy compared with the control group (p < 0.05) [28].

Discussion

The present systematic review synthesized evidence on various counselling interventions aimed at enhancing marital intimacy among infertile couples. We explored a spectrum of approaches, ranging from group-based modalities (e.g., quality of life therapy, emotion-focused therapy) to couple-centered interventions (e.g., self-regulatory therapy, the Gottman method and positive couple therapy). The collective findings indicate that these psychosocial strategies hold consistent and positive efficacy in improving marital intimacy across diverse cultural settings. This finding reinforces the notion that addressing the psychological sequelae of infertility is as crucial as treating its medical aspects.

Group-based interventions targeting women, such as quality-of-life psychotherapy (Adl, 2016) [11] and emotion-focused therapy (Rahmanian, 2023 and Habibollahi Najafabadi, 2023) [26, 27], have consistently led to significant improvements in marital intimacy. These interventions often emphasize individual coping strategies, emotional regulation, and peer support. Notably, group settings provide a platform for shared experiences, which may have amplified therapeutic outcomes for women [40, 41].

In contrast, couple-centered interventions (e.g., Hosseinpoor, 2022; Kazemian, 2013) [16, 20] directly address the dyadic challenges of infertility. By focusing on communication skills, mutual support and empathy, as well as shared coping strategies (e.g., emotional focused therapy), work to mitigate the relational conflicts that often undermine intimacy. The observed gender disparity in outcomes, where males sometimes show less pronounced gains (Hosseinpoor, 2022), is a critical point. This underscores that a one-size-fits-all approach is insufficient. This disparity may stem from societal norms around masculinity and men’s emotional expression, a factor previously identified in studies on men’s ability to cope with infertility [42].

Comparative analyses revealed that while both modalities are effective, couple therapies may offer broader relational benefits by addressing systemic dynamics, whereas group interventions for women excel in individual empowerment. Future research should explore hybrid models to integrate these strengths.

The present study addresses a significant gap in the literature, as—to the best of our knowledge—no prior systematic review has specifically investigated the impact of infertility counselling on marital intimacy in infertile couples. This absence precludes a direct comparison of our findings with those of other systematic reviews on this topic. However, our results are broadly consistent with the existing body of evidence on psychosocial interventions for infertility, although critical nuances merit discussion.

The results of our systematic review corroborate those of prior meta-analyses, such as those of Alirezaei et al. (2022) and Frederiksen et al. (2015). A meta-analysis by Alirezaei et al. (2022) revealed that psychological interventions increase marital and sexual satisfaction [24]. Similarly, the review by Frederiksen et al. (2015) supports the efficacy of psycho-cognitive and psychosocial interventions in reducing marital distress and improving marital relationships [29].

However, a critical comparison with recent literature reveals nuances that merit further discussion. For instance, our findings are partially consistent with those of Shahrahmani et al. (2023), who reported significant improvements in marital compatibility and commitment but not in marital satisfaction following emotion-focused counselling [43]. This discrepancy underscores a crucial point: not all counselling interventions uniformly affect all dimensions of the marital relationship. It could be speculated that marital intimacy may share more conceptual ground with constructs such as compatibility and commitment than with global marital satisfaction, which might be influenced by a wider array of external factors beyond the scope of short-term interventions.

The overarching conclusion from a synthesis of the evidence, including the critical review mentioned [43], is that psychosocial interventions yield positive outcomes for couples with infertility. Nevertheless, our review critically appraises the mechanism behind this improvement. Rather than having a uniform effect, the benefits likely depend on the intervention’s theoretical orientation. For example, emotion-focused therapies primarily enhance emotional bonds and intimacy, whereas cognitive‒behavioral approaches target dysfunctional cognitions about infertility that fuel marital discord.

Counselling interventions are a beneficial adjunct to medical treatment, positively affecting psychological well-being and marital functioning. This is further reinforced by experimental studies [44–46]. El-Feshawy et al. (2023) conducted a randomized controlled trial with 136 infertile women, demonstrating that collaborative infertility counselling can be employed to increase the marital satisfaction of women undergoing IVF [44]. Latifnejad Roudsari et al. (2017) also reported that collaborative infertility counselling enhanced women’s marital satisfaction during IVF [45]. However, even here, a critical comparison is insightful. The work of Safaei Nezhad et al. (2020) presents a fascinating counterpoint; their study revealed that choice theory-based counselling reduced irrational parenthood cognition but did not improve overall marital quality [46]. This finding challenges the assumption of a direct trickle-down effect from cognitive change to global marital improvement and suggests that interventions may need to be more targeted to specifically enhance intimacy and relationship dynamics rather than focus solely on individual cognitions.

This study was not without limitations, the most significant being the inability to perform a meta-analysis due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies. This limitation is a critical commentary on the current state of the literature. The diversity in interventions, durations, and outcome measures makes synthesizing quantitative evidence difficult and necessitates a cautious interpretation of the results. We strongly recommend that future high-quality randomized controlled clinical trial studies, which are designed with larger samples, long-term follow-ups, and standardized, validated measures, be conducted to build a more robust and comparable evidence base specifically for marital intimacy outcomes. Second, a number of the included studies had incomplete reporting of methodological details and findings, leading to missing data during the extraction process. Despite efforts to contact the authors, some data remained unavailable and were recorded as ‘Not reported’ or ‘description not available’. This incompleteness of data may have impacted the robustness and overall interpretation of our findings. The third limitation is the relatively high number of studies with low methodological quality.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesized evidence on the effectiveness of various counselling interventions in enhancing marital intimacy among women and couples experiencing infertility. These findings consistently indicate that psychosocial interventions hold significant promise in mitigating intimacy-related challenges within infertile relationships, underscoring the importance of integrating counselling approaches into infertility care.

However, the considerable clinical heterogeneity in interventions (diverse counselling approaches, varying measurement tools, and different participant characteristics) precluded a meta-analysis, highlighting the need for more methodologically uniform and robustly designed trials. Future research should prioritize developing evidence-based, standardized protocols tailored to the unique relational dynamics of infertility. Furthermore, exploring the mechanisms through which counselling fosters intimacy—whether through improved communication, emotional regulation, or shared meaning-making approaches—could deepen our theoretical understanding and clinical efficacy. Ultimately, advancing this field aligns with broader trends in personalized, holistic infertility care that addresses not only medical but also relational well-being.
