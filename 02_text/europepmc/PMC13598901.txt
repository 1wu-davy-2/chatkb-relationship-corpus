ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluated changes in attachment behaviors among Spanish‐speaking couples participating in emotionally focused couple therapy (EFCT), using data from a randomized clinical trial of EFCT in this population. The study examined perceived secure attachment behaviors ‐accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement‐ measured by the Brief Accessibility, Responsiveness, and Engagement (BARE) scale, and their association with dyadic adjustment measured by the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS). Seventy couples were randomly assigned to EFCT or a waiting list; two withdrew before beginning the intervention, resulting in a final analyzed sample of 68 couples. Results showed some changes in attachment behaviors, with significant effects observed only in women's perceptions of their partners. Increases in both self‐ and partner‐perceived secure attachment behaviors significantly predicted improvements in dyadic adjustment. These findings highlight the clinical relevance of targeting secure attachment behaviors as mechanisms of therapeutic change related to relationship satisfaction.Keywords: attachment behaviors, brief accessibility responsiveness and engagement scale, dyadic adjustment, emotionally focused couple therapy, randomized clinical trial, Spanish‐speaking couples

BODY
Emotionally focused couple therapy (EFCT; Johnson 2004, 2020) has established itself as one of the two major models of couples therapy with strong empirical support (Beasley and Ager 2019; Lebow et al. 2012; Lebow and Snyder 2022; Spengler et al. 2024). Beasley and Ager (2019), in a meta‐analysis of nine randomized controlled trials (RCTs), found that EFCT significantly improves relationship satisfaction, with these results remaining stable over time. Similarly, Spengler et al. (2024) conducted a comprehensive meta‐analysis of 20 studies, further confirming EFCT's effectiveness in enhancing relationship quality and satisfaction.

Despite this robust evidence, most studies have historically prioritized dyadic adjustment and relational satisfaction as primary outcomes, leading to a relative underrepresentation of attachment‐related processes, which provide the major theoretical underpinnings of the model. This focus may be due to the longstanding reliance on relational satisfaction as the primary indicator of therapeutic success, as well as methodological challenges in assessing attachment‐related changes throughout the therapeutic process. More recently, EFCT research has increasingly sought to understand how changes in attachment contribute to therapeutic progress and long‐term relationship stability, primarily through longitudinal observational studies (Burgess Moser et al. 2016; Burgess Moser et al. 2018; Dalgleish et al. 2015; Dalgleish et al. 2015; Wiebe et al. 2017; Wiebe et al. 2017). This shift aligns with EFCT's foundational view of close relationships as attachment bonds, in which unmet attachment needs may become organized into negative interactional cycles that maintain relational distress, highlighting the importance of addressing attachment insecurity to foster lasting relational change (Johnson 2020).

Consequently, researchers have begun evaluating attachment as a key therapeutic outcome, often using measures like the Experiences in Close Relationships scale (ECR; Brennan et al. 1998). However, the ECR primarily assesses individuals' perceived attachment security within close relationships, and offers limited insight into relationship‐specific attachment processes, as only a small number of items explicitly refer to the romantic partner.

In this context, most studies have focused on global attachment styles or internal representations rather than on observable secure base behaviors, such as accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement (A‐R‐E). These behaviors represent core components of secure attachment interactions in intimate relationships. Accessibility and responsiveness were identified by Bowlby (1973) as key regulators of security and distress, referring to emotional availability and attuned responding to a partner's needs. Extending this framework, Johnson (2008a) conceptualized engagement as an interactional process specific to adult relationships, involving the active expression and reciprocation of emotional signals that foster bonding. Together, these behaviors facilitate proximity seeking, comfort provision, and bonding processes (Johnson 2004). Given that EFCT aims to reshape attachment bonds, incorporating direct assessments of attachment behaviors is essential for a more comprehensive evaluation of therapeutic outcomes.

Addressing this need, Burgess Moser et al. (2016) examined both attachment styles and observable attachment behaviors in 32 couples undergoing EFCT. Their work incorporated both self‐report measures of attachment (e.g., ECR; Brennan et al. 1998) and observational assessments of interactional processes during conflict resolution tasks (Secure Base Scoring System; SBSS; Crowell et al. 2002), providing a more integrated perspective on attachment change. Findings showed reductions in attachment avoidance over the course of therapy, while decreases in attachment anxiety were observed primarily in couples who achieved a key therapeutic change event ‐blamer softening‐ which facilitates emotional engagement and vulnerability within the model. In addition, improvements in secure base use and support behaviors ‐referring to partners' ability to seek comfort and to respond sensitively to each other's emotional needs‐ were associated with increases in relationship satisfaction.

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of addressing both internal attachment representations (attachment styles) and external interactional patterns (attachment behaviors) to comprehensively understand how EFCT facilitates lasting therapeutic change. This theoretical distinction is further supported by empirical findings from a cross‐sectional study with couples in a married relationship (Sandberg et al. 2017). In light of this, the development of tools capable of assessing attachment‐related behaviors enacted within couple interactions becomes essential. The Brief Accessibility, Responsiveness, and Engagement (BARE) Scale, introduced by Sandberg et al. (2012), provides such an assessment by capturing these core interactional dimensions (A‐R‐E), grounded in attachment theory and clinical practice, particularly within the framework advanced by Susan Johnson.

Psychometric evaluation has demonstrated its reliability, construct validity, and predictive utility for key outcomes such as relationship satisfaction (Sandberg et al. 2012; Sandberg et al. 2016), stability (Sandberg et al. 2012) and marital quality (Sandberg et al. 2017). Additionally, the BARE has been linked to a range of relational and individual factors, including couple communication, relational aggression and violence, video game and pornography use, family of origin dynamics, self‐regulation, self‐esteem, physical activity, and dietary behaviors (Knapp et al. 2015; Oka et al. 2014; Sandberg et al. 2016). However, although the BARE has been applied in clinical samples, no published studies to date have examined its use in Spanish‐speaking clinical populations. Furthermore, its use in psychotherapy research remains limited (Sandberg et al. 2016), particularly as an outcome measure for assessing therapeutic change over time, highlighting an important direction for future research.

To address these gaps, the present study contributes to the literature in two main ways: it focuses on observable attachment behaviors rather than global attachment styles in EFCT, and applies the BARE as a behavioral measure of A‐R‐E dynamics in an RCT with a Spanish‐speaking clinical population. To our knowledge, this is the first RCT within the EFCT attachment literature conducted in a non‐English‐speaking context, thereby extending EFCT outcome research beyond traditionally studied Anglo‐Saxon samples (Rathgeber et al. 2019) and complementing prior qualitative evidence on therapists' perceptions of the model's cultural appropriateness in Spanish‐speaking contexts (Sandberg et al. 2024).

Building on this, the present study aims to examine the impact of EFCT on perceived attachment behaviors in romantic relationships. Specifically, it seeks to: (1) Analyze pre–post changes and longitudinal trajectories in perceived secure attachment behaviors, including both self‐ and partner‐reported perceptions. (2) Examine the relationship between longitudinal changes in perceived secure attachment behaviors and dyadic adjustment over time.

Method

Study Design

The data for this study come from the E(f)FECTS project (Efficacy of Emotionally Focused Therapy among Spanish‐Speaking Couples: A Randomized Clinical Trial), a randomized trial of couple therapy conducted in a Spanish‐speaking context. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Universidad de Navarra (IRB approval code 2019.149). Prior to participation, all participants received information about the study aims, voluntary participation, and response confidentiality, and provided written informed consent.

A detailed explanation of the study methodology can be found in the published protocol on ClinicalTrials.gov (Rodríguez‐González and Anderson 2020) and in the published open‐access article (Rodríguez‐Gonzalez et al. 2022), where the participant flow from enrollment to allocation is fully described. Participants included 70 couples from Argentina (n = 18), Costa Rica (n = 8), Guatemala (n = 8), Spain (n = 16), and Mexico (n = 20). However, two couples (one from the control group and one from the treatment) left the study just after the group assignment, before starting the intervention. Therefore, our total sample was 68 couples. No further participant attrition occurred during the study; however, some participants did not complete all assessment measures at every time point, resulting in partially missing data.

Couples were required to have been in an exclusive relationship for at least 1 year, with both partners being 25 years or older, and to present mild to moderate relational distress. The latter criterion was operationalized using the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS; Spanier 1976), with an initial eligibility range of 80–100, informed by the original DAS normative data and later clinical cutoff recommendations, especially those reported in Spanish samples (Cáceres et al. 2013; Cano‐Prous et al. 2014). However, due to recruitment challenges, this criterion was slightly relaxed. Specifically, we included couples in which one partner scored between 63 (lowest) and 119 (highest) on the DAS. Nevertheless, the mean baseline score for both groups remained close to 90 points, consistent with the original criterion. The control group had a mean score of 95.98 (SD = 11.46), and the treatment group had a mean of 93.78 (SD = 12.01). A Welch's t‐test indicated that this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.275).

The mean age of female participants was 44 years (SD = 7.84), and of male participants 46 years (SD = 8.55), with no significant differences between groups (male: p = 0.528; female: p = 0.831). Most couples were married (n = 54), had cohabited for more than 5 years (n = 56), and had at least two children (n = 44), with no significant group differences in marital status (p = 0.536), relationship duration (p = 0.692), or number of children (p = 0.525). Most participants held a university degree (women: n = 51, men: n = 54) and were employed for at least 20 h per week (women: n = 56, men: n = 62), with no significant differences between groups (see Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

Procedure

Couples in the treatment group received an average of 18.8 EFCT therapy sessions (SD = 2.92, range = 8–20), each lasting approximately 75 min, with a weekly or biweekly frequency. The intervention was delivered by 17 therapists (16 psychologists and one psychiatrist). Twelve were EFCT‐certified, and the remaining five had completed the required EFCT training and were receiving supervision toward certification. Treatment fidelity was monitored through supervision and independent fidelity assessment; further details on the procedures and corresponding results are presented in the primary trial report (Sandberg et al. 2026). As part of EFCT case conceptualization, therapists identified the partners' withdrawn/avoidant and pursuing/anxious positions within the negative interactional cycle. Throughout the intervention, treatment‐group couples completed a set of questionnaires after each session. During the same period, the control group couples did not receive the study intervention, but received a weekly email with the same set of questionnaires. Assessment points were selected pragmatically to balance the need to capture change over time with the goal of minimizing participant burden. Couples in both groups were financially compensated for completing the questionnaires ($60 per hour), and control‐group couples were additionally offered free participation in a Hold Me Tight (HMT; Johnson 2008b) experiential workshop based on the EFCT model.

Measures

Brief Accessibility, Responsiveness, and Engagement Scale (BARE; Sandberg et al. 2012)

The BARE assesses attachment behaviors through three core dimensions: accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement, all of which are associated with secure attachment (Bowlby 1973; Johnson 2008a). This measure consists of 12 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging from “Never True” to “Always True.” It includes two subscales: (1) Me Scale, which evaluates self‐rated attachment behaviors, and (2) Partner Scale, which assesses one partner's perception of the other's attachment behaviors. Each subscale contains six items: two items per attachment dimension. Higher scores indicate stronger attachment‐related behaviors. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha based on the first administration of the instrument (Session 2). The analysis yielded coefficients of α = 0.75 for the Me Scale and α = 0.80 for the Partner Scale, indicating acceptable to good internal consistency within this clinical sample.

The BARE was administered in sessions 2, 5, 9, 13, 17, and 20 for the treatment group, and in times 2, 7, 13, 16, and 20 for the control group. The instrument was translated into Spanish by the research team using a collaborative translation process aimed at preserving conceptual and semantic equivalence.

Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS; Spanier 1976; Sabourin et al. 2005)

The DAS‐4 (Sabourin et al. 2005) is a four‐item measure that assesses relationship adjustment through ratings of disagreements and positive exchanges using Likert‐type scales. Higher scores reflect greater relationship satisfaction and adjustment. This measure has shown acceptable reliability across therapy sessions, with Cronbach's alpha values ranging from 0.70 to 0.85, supporting its suitability for repeated assessments. In our study, in the treatment group, the DAS‐4 was administered in sessions 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 13, 15, 17, and 19; in the control group, it was administered at time points 2, 4, 7, 13, and 18. Internal consistency in the present study, calculated at baseline (Session 0), was α = 0.60, which may be considered acceptable in the context of early‐stage or exploratory research in this population. The Spanish translation of the four items of the DAS‐4 was taken from the adaptation carried out by Cano‐Prous et al. (2014), which has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties in Spanish‐speaking populations.

Analyses

As a preliminary analysis, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to examine the internal structure of the BARE. A two‐factor model was specified, distinguishing between self‐ and partner‐related dimensions. Each factor was defined by six observed items, and residual covariances were allowed between item pairs belonging to the same subdimensions. The model was estimated using the WLSMV estimator, appropriate for ordinal data.

To assess baseline differences in the BARE scores at the session 2 assessment between the control group and the treatment group, the Mann–Whitney U test was used as the normality assumption was not met for all variables. Intragroup pre‐post comparisons assessed changes within each group (treatment or control) from pre‐ to post‐intervention, stratified by gender (Hypothesis A). These comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon matched‐pairs signed‐rank test. To examine whether the magnitude of these changes differed between groups, Wilcoxon/Mann‐Whitney rank‐sum tests for intergroup comparisons were used.

Multilevel modeling (MLM) was used to examine progressive changes in attachment behaviors throughout the intervention (Hypothesis A) and to analyze the association between longitudinal changes in attachment behaviors and dyadic adjustment (Hypothesis C). MLM is a statistical method designed for nested data structures (Tasca and Gallop 2009). In the present study, repeated measures over time (level 1) were nested within individuals (level 2), and individuals were further nested within couples (level 3). Regarding Hypothesis A, two models were analyzed, one with each of the dependent variables (BARE‐Me and BARE‐Partner). Both models included the following fixed effects: gender, group (intervention vs. control), session, and the session × group interaction. This last effect is the main focus of these analyses, since it indicates the differential change trajectories across groups. Regarding Hypothesis C, the dependent variable was dyadic adjustment (DAS4), and additional fixed effects were added for both attachment behavior variables (BARE‐Me and BARE‐Partner).

Reliable change index (RCI; Jacobson and Truax 1991) was used to assess clinically significant changes in attachment behaviors (Hypothesis B). An absolute RCI value of 1.96 or higher (p < 0.05) was considered indicative of significant change. This allowed classification into three categories: reliable improvement, no reliable change, and reliable deterioration.

Missingness was evaluated using logistic regression models predicting missing observations based on treatment condition, session, and their interaction. For pre‐post analysis and RCI calculations, missing data were handled using case‐wise deletion, meaning that participants with missing values in key variables were excluded. In contrast, multilevel models (MLM) were estimated using all available data under the assumption of missing at random (MAR), which allows the inclusion of participants with incomplete observations across time. All analyses were conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp 2023).

Results

Preliminary Analyses

Missing data were low across assessment points, ranging from 0% to 4.5% for both the DAS‐4 and the BARE. Logistic regression analyses showed no significant effects of treatment condition, session, or their interaction on the likelihood of missing observations (all ps > 0.05).

Results of the CFA indicated an acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.985, TLI = 0.979, RMSEA = 0.083, SRMR = 0.077). All factor loadings were statistically significant and ranged from moderate to high in magnitude (0.45–0.76). Overall, these findings provide preliminary support for the proposed structure of the BARE.

Discussion

Recent reviews indicate that no empirical studies to date have systematically examined changes in attachment behavior within randomized clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of EFCT (Beasley and Ager 2019; Lebow and Snyder 2022; Spengler et al. 2024). The findings presented in this study therefore contribute additional and needed empirical evidence by addressing a previously unexplored outcome variable ‐attachment behavior‐ and by doing so in a Spanish‐speaking population that has not been represented in EFCT research to date, thereby extending the model's empirical support beyond the traditionally studied Anglo‐Saxon samples (Rathgeber et al. 2019; Sandberg et al. 2024).

The change process in EFCT allows partners to engage in the experiential exploration and expression of their attachment needs, while also offering the opportunity to discover that these needs can be met by the other in ways that are more emotionally responsive and attuned than in previous relational experiences (Johnson 2004). Given this foundational view of romantic relationships as attachment bonds, assessing changes in attachment is essential to understanding the core mechanisms of change in EFCT. Although previous studies have emphasized the importance of this assessment, most have focused on internal representations of attachment styles using the ECR scale (e.g., Burgess Moser et al. 2016; Burgess Moser et al. 2018). To address this limitation, the present study incorporates the BARE scale as a measure of perceived secure attachment behaviors, aiming to examine its association with change trajectories in dyadic adjustment, as measured by the DAS‐4.

While we expected changes in perceived secure attachment behaviors to be more pronounced in the EFCT group than in the control group, both in pre‐post comparisons and in progressive changes over the course of therapy (Hypothesis A), this pattern was not consistently observed in our study. In the pre‐post analysis, between‐group comparisons revealed that the only statistically significant difference occurred in women from the EFCT group, specifically in their perceptions of their partners' attachment behaviors. Furthermore, both groups showed significant increases over time in partner‐perceived secure attachment behaviors. In the treatment group, these changes occurred progressively throughout the course of the intervention, while in the control group, comparable improvements were also observed over time, despite participants not having received the study intervention during the assessment period, as the HMT workshop was offered only after completion of post‐treatment measures.

These improvements in both groups may partly reflect non‐specific change processes, including spontaneous improvement or regression to the mean, given that participating couples presented moderate relational distress at baseline and may have entered the study during a particularly difficult period in their relationship. In addition, assessment‐reactivity effects may have occurred, whereby repeated relational assessments fostered self‐monitoring and reflection on one's own and one's partner's attachment‐related behaviors. Changes in family, work, or relational circumstances during the assessment period, as well as informal support or help‐seeking and professional support outside the study, may also have contributed to the improvements observed in the control group. Finally, the expectation of receiving the HMT program after completion of the assessment period may have generated positive anticipatory effects among control‐group participants.

Although the non‐significant interaction between session and treatment group indicates that the rate of change was statistically equivalent across both groups, these findings may still align with prior research conceptualizing attachment‐related behaviors as measurable relational processes (Mikulincer and Shaver 2007; Sandberg et al. 2012; Sandberg et al. 2016; Sandberg et al. 2017). However, the modest magnitude of between‐group differences may also reflect the relative stability of attachment‐related behaviors and the difficulty of modifying habitual relational patterns within a short‐term intervention.

The positive change observed exclusively in women's partner‐rated perceptions may be understood in relation to the clinical structure of EFCT. In this model, therapists often work first with the partner occupying the more withdrawn position in the negative interactional cycle to facilitate emotional re‐engagement and increase accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement within the relationship (Johnson 2004). In the present study, therapists reported that approximately 80% of partners occupying this withdrawn position were men. Thus, women may have perceived greater changes in their partners' attachment‐related behaviors because these were precisely the behaviors initially targeted in the intervention. This interpretation is also consistent with the distinction between self‐ and partner‐reports, as partner‐rated measures may capture observable relational changes that individuals themselves do not report to the same extent.

In addition, prior research suggesting that women may be more attuned to social and interpersonal cues (Yang and Girgus 2019) may help explain why these changes were more clearly reflected in women's perceptions of their partners. Gender socialization may also contribute to such differences by shaping how partners attend to and report subtle relational and emotional changes. At the same time, the limited favorable outcomes observed among men may be consistent with prior EFT research indicating that men are often slower to show change in the early stages of treatment, particularly when they occupy more withdrawn or disengaged positions in the interactional cycle; however, when shifts in engagement occur, they may have substantial relational impact (Schade et al. 2015). Therefore, the present findings are better interpreted in relation to partners' positions within the negative interactional cycle, differences between self‐ and partner‐reports, and the intervention's initial therapeutic focus, rather than as evidence of stable gender differences in responsiveness to EFCT.

These differences between informants also underscore the importance of considering the measurement characteristics of the BARE when interpreting the present findings. As Sandberg et al. (2012) point out, although each BARE subscale includes only two items per attachment dimension, this format is quick and convenient but may be more vulnerable to misinterpretation if a respondent misunderstands one of the questions. Moreover, the brevity of the instrument may limit its reliability and sensitivity to detect clinically meaningful change over time, particularly when change is expected to be gradual or subtle. We were not able to examine this possibility in our dataset; however, this limitation may have affected the reliability of some responses, particularly in cases where unexpectedly low scores were observed.

Regarding Hypothesis B, although a higher proportion of participants in the treatment group exhibited reliable improvement in self‐rated attachment behaviors (BARE‐Me), these differences were not statistically significant for either men or women as predicted. In the case of partner‐rated attachment behaviors (BARE‐Partner), only women in the treatment group demonstrated a marginally significantly greater proportion of reliable improvement compared to those in the control group. However, this effect was small and was not observed among men. Consistent with the interpretation discussed above, this pattern may partly reflect differences between self‐ and partner‐reports, as individuals tend to self‐enhance and overreport their own positive relational behaviors (Novak et al. 2018). From this perspective, the divergence between self‐ and partner‐rated outcomes observed in the present study may reflect systematic differences in reporting rather than an absence of behavioral change. In addition, changes in attachment‐related behaviors—particularly among men—may unfold more gradually over time and may not be fully captured within the assessment window of the present study.

Interestingly, the change observed in women's perceptions of their partner's attachment behaviors, although limited to the pre‐post analysis, resonates with findings from Burgess Moser et al. (2016), who reported improvements in secure base use and support behaviors during EFCT. These shifts reflected greater emotional attunement between partners and aligned closely with EFCT's central goal of enhancing accessibility and responsiveness within the attachment bond (Johnson 2020). Importantly, that study also suggested that some dimensions of attachment‐related change may depend on emotionally pivotal therapeutic events, such as blamer softening, which may help explain why short‐term or early‐stage assessments do not always capture consistent behavioral change.

Overall, while the trend in our data suggests potential treatment‐related gains in attachment‐related behaviors, the limited statistical support, particularly in the self‐rated domain, warrants a cautious interpretation of these results and highlights the need for further investigation into how, and for whom, these processes are most responsive to EFCT.

Finally, the findings strongly support our third hypothesis, which proposed that increases in secure attachment behaviors would be positively associated with greater improvements in dyadic adjustment. Results from the multilevel analysis indicated that both self‐rated secure attachment behaviors (BARE‐Me) and perceived partner behaviors (BARE‐Partner) were significant predictors of dyadic adjustment over time. That is, individuals who reported higher levels of secure attachment, both in their own behavior and in their perception of their partner's behavior, also reported greater satisfaction in their relationship.

These findings are related to those initially reported by Sandberg et al. (2012) during the concurrent validation of the BARE, where the scales demonstrated strong associations with relationship satisfaction, relational stability, and positive communication, as reported by both individuals and their partners in a cross‐sectional study with a non‐clinical sample. Subsequently, Sandberg et al. (2016) also confirmed that increasing attachment behaviors may likely have a positive influence on couple communication, satisfaction, and stability as well within clinical populations, with the BARE being able to effectively distinguish between community and clinical samples. Additionally, Sandberg et al. (2017) demonstrated that attachment behaviors measured by the BARE are stronger predictors of marital quality than attachment styles. Similarly, other studies have found that it may be that attachment behaviors can help to moderate existing negative patterns in couple relationships (Knapp et al. 2015; Oka et al. 2014).

Although these previous findings were obtained mainly using a relationship satisfaction measure that focused on specific domains—namely time spent together, expressions of love and physical intimacy, conflict resolution, communication, perceived equality, and overall satisfaction—rather than the widely used DAS, this pattern of results is consistent with the theoretical framework of EFCT, which posits that the development of secure emotional bonds is a central mechanism of change underlying improvements in relationship functioning. By empirically confirming the link between attachment‐related behaviors and perceived relationship quality in EFCT, in a longitudinal clinical trial, our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence supporting the attachment foundations of this model.

Our findings are also aligned with the central role of sensitive responsiveness in the development and maintenance of secure attachment bonds (Ainsworth et al. 1978). When individuals perceive their partners as more accessible, responsive, and engaged ‐core dimensions of the BARE—it is reasonable to expect a positive impact on how they experience the relationship, as reflected in higher levels of dyadic adjustment. Furthermore, this consistent evidence supports the idea that EFCT enables therapists to foster the creation or strengthening of a secure bond between partners (Johnson 2020), and they also reinforce a key result in this research field: that the degree of security or insecurity in the attachment bond significantly influences how individuals experience their relationship (Ortiz Barón et al. 2002; Kuncewicz et al. 2021).

Taken together, the results of this study provide mixed but clinically meaningful evidence regarding changes in perceived secure attachment behaviors during EFCT. Although the findings do not offer strong evidence of consistent between‐group differences, the association between self‐ and partner‐perceived attachment behaviors and dyadic adjustment observed in the treatment group suggests that attachment‐related processes may play a meaningful role in relationship satisfaction among Spanish‐speaking couples. More importantly, these findings extend the existing evidence base for EFCT to a culturally diverse Spanish‐speaking population and support the relevance of examining attachment‐related behaviors as relational process variables within this model. At the same time, the limited support for EFCT's impact on perceived attachment behaviors highlights the complexity of measuring change in constructs that may be shaped by gendered relational positions, perception, response sensitivity, and cultural factors, particularly in a population in which the BARE had previously been examined only in cross‐sectional designs (Knapp et al. 2015; Oka et al. 2014; Sandberg et al. 2012; Sandberg et al. 2016; Sandberg et al. 2017).

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the sample size, although comparable or larger than that of other couple therapy trials, may have limited the power to detect small effects, especially in variables where significant between‐group differences were not observed. Replication with larger samples is necessary to confirm these findings and improve statistical precision.

Second, although the sample included couples from diverse Spanish‐speaking regions (North, Central, and South America, and Spain), the heterogeneity of the broader Spanish‐speaking population limits the generalizability of our findings. Most participants reported medium‐to‐high socioeconomic status and educational attainment, suggesting a potential sampling bias. Moreover, all participating couples were different‐sex couples, while participants' sexual orientation was not formally assessed. This limits the generalizability of the findings to other couple configurations and sexual orientations. Additionally, the absence of a cross‐cultural comparison group further limits conclusions about cultural generalizability. Future studies should aim for greater representation across sociocultural, economic, educational, relational, and sexual orientation subgroups and may incorporate acculturation‐related variables to better capture within‐group variability.

Third, while some of the instruments used have been validated in certain Spanish‐speaking populations, their applicability across the broader Latin American context remains uncertain. Specifically, the DAS (Spanier 1976) has been validated for use in Spain (Cano‐Prous et al. 2014), and examined in samples of Chilean (Kinkead et al. 2023) and Hispanic American couples (Youngblut et al. 2006), although these populations do not fully correspond to the Latin American countries represented in our study. However, the short version DAS‐4 (Sabourin et al. 2005) has not yet been explored in diverse populations beyond the French‐Canadian context in which it was validated. Similarly, the BARE (Sandberg et al. 2012) has widely demonstrated its reliability in Anglo‐Saxon contexts but has not undergone formal validation in Spanish‐speaking populations. Despite these concerns, both instruments demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in our sample (e.g., α = 0.60 for DAS‐4; α = 0.75 for BARE Me and α = 0.80 for BARE Partner), supporting their reliability in this context. Future research should nonetheless explore the cultural and linguistic equivalence of these measures through targeted validation studies in underrepresented Spanish‐speaking settings, including analyses of factorial structure, measurement invariance, semantic equivalence, and responsiveness to change.

Fourth, while the trial protocol includes a follow‐up phase, these data were not analyzed in the present study. Therefore, the current findings are limited to the intervention period and do not allow us to determine whether changes in perceived attachment behaviors were maintained, emerged gradually, or became more clearly differentiated between groups after treatment completion. This is particularly relevant given that attachment‐related behaviors may require time to consolidate within the couple relationship and may not be fully captured during the active intervention period alone.

Lastly, although therapy took place in clinicians' regular practice settings, enhancing ecological validity compared to highly controlled university‐based trials, participation in a randomized clinical trial inherently introduces context effects. Being aware of being recorded and receiving free therapy may have influenced participant behavior. Additionally, the use of a randomized sample may have introduced similar effects across participants, all of whom met the same distress criteria at baseline, regardless of whether they would typically be classified as part of a community or clinical population. Future studies should continue to balance methodological rigor with ecological realism.

In addition to the aforementioned considerations, and contingent upon the validation of the BARE in Spanish‐speaking populations, future research could explore its potential utility as a tool for routine outcome monitoring (ROM) in couple therapy. Given its brevity, ease of administration, and potential usefulness for tracking clinically relevant relational processes, the BARE may be particularly well‐suited for monitoring fluctuations in accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement throughout the course of treatment. Specifically, studies should assess whether repeated BARE administration is sensitive to session‐by‐session change and whether BARE‐informed feedback contributes to clinical decision‐making. Nevertheless, it is important to highlight, as noted by Sandberg et al. (2016), that although the BARE is a valid and reliable tool for clinical use, it does not offer therapists direct guidance on in‐session interventions. Further observational research is also needed to identify how specific therapist behaviors contribute to the development of bonding moments during therapy and to establish sequential links between therapist actions and client change.

First, studies could combine the BARE with more comprehensive measures of attachment security to clarify how moment‐to‐moment behavioral expressions relate to broader attachment functioning. Second, more longitudinal studies, including follow‐up assessments, are needed to assess the instrument's sensitivity to change across the course of therapeutic intervention and after treatment completion in diverse populations. Third, integrating BARE me‐ and partner‐reports with observational data could help assess the extent to which perceived attachment behaviors align with observed patterns of interaction, as self‐report measures alone may not fully capture relational process dynamics. Fourth, the BARE's use as a real‐time process tool could be explored, including the impact of item‐level feedback on therapeutic decision‐making. Finally, future intervention research may investigate whether tailoring treatment strategies based on distinct BARE profiles improves outcomes by offering more personalized therapeutic pathways.

Clinical Implications

The results yielded in our study have several clinically meaningful insights for practitioners working with Spanish‐speaking couples. First, given that this is, to our knowledge, the first RCT of EFCT conducted in a Spanish‐speaking context, the findings provide initial empirical support for this model in a cultural context different from the predominantly Anglo‐Saxon settings previously studied. Second, although observed changes in attachment‐related behaviors were more modest and at times statistically inconsistent, there was a general trend toward increased secure behaviors over time, which suggest the relevance of EFCT in enhancing emotional bonding. Considering the significant improvements observed in women's perceptions of their partners' attachment behaviors, it is plausible to assume that observable changes may have occurred in men during the intervention, even if these were not self‐reported. For clinicians, such findings may point to a new focus of change that aligns more closely with women's perspectives and could support men in analyzing and reflecting on their own attachment behaviors. Overall, these results suggest that partner reports may provide clinically useful information about observable behavioral change within the couple interaction.

Third, the stable association between secure attachment behaviors and dyadic adjustment reinforces the therapeutic value of addressing both emotional bonding and relational functioning. This pattern aligns with the theoretical model underlying EFCT, which posits that fostering secure emotional connection is a central mechanism of change. Individuals who reported higher levels of secure behaviors also reported greater relationship satisfaction over time. Accordingly, therapists should attend not only to global relational indicators (e.g., satisfaction, cohesion, consensus, as measured by the DAS), but also to the observable behaviors that signal secure attachment, namely accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement, as essential therapeutic targets.

For instance, accessibility can be strengthened by helping partners become more emotionally available, reachable, and open during moments of relational distress. Responsiveness can be fostered by supporting partners in recognizing attachment needs, validating emotional experience, and responding contingently rather than defensively. Engagement can be promoted by encouraging active emotional involvement, sustained attention, and willingness to remain present in vulnerable interactions. Strengthening these patterns may not only enhance emotional bonding but also contribute to broader improvements in relationship satisfaction. In this regard, given that many couples pursue therapy or relationship education to enhance the quality and stability of their partnerships, practitioners may find it valuable to closely examine the couple's attachment behaviors, as it has shown to be a strong predictor of key relational outcomes. Targeting attachment processes through specific behavioral expressions offers a promising pathway for addressing a range of presenting concerns in couple therapy, particularly when integrated with established relational goals.

Furthermore, these behavioral dimensions—specifically accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement, as measured by the BARE—may also serve as clinically relevant reference points during intervention. Because the BARE is a published full‐access instrument, its items can be used to illustrate and model in session what accessible, responsive, and engaged behaviors may look like in everyday couple interactions. Items from the BARE, such as “My partner finds it hard to get my attention” or “I listen when my partner shares deeply personal feelings”, can guide therapists in identifying and monitoring key interactional patterns. In this way, the BARE may have potential value as a brief ROM‐informed tool, providing feedback that can support session‐by‐session reflection on relational change.

Conclusions

This study represents the first attempt to examine changes in attachment‐related behaviors using the BARE within a Spanish‐speaking population, embedded in a randomized controlled trial of EFCT. The inclusion of a control group supports a more rigorous examination of intervention‐related effects alongside natural relational fluctuations over time, compared with prior research on attachment dynamics during EFCT that lacked control conditions (e.g., Burgess Moser et al. 2016; Burgess Moser et al. 2018; Wiebe et al. 2017). Importantly, the present study constitutes the first longitudinal application of the BARE in this area of research, extending its traditional use as a cross‐sectional measure (Sandberg et al. 2012; Sandberg et al. 2016; Sandberg et al. 2017). Taken together, these findings provide an initial basis for further examining the BARE's sensitivity to attachment‐related behavioral change over time, while offering some evidence of its predictive relevance to dyadic adjustment in couple therapy research.
