ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe phenomenon of violence, which can be seen at every stage of human life, is a mental and public health problem and violates human rights.The study was conducted to examine the effects of university students’self-esteem and belief in dating violence myths on their attitudes towards dating violence.MethodsThe study had a descriptive design and was conducted online with the participation of 1305 university students between June 3 and March 26, 2025. The data were collected using a questionnaire form that included the Personal Information Form, Two-Dimensional Self-Esteem Scale, Dating Violence Myths Scale, and Dating Violence Attitudes Scale. The SPSS V-27.0 software was used to evaluate the data.ResultsUniversity students scored 57.88 ± 9.89 on the total of the Two-Dimensional Self-Esteem: Self-Love/Self-Efficacy Scale, 22.75 ± 9.80 on the Dating Violence Myths Scale, and 117.22 ± 18.08 on the Dating Violence Attitudes Scale. The Self-Esteem Scale explained 1.3% of the variance in the Dating Violence Attitudes Scale. Then, using the Hierarchical Regression Model, the total of the Dating Violence Myths Scale was included in the model, and the result suggested that the total of the Two-Dimensional Self-Esteem and Dating Violence Myths Scale explained 21.5% of the variance in dating violence attitude.ConclusionThe study concluded that students do not support dating violence, have moderate self-esteem, and hold few myths about dating violence. Based on the results, awareness about dating violence needs to be increased and training programs expanded.

BODY
Introduction

Violence, which has been a psychical and public healthcare concern throughout human history and is also considered a human rights violation in many societies, is a phenomenon that can be seen at every stage of the human life cycle (from the mother’s womb to death). Because this phenomenon of violence has many dimensions, it is quite difficult to provide a general definition. For this reason, there is more than one definition of violence [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, violence is “The intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person, or against a group or community, that either results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment or deprivation” [3]. According to UNHCR (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees), gender-based violence is “violence directed against a person because of their gender or sex.” Although women and girls make up the vast majority of victims of gender-based violence, men and boys can also be harmed [4].

Dating is a state of closeness and attachment between two people that develops through emotional, psychological, and sexual attraction. In dating relationships, alongside feelings like love, happiness, and romance, emotions such as anxiety and unease can also occur, depending on the duration and nature of the relationship [1]. Dating violence is a form of interpersonal violence perpetrated by a dating partner or former partner that causes physical, sexual, and psychological harm [1, 2, 5]. Dating violence is a significant psychological and social problem that is particularly prevalent between the ages of 16 and 25 and poses a serious threat to an individual’s life [6, 7]. Studies have shown that dating violence is quite risky and widespread among young people of college age [6–8]. This is because one of the developmental tasks of this age group is engaging in flirtatious behaviors [9]. Thus, an individual may gain high self-esteem through flirting behaviors, or if they have low self-esteem, dating violence may result.

One of the biggest causes of dating violence is the myths that exist in society. Myths represent misperceptions, overgeneralizations, or ideas that many people believe but do not fully reflect reality. One of the most common myths is that dating violence is limited to physical violence [10]. However, studies have shown that dating violence encompasses not only physical violence but also other dimensions, such as psychological and sexual violence [10, 11]. Dating violence myths are false beliefs that arise from stereotyped thoughts, especially among students, and pave the way for this situation to become widespread [12]. In addition, Furthermore, individuals who believe in myths about dating violence may normalize violent behavior [13].

Believing in myths about dating violence can lead individuals to perceive violence as normal and acceptable in dating situations. According to Polat’s (2016) study, individuals, especially those aged 16–25, sometimes view dating violence as a natural part of romance, which leads to the normalization of violence [6]. Katz’s (2006) results, however, indicate that individuals with low self-esteem tend to believe these myths and are more likely to legitimize violence [13].

The relationship between belief in dating violence myths and self-esteem is critical in shaping attitudes toward dating violence. A 2018 study by Tagay et al. examined the relationship between perceived abuse in romantic relationships and self-esteem in depth [14]. The study revealed that women experience higher rates of physical and sexual abuse than men, while men experience more emotional and psychological abuse [14]. In this context, for individuals with high self-esteem, feeling valued is critical for developing healthy relationships.

Self-identity is the sum of a person’s views of themselves, their way of knowing and evaluating themselves [2]. Self-esteem is an important psychological concept that determines individuals’ perceptions of themselves and their environment [15–17] Self-esteem is the perception of oneself as capable, competent, and valuable, as well as the state of acceptance and rejection [15]. Self-esteem affects many aspects of individuals’ lives, including their mental health, interpersonal relationships, and life satisfaction [18, 19]. It has been found that individuals with high self-esteem tend to set healthier boundaries in relationships and defend their personal values [17]. Individuals with low self-esteem, in particular, are more prone to controlling and violent behaviors in relationships and accept these behaviors to maintain the relationship or ensure security [17]. In this context, self-esteem is thought to play an important role in attitudes toward dating violence. Examining the relationship between university students’ self-esteem, belief in dating violence myths, and their attitudes toward dating violence can help young people develop healthy relationships [8, 20, 21]. Dismantling dating violence myths is critical for victims to recognize violence, seek help, and combat it. Awareness and education programs can help reduce violence by raising awareness about dating violence, particularly among young people. In this context, dating violence should be considered a social phenomenon reflecting general trends toward violence in society.

The aim of this study is to determine the effect of university students’ self-esteem and their belief in dating violence myths on their attitudes towards dating violence.

Methods

Methodological design

This descriptive, correlational, cross-sectional study was conducted between June 3, 2024, and March 26, 2025, to examine the effects of university students’ self-esteem and belief in dating violence myths on their attitudes toward dating violence. The study was conducted with students enrolled at XXX University in the Eastern Anatolia Region. XXX University has a total of 45 academic units, including 11 faculties, 3 colleges, 9 vocational schools, 1 state conservatory, 3 institutes, and 18 study centers on 5 separate campuses. The population of the study consisted of 19,733 students enrolled in the 2023–2024 academic year. The sample size of the study was determined as a total of 644 individuals using the known universe sampling method with a 99% Confidence Interval and ± 5% sampling error. Convenience sampling was used in sample selection. The advantages of this method are that it allows for reaching a large audience quickly, easily, and economically [22]. 1.312 students participated in the study. Seven of the students were excluded from the study because they filled out the survey form incorrectly, and the study was completed with 1.305 students. Students actively enrolled in the state university participated in the study. And those who did not have any communication problems, such as hearing, etc., to participate voluntarily were included. Students who wanted to leave the study at any stage or who filled out the questionnaire incompletely or incorrectly were not included in the study.

Measurements

Personal information form

The form, which was prepared by the researchers in line with the literature data [7, 12, 23]. included 18 questions regarding socio-demographic information, dating violence, and self-esteem.

Two-dimensional self-esteem scale (2D-SES)

The scale was developed by Tafarodi and Swan in 2001 to measure self-esteem [24]. The scale, adapted into Turkish by Doğan, has a 5-point Likert style, 16 items, and 2 sub-dimensions [25]. Eight items are included in the “Self-love” sub-dimension, and the other 8 items are in the “Self-sufficiency” sub-dimension. The eight items in the scale (1-6-7-8-10-13-15-16) are reverse-coded. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was found to be 0.72. The minimum score that can be obtained from the scale is 16, and the maximum is 80. An increase in the average score is interpreted as an increase in the individual’s self-esteem and self-efficacy levels.

Dating violence myths scale (DVMS)

Developed by Toplu-Demirtaş et al. in 2023, the scale is a 6-point Likert-type scale and consists of 9 items and 3 sub-dimensions [10].The first 3 items belong to the “Myths about victims of violence” sub-dimension, items 4, 5, and 6 belong to the “Myths about dating relationships” sub-dimension, and the last 3 items belong to the “Myths about sexual dating violence” sub-dimension. The lowest score on the measurement tool is 9, and the highest is 54. The Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient for the total items of the scale is 0.680.

Dating violence attitudes scale (DVAS)

Developed by Terzioğlu et al. in 2016, it is a 5-point Likert-type scale consisting of 28 items and 5 sub-dimensions. The first 5 items belong to the “General Violence Sub-dimension,” items 6–10 to the “Physical Violence Sub-dimension,” items 11–16 to the “Emotional Violence Sub-dimension,” items 17–21 to the “Economic Violence Sub-dimension,” and items 22–28 to the “Sexual Violence Sub-dimension” [26]. A total of 23 of the items in the measurement tool are reverse-coded. The Cronbach’s Alpha internal consistency coefficient of the DVAS is 0.91. A scale score average approaching 5 indicates that individuals’ attitudes towards dating violence do not support dating violence. Data collection process

Data were collected both in person and online via Google Forms. Students meeting the inclusion criteria were interviewed individually or in groups through online platforms. The purpose of the study was explained to the students. Students who volunteered to participate were given the questionnaire; no incentives were offered for participation. Electronic informed consent was obtained before proceeding to the main section of the questionnaire. In cases where a sufficient number of students could not be reached, researchers collected data through in-person interviews after obtaining written informed consent from the students who could be reached. Participants were assured that their data would remain anonymous and confidential and that they could withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences. Questionnaire responses were checked, and only fully completed questionnaires were included in the analysis.

Analysis

The data were evaluated on the computer using the SPSS V-27.0 package program. Descriptive statistics such as number, percentage, mean and standard deviation were used in the evaluation of the data in the studyThe Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the suitability of quantitative variables for normal distribution. In this test, p < .05 indicates that the data are normally distributed [27, 28]. To evaluate the difference between groups with normal distribution, t-test and One-Way ANOVA test were used for independent groups, and LSD and Bonferroni Posthock tests were used as further analysis. Correlation and simple linear regression analyses were used to determine the relationship and effect between the scales. The following values ​​were taken as basis for the coefficient: Pearson coefficients (r); r = .00 is considered as no relationship, r = .01–0.29 as low level of relationship, r = .30–0.70 as moderate level of relationship, r = .71–0.99 as high level of relationship, r = 1.00 as perfect relationship [29]. In statistical analysis, the significance level was accepted as p < .05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethics committee approval was received from the University’s Faculty of Health Sciences Non-Interventional Studies Ethics Committee for the study (29.03.2024, 81829502.903/37). To conduct the research, institutional permission was obtained from the University where the research was conducted, dated 31.05.2024 and numbered E-10829923-929.01-19453. Informed consent for participation was obtained from all students participating in the study.The study was conducted only with students who agreed to participate after the participants were informed about the questionnaire form. The necessary permissions for use were obtained for the Two-Dimensional 2D-SES, DVMS, and DVAS used in the study. The study was designed and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and STROBE guidelines.

Expected benefits of the study

The results obtained in the study, which is expected to contribute to the literature data, are also expected to contribute to the identification of factors influencing university students’ attitudes toward dating violence and to the planning of training programs addressing the factors that lead to negative attitudes.

The mean age of the individuals was 21.72 ± 2.37, the mean age of the current dating relationship was 6.76 ± 15.46, and the mean age of the longest dating relationship was 10.74 ± 16.50. Of the participants, 63.8% were female, 63.3% were in health-related departments and 86.4% were studying at the undergraduate level, 69.3% had a nuclear family, 28.7% lived in the city, 48.7% had a father and 47% had a mother who was a primary school graduate, 68.2% did not smoke and 85.6% did not drink alcohol, 72.2% had a medium-level economic income, 70% lived in a dormitory, 72.1% had not witnessed violence in the family, 63.1% were not currently in a dating (dating partner) relationship, 93.9% had not ended a relationship because of dating violence, and 74.4% stated that they loved themselves (Table 1).

When the participants’ Dating Violence Myths Scale scores were compared with their demographic data, the scores were found to be statistically significantly higher for males than females, for those studying in non-health science programs than for those studying in health science programs, for those with extended families than for those with nuclear families, for those living in villages/towns, districts, and provinces than for those living in metropolitan areas, for smokers than for non-smokers, for alcohol users than for non-smokers, for those who perceived their economic status as poor and very poor versus rich and very rich versus those who perceived it as moderate, for those who had previously used violence against their date (dating partner) than for those who had not, for those who had previously ended a relationship because of dating violence versus those who had not, and for those who did not love themselves versus those who loved themselves and those who sometimes loved themselves (p .005) (Table 3).

When the Dating Violence Attitude Scale scores were compared with demographic data, the scores were found to be statistically significantly higher for women than for men, for those studying in health sciences than for those studying in non-health sciences, for those with nuclear families than for those with extended families, for those living in villages/towns, districts, and provinces than for those living in metropolitan areas, for those who do not smoke cigarettes than for those who do, for those who do not drink alcohol than for those who do, for those who perceive their economic status as poor, very poor, medium, and rich than for those who perceive it as very rich, for those currently living in dormitories and with their families than for those living with friends, for those who have never used violence against their date (dating partner) than for those who do, and for those who love themselves than for those who sometimes love themselves (p .005). Discussion

Dating violence poses a public healthcare concern because it is a life-threatening, most common form of violence among young people, and impacts them psychologically and socially. Addressing dating violence is crucial for reducing the problem. This is why this study was conducted. The results are discussed as follows.

The results of the study indicate that almost three-quarters of the students love themselves. More than half of the participants reported not currently being in a dating relationship, and for those who were, the average duration of their relationship was six months. The longest dating relationship average was 12 months; 6.1% of the students had ended their relationships because of dating violence. In a study, it was found that 45.7% of students are currently in a relationship and 16.5% reported having experienced violence in a previous relationship [30]. In a study conducted with university students, it was found that 13.8% of the students were exposed to dating violence [1]. These differences are thought to be because of individual and regional differences among the students. At the same time, when we look at the literature and the results of our study, we see that dating violence is still a significant problem.

The results also indicate that students have a moderate level of self-esteem. The results of studies conducted with healthcare professionals, nursing students, and university students in the literature are similar to the results of our study [31–33]. Individuals with high self-esteem are thought to have high levels of life satisfaction, extroversion, and self-love. In this study, more than half of the students were studying in the health department and were found not to support dating violence. Studies conducted with health sciences students and nursing students have indicated that students do not support dating violence [23, 34]. The results of our study are similar to the literature. This is thought to be because of the low awareness level of healthcare professionals about violence. The study also found that students hold, albeit small, myths about dating violence. This suggests that students are part of society, influenced by certain beliefs, and for this reason, cannot fully purge themselves of myths about dating violence. Since the majority of the students participating in the study are future health professionals, it is thought that having myths about dating violence will negatively affect the quality of health care.

When the students’ 2D-SES scores and sociodemographic characteristics were examined, it was found that men had higher self-esteem according to gender. When the literature was examined, the study conducted by Şiraz et al. (2024) with university students was similar to the result of the study conducted by Taşgit (2012) with students [35, 36], while Saygılı et al. (2015) reported that women had higher self-esteem than men in their study [37]. Some studies found that self-esteem did not differ according to gender [31, 38, 39]. Different results were obtained in the literature between self-esteem and gender variables. These similarities and differences are thought to arise from the sample groups, the times when the studies were conducted, and the fact that gender roles differ from culture to culture. The study observed that undergraduate students had higher self-esteem. Balcı et al. (2020) conducted a study with healthcare professionals and found that undergraduate and graduate students had high self-esteem [31]. This suggests that increasing individuals’ educational levels positively impacts their self-esteem. Students who have not witnessed domestic violence have higher self-esteem. Durgun (2022) conducted a study with university students and found that students who were exposed to dating violence had low self-esteem [40]. The study results suggest that witnessing or being exposed to dating violence negatively affects self-esteem, or that individuals with low self-esteem have a tendency toward dating violence. The study indicated that students who love themselves have high self-esteem. Loving themselves and feeling valued demonstrate a positive relationship with self-esteem. Our study found that students who were dating partnered had higher self-esteem. A study by Erdem et al. (2022) found higher self-esteem in married students [41], which suggests that having a relationship and increased sharing positively impact self-esteem.

The study also found that students held myths about dating violence, albeit to a small extent. When the students’ DVMS scores and sociodemographic characteristics were examined, it was seen that men held more dating violence myths than women. A study by Güdül Öz et al. (2021) reported that male students held more sexual myths than female students [42]. Students studying in the health field were observed to have fewer dating violence myths, which is considered to be because of the increased awareness and information provided to prospective health professionals [42]. Students living in extended families, those who smoke and drink alcohol, those with low economic status, those who perpetrate violence against their partners, and those who do not love themselves were observed to hold more sexual myths. This suggests that individual, cultural factors, and socioeconomic status have significant effects on the formation of sexual myths. Furthermore, a person’s decreased self-esteem can pave the way for the formation of sexual myths [43].

When examining students’ attitudes towards dating violence and their sociodemographic characteristics, the study found that women scored higher than men when their attitudes towards dating violence were examined by gender, which suggests that women are less supportive of dating violence than men. Öztürk et al.’s (2022) study, however, found that men had higher average scores for dating violence [11]. This is thought to be because of the significant gender differences in the samples of both studies. In the study, students studying in health departments scored higher than students studying in non-health departments. Öztürk et al.’s (2022) study with health students also found higher scores [11]. This suggests that health professionals have a higher awareness of dating violence because of their education. Students living in dormitories with their families were found to have higher scores than those living with friends. Akkaya and Ertekin Pınar’s (2025) study with midwifery and nursing students found that students living in dormitories did not support dating violence [44]. This could be explained by the positive support students receive from their friends in the dormitory regarding dating violence. Students living in nuclear families, not smoking or drinking alcohol, not using violence against their dates, and loving themselves were found to have higher dating violence attitude scores. For this reason, students’ habits, self-esteem, and individual, social, and cultural factors are thought to influence dating violence attitudes.

As students age, their relationship duration and longest dating experience also increase. It also appears that students’ perceptions of myths about those subjected to violence increase with age and that they do not support dating violence. A study by Sünetçi et al. (2016) also found that as students age, their relationship duration and the duration of long-term relationships increase [1]. Positive factors, such as students’ increasing awareness of violence, their awareness through education, and their knowledge of what they want with age, suggest that these factors positively influence their relationship duration and attitudes toward dating violence.

It was observed that as students’ relationships continue, their myths about dating violence decrease, their self-esteem increases, and they become more self-loving and less supportive of dating violence. High self-esteem fosters a trusting environment in relationships and provides a free environment for their dates [45]. As the study shows, as students’ self-esteem increases, they become less supportive of dating violence and have fewer myths about dating violence.

The study observed that self-esteem explained 1.3% of dating violence attitudes, while the Self-Esteem and Dating Violence Myths Scale combined explained 21.5% of dating violence attitudes. Literature shows that individuals with low self-esteem tend to be violent in their relationships and generally resort to this violence to maintain the relationship [17]. imilarly, individuals who hold myths about dating violence may perceive violence in relationships as normal behavior [13].

Limitations and future research recommendations

Most of the data in the study was collected through an online survey. This data collection method caused challenges for the study, including the potential for low survey participation and the inability to answer misunderstood questions. The results are limited to data from students at XXX University and cannot be generalized to the entire population. The data were also limited to the dimensions measured by the measurement tools used. Based on these results, awareness training on dating violence should be provided, and educational programs should be organized. More comprehensive research is needed to better understand dating violence and sexual myths.

Practical implications

Creating a social climate where dating violence is not tolerated, supporting the empowerment of women and young people, and transforming stereotypical norms regarding gender roles are key elements in this process. Awareness campaigns conducted in educational settings and at the societal level can be an effective tool for teaching healthy relationship dynamics and reducing attitudes that accept violence. Understanding and defining types of violence and developing effective intervention strategies contributes to the establishment of safer and healthier relationships in society. Intervention programs targeting dating violence should be designed in a multi-component manner at the individual, peer, school, and community levels and should be implemented through collaboration between mental health professionals, educators, social workers, and policymakers.

Conclusion

This study aimed to determine the impact of university students’ self-esteem and their beliefs about misconceptions regarding dating violence on their attitudes towards dating violence. As a result of this study;

The results indicate that the students have a moderate level of self-esteem, more than half of whom are healthcare department students, do not support dating violence, and hold a small number of myths about dating violence. It was observed that self-esteem had very little effect on attitudes towards dating violence. It was concluded that university students’ belief in myths about dating violence affected their attitudes towards dating violence scores. The results of the study show that dating violence is not only an individual problem but also a public mental health issue. In order to protect and strengthen public mental health, preventing dating violence and raising awareness should be addressed as part of protective and preventive mental health services.
