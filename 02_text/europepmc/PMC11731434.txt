ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND:People who receive social support in their marital life have a higher chance of experiencing more marital satisfaction. Pleasant healthy sexual relations and a couple’s sexual assertiveness are of prime significance in such satisfaction. This study aimed to determine the relationship between social support and both sexual assertiveness and marital satisfaction in married women referring to health centers in Tabriz, Iran.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This is a descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study on 292 women out of 22308 reproductive-age married females referring to the health centers of Tabriz in 2022. Sampling was done by cluster sampling method. Data collection instruments included a socio-personal information questionnaire, personal resource questionnaire 85 (PRQ85-Part 2) as a social support measure, Halbert index of sexual assertiveness (HISA), and enrich marital satisfaction (EMS) scale. Data were analyzed using Pearson, independent t, one-way ANOVA tests, and the general linear model.RESULTS:The mean (SD) of social support, marital satisfaction, and sexual assertiveness scores were 103 (19.8), 135.9 (11.8), and 52.2 (19.5), respectively. Social support had an average positive correlation with marital satisfaction (P < 0.001; r = 0.57) and a good positive correlation with sexual assertiveness (P < 0.001; r = 0.73). An adjusted general linear model showed a significant statistical relationship between social support with sexual assertiveness (B: 0.7; 95% CI: 0.6–0.8; P <0.001), and marital satisfaction, (B: 0.3; 95% CI: 0.2–0.4; P <0.001).CONCLUSION:An increase in social support, increases their marital satisfaction and sexual assertiveness. Focusing on social support could be an effective approach in family counseling or family education programs to improve marital satisfaction.Keywords: Marital satisfaction, sexual assertiveness, social support

BODY
Introduction

Marriage is couples’ most significant source of social support. It enhances their emotional and psychological well-being.[1] Also, marriage is like a social institution that improves individuals’ general health and satisfaction in life.[2] According to Hawkins’ definition, marital satisfaction is defined as the feeling of satisfaction and happiness that a husband or wife experiences considering all aspects of their marriage.[3] However, many factors affect marital satisfaction such as age and duration of the marriage, personal characteristics, communication skills, problem-solving skills, education status, family financial status, behavioral patterns, violence, presence of children, relationships with family and friends, marital conflicts, and sexual relations.[4,5] A pleasant healthy sexual relationship can be the most significant factor,[6] and women may remain in an unpleasant relationship for various reasons such as cultural, economic, and social.[7] However, not all the factors affecting marital satisfaction are fully identified.[8]

Talking about sexual feelings and desires is the best way to know about a spouse’s sexual wishes and complete a couple’s sexual information about each other.[9] A person’s sexual health begins with a sexual self-concept. It is a multidimensional construct that reflects a person’s beliefs about one’s ability to control aspects of sexuality in dealing with oneself and others as s sexual person.[10] The origin of sexual self-concept is a person’s sexual experience from the past to present, which has influenced the process of sexual behaviors related to social information and also, directly related to social support.[11,12] Sexual satisfaction and assertiveness are important dimensions of sexual self-concept.[13] Sexual assertiveness is a crucial concept and factor in marital and in particular sexual, satisfaction.[14] It is the ability to express sexual preference verbally and non-verbally.[15] Its interwoven components are the ability to initiate a desirable sexual relationship, refuse unwanted ones, and speak about birth control methods to prevent unplanned pregnancy.[16]

In many societies, including Iran, women experience difficulties in expressing their sexual needs and desires and maintaining independence in their sexual relations. They suffer from low self-esteem[17] which makes it difficult for them to express such needs and maintain their independence in marital relations.[18] Reports indicate that many Iranian couples suffer sexual dissatisfaction. It is the reason for 50–60% of divorces and 40% of cheating and affairs.[19,20] Hurlbert et al.[21] study showed that women with higher sexual assertiveness score higher in all subscales of sexual function, that is, sexual activity frequency, number of orgasms, sexual desire, sexual satisfaction, and marital satisfaction. A decrease in sexual assertiveness increases marital conflict. In addition to the developmental process that plays a role in sexual assertiveness, it is also the result of a combination of social facilitating and inhibiting and psychosexual factors.[22] Indeed based on the Santos-Iglesias et al.[23] review study, sexual assertiveness is part of a complete set of individual characteristics associated with higher levels of social empowerment and interactions. The results of Dastyar et al.’s study[24] showed a positive impact of assertiveness-based sexual training on the quality of marital relationships among educated women.

Social support is another effective factor in marital satisfaction.[4] Social support may be used as a resource to strengthen marital relationships and potentially reduce marital distress.[25] It can be defined as the presence or availability of reliable friends. Those who let us know they care about, value, and love us.[26] Social support emphasizes the availability of and a quality relationship with people who provide us with supportive resources as necessary. It consists of emotional, instrumental, and informational support.[27] Its existence helps individuals maintain physical health, improves adjusting to and treating illness, and reduces psychiatric symptoms. It also helps an individual deal with the anxiety and loneliness following the death of a family member.[2] Couples can reduce stressful events in life or prevent them through social support. It creates positive reinforcement and a sense of belonging and enhances satisfaction in marriage and life. This support acts as a shield or buffer against environmental stresses such as marital issues and loss. Those receiving social support in their marriage are less prone to physical and psychological issues and experience more marital satisfaction.[28,29]

Several studies have reported a direct relationship between social support and marital satisfaction in people with a physical disability such as multiple sclerosis[30,31] and infertile couples[32,33] but these studies are limited to healthy people. Some studies showed that intimacy plays a determining role in the prediction of sexual satisfaction and marital adjustment.[34,35] Also, one of the important factors in creating a satisfactory marital relationship is sexual assertiveness.[36] Sexual assertiveness is low in Iranian women.[17] But despite this and the importance of sexual assertiveness as one of the most important factors in promoting and improving marital relationships, this variable and its relationship with social support in Iranian culture have not been well studied. Based on the review of the literature, this is the first study in Iran that has been conducted to investigate the impact of social support on sexual assertiveness and marital satisfaction. So, this study aimed to determine the relationship between social support and both sexual assertiveness and marital satisfaction in married females referring to health centers in Tabriz.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This is a descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study on 292 married women of reproductive referring to health centers in Tabriz-Iran in 2022.

Study participants and sampling

The study population was women of reproductive age who have electronic health files in Tabriz health centers. Inclusion criteria were being married and of reproductive age (15–49), also consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria were breastfeeding, having a child aged 6 months or less, menopausal women having the last period at least 1 year before or proven so by lab test (FSH >40), pregnant or plan pregnancy within 3 months, experiencing grievous events in the last 6 months (such as death of a child or dear ones, cheating of husband, serious illness of a close family, prison conviction of the individual or an immediate family member), suffering from certain physical and psychological diseases, a history of consuming hormone replacements in the last 6 months, suffering from certain kinds of cancer such as uterine, ovarian, or breast, a history of dependence on narcotics, stimulants, or alcohol, or has been treated for dependence on them in the last 2 years, suffering self-reported sexual function disorders such as vaginismus, infertility, being peri-menopausal with irregular vaginal bleeding, a self-reported history of taking medications affecting sexual function including antidepressants (such as Fluoxetine, Sertraline, Paroxetine, Nortriptyline, Amitriptyline, Clomipramine, Desipramine, Imipramine, and Doxepin), and not interested in participating in the study.

Sampling

The cluster sampling was performed by random computer-assisted (www.random.org) selection. First, four clusters of health centers which included 20 centers, were selected randomly. In each selected center, the appropriate number of samples was calculated as a fraction of the total sample size according to the center’s demographics (number of married female clients of the center between 15 and 49 years old). A numbered list of female clients was extracted for each center. Actual samples were randomly chosen among 22308 married women of reproductive age by computer selection (www.random.org) from those lists according to the center’s quota (proportional to size sampling). The researcher contacted those people using their filed contact numbers and gave them a brief explanation of the research reasons, workflow, and methods. Individuals were evaluated by inclusion and exclusion criteria in the same call. The eligible ones were asked to attend the corresponding health center on a certain date and time to fill out the questionnaires. The visiting people were provided with comprehensive information regarding the research necessity, benefits, results, workflow, and confidentiality of information. They were then asked to fill out a consent form. Finally, data were collected through study questionnaires via interviews with participants. A total of 19 women were excluded from the study. Among them, 10 women did not want to participate in the study, three women due to the use of antidepressants; two women due to planning for pregnancy within the next 3 months; one woman due to pregnancy; two women due to secondary infertility; and two women due to experiencing grievous events in the last 6 months.

Data collection tool and technique

The instruments for this study are the socio-demographic characteristics questionnaire, the Personal Resource Questionnaire (PRQ85-part 2) as a social support measure, the Hulbert Index of Sexual Assertiveness (HISA), and the Enrich Marital Satisfaction (EMS) scale. The instruments were completed through interviews with participants.

Socio-demographic characteristics questionnaire: This consists of questions about age, duration of the marriage, education level, women’s job, husband’s job, the adequacy of household income, place of residence, marital satisfaction, and the number of children.

Personal resources questionnaire85-part 2 (PRQ85-part 2)

PRQ85-Part 2 is designed to measure the perceived level of social support.[37] It was selected due to its ease of use, clarity, and proven reliability and validity for such measurement. Its 25 items are scored from 1 (totally disagree) to 7 (totally agree). The total score is in the range of 25–175. The high scores indicate high levels of social support. The instrument’s Cronbach’s alpha and interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) were 0.84 and 0.9, respectively.[38]

Hulbert index of sexual assertiveness (HISA)

David Hulbert developed the HISA scale to measure explicit sexual desire during sexual activities in 1991.[20] It consists of 25 items with 5-point Likert scale responses. Each question is scored from 0 (always) to 4 (never). Items 3, 4, 5, 7, 12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 21, 22, and 23 are reversely scored (always = 4, never = 0). Summing the participants’ scores gives the level of their sexual assertiveness. The scores range from 0 to 100. A higher score indicates more sexual assertiveness. HISA is a valid scale with an internal consistency of 0.91. Yousefi et al.[39] confirmed the validity and psychometric properties of the Persian version of the questionnaire.

Enrich marital satisfaction (EMS) scale

EMS scale[40] was used in this study. The short form (Soleimanian 1994) used consists of 47 closed-ended questions. Responses (totally agree, agree, disagree, and totally disagree) are 4-point Likert scales. A total score of 160 shows the highest level of satisfaction. The original questionnaire has 125 questions and 12 subscales. The first two scales are standard responses that evaluate the consistency of the other 10 scales with marital satisfaction.[41] The remaining scales are personality aspects, marital communication, conflict resolution, financial management, leisure activities, sexual relations, children and parenting, family and friends, equative roles of men and women, and religious orientation.[40] The validity coefficient of the 47-item version was 95%.[41]

PQR85-part 2, HISA, and EMS are standard scales. Their Persian versions are psychometrically evaluated in Iran. The socio-personal information questionnaire was the only one in which face and content validity were evaluated. The reliability of the other three scales was re-assessed using internal consistency. The Cronbach’s alphas for social support, sexual assertiveness, and marital satisfaction were 0.82, 0.92, and 0.95, respectively.

Ethical consideration

Participants received written and oral information about the study and written informed consent was obtained from them. They were free to decline participation or withdraw at any time. The Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences approved this study (Approval ID: IR.TBZMED.REC.1398.777). All methods were performed following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the Abbasi et al.[29] study. Considering the correlation coefficient (r) of 0.26 between social support and marital satisfaction in nurses, α = 0.05 and power = 0.95, the sample size was calculated as 155. Assuming a 1.5 design effect and a drop-out probability of 25%, the final sample size was estimated to be 291.

Statistical analysis

Data collected by questionnaires were entered into SPSS ver. 24. Quantitative and qualitative variables were reported using mean (SD: standard deviation) and frequency (percentage) indices, respectively. The normality of the data was measured using kurtosis and skewness. All the data were normally distributed. Pearson correlation test, one-way ANOVA, and independent t-test were used for the univariate analysis of the relationships between social support, and both marital satisfaction and sexual assertiveness, and a general linear model adjusted by socio-demographic information was used for the multivariate analysis. The socio-demographic variables that had a relationship with marital satisfaction or sexual assertiveness were entered into the model as possible confounders. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The mean (SD) of participants’ age and marriage durations were 36.1 (10.7) and 14.3 (9.5). A little more than half of them (50.7%) were high school graduates and most of their husbands were either college graduates (48.3%) or high school graduates (43.2%). The reported occupation of about half of their husbands was self-employment (48.3%) and the reported household income was somewhat sufficient (70.2%). Nearly half of them (44.5%) had two or more children and were somewhat satisfied with their marriage (62.3%).

There was no significant correlation between sexual assertiveness with age (P = 0.659) and marriage duration (P = 0.830). Discussion

This study aimed to determine the relationship between social support and both marital satisfaction and sexual assertiveness, in married women referring to health centers in Tabriz, Iran. Its results indicated that the mean social support and marital satisfaction scores of participants were high, and sexual assertiveness was average. The positive correlation was moderate between social support and marital satisfaction and good between social support and sexual assertiveness. An adjusted general linear model showed a significant relationship between social support and both sexual assertiveness and marital satisfaction.

In response to the first hypothesis of this study, “there is a correlation between social support and marital relationship,” the results of this study demonstrated a significant statistically positive correlation between social support and marital satisfaction. Theoretical models of social support emphasize the priority of support received from spouses to improve marital performance.[42] Such support acts as a psychological distress alleviation tool in couples’ relationships. It is correlated negatively with depression and anxiety and positively with marital adjustment.[29] Supportive measures initiate several cognitive and emotional development in couples. These developments enhance the marital relationship and prevent marital conflict, distress, and deterioration.[43] Our findings are consistent with Rafiee et al.[44] results showing that lifestyle and social support predict marital satisfaction in the elderly. Khodabakhshi-Koolaee et al.[45] results also indicated a positive significant correlation between family, friends, and other people’s social support components and sexual satisfaction. Abbasi et al.[29] observed a significant correlation between social support and marital satisfaction in nurses. Marital satisfaction also increases with the husband’s support, as the most significant source, in mothers suffering from breast cancer.[2]

In response to the second hypothesis of this study, “there is a correlation between social support and sexual assertiveness,” the results of this study demonstrated a significant statistically positive correlation between social support and sexual assertiveness. Younes (2021)[46] showed a significant positive relationship between assertiveness and social support and its domains among university students. But as mentioned earlier, the studies on sexual assertiveness and the factors affecting it, especially social support, are very limited. Based on the study of Nasrollahi Mola et al. (2020),[35] marital adjustment is a considerable predictor of sexual assertiveness in Iranian married women. Sexual assertiveness can lead to more satisfying relationships within the context of marital relationships.[36] The results of Lammers and Stoker’s study[47] showed that social power was associated with greater sexual assertiveness in achieving sexual goals and also a high perception of one’s ability to achieve a satisfactory sexual relationship. In Alvarado’s qualitative study, women expressed that their family is an important barrier to the development of sexual assertiveness. They may accept undesirable demands due to social and cultural reasons.[22] In Brasileiro’s cross-sectional study that was conducted on high school girls, strengthening social self-efficacy in them is associated with higher sexual rights.[48] Based on the Schry et al. study, there is an indirect effect of social anxiety on the risk of reduced sexual assertiveness.[49]

According to existing studies, the couples’ ability to express sexual desire is one of the most effective factors in sexual function and marital satisfaction.[19,50,51] Lower sexual assertiveness can cause marital despair.[14] It is mostly due to the more frequent sexual relations of women who share their sexual desires and feelings with their husbands and have high sexual intimacy with them. This leads to improved marital satisfaction.[21] Extensively promoting sexual assertiveness education, particularly in societies where women generally suffer low sexual assertiveness and patriarchal gender stereotypes prevail, (including many Asian countries such as Iran) seems essential.[52]

Limitation and recommendation

One of the limitations of the study is that the causal relationship between the variables cannot be determined in such a cross-sectional study. Also, the study considered only married women and not their husbands. Speaking of sexual subjects is taboo in Iran and there is a chance the questions are not properly answered. Sexual subjects, particularly sexual assertiveness, depend on personal, social, and cultural variables, so care should be taken when generalizing the results of this study to women of other ethnic, cultural, and religious backgrounds. Family authorities and policymakers’ re-evaluation of the support systems can effectively reduce marital conflicts and dissatisfaction with sexual performance in couples suffering from those. We also propose developing and executing education programs on effective communication skills in pre-marriage counseling courses to promote marital satisfaction and sexual assertiveness. Showing gender differences regarding the relationship of social support with marital satisfaction and sexual assertiveness in future studies may provide a partial explanation of the differences between men and women in this regard. Also, qualitative studies should be used to better understand the processes associated with social support, marital satisfaction, and sexual assertiveness

Conclusion

This study showed a moderate positive correlation between social support and marital satisfaction in married women referring to health centers. It also indicated a good positive correlation between social support and sexual assertiveness in the same group. An increase in social support, particularly in the couple’s support of each other as its most significant type, increases the couple’s marital satisfaction and sexual assertiveness, especially in women. Family counselors and therapists can utilize our results for a deeper understanding of the couples’ basic marital life and sexual function. This can increase marital satisfaction and eventually reduce the high rate of divorce in Iran.
