ABSTRACT
The aim of this study is to examine the predictive relationship with gaslighting exposure on psychological well-being among Turkish young adults and to test the roles of emotion regulation and psychological resilience as mediating psychological mechanisms in this relationship. The sample of the study consists of 336 individuals over the age of 18 living in Turkey who have experienced romantic relationships. The study utilized the personal information form, the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), the Gaslighting Relationship Exposure Inventory (GREI), the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), and the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS) scales. Data were collected through online forms using convenience sampling. Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the relationships between the variables in the study, and regression-based mediation analysis (Process macro model 80) was used to test the mediation model tested in the study. When the findings were examined, it was determined that there was a positive and statistically significant relationship between gaslighting exposure and suppression (r = .170, p = .002). On the other hand, negative and statistically significant relationships were observed between gaslighting exposure and reappraisal (r = -.272, p < .001), resilience (r = -.208, p < .001), and well-being (r = -.413, p < .001). The mediation analysis, tested with 10,000 bootstrap resamples at a 95% confidence interval, revealed that emotion regulation dimensions and psychological resilience have a partial mediating effect. The findings show that exposure to gaslighting weakens psychological well-being not only directly but also indirectly through emotion regulation and psychological resilience. Considering these findings, it was concluded that the psychological well-being of individuals exposed to gaslighting should be supported not only through awareness programs but also through emotion regulation and psychological resilience skills.

BODY
Introduction

Establishing close relationships, one of the developmental tasks of young adulthood, determines how an individual copes with the crisis they experience during this period1. Healthy structuring of these relational processes contributes to individuals’ well-being2. However, manipulating the partner’s feelings and thoughts in close relationships3, ignoring the other’s wishes and desires4, or attempting to position them as an ideal self by distancing them from their dispositional authenticity5 causes the individual to experience negative emotions. In fact, controlling and manipulating emotions, thoughts, behaviors, and feelings in romantic relationships negatively predicts mental well-being6. In this context, the concept of gaslighting, defined as a form of psychological manipulation, is considered a concept that draws attention because it involves misdirection and deception in close relationships and destabilizes the individual’s perception, beliefs, and mental well-being7. A person exposed to gaslighting may doubt their mental well-being, thinking that they cannot reason and perceive things properly8. A recent scoping review9 shows that young people sometimes unknowingly risk becoming victims of gaslighting in their romantic relationships. This is because gaslighting aims to get the victim to indirectly comply and accept an agreement that puts them under control, rather than through direct coercion10. This process typically begins with ‘love bombing,’ characterized by extravagant gifts and excessive affection, and over time, the victim becomes trapped in the relationship with various forms of abuse11. It is foreseeable that this situation poses a significant challenge to mental health12. Therefore, exposure to gaslighting in romantic relationships during young adulthood warrants further research.

In this context, the Psychological Well-being Model put forward by Ryff13 will also be taken as a basis in the study. According to this model, the concept of psychological well-being reflects the individual’s ability to use their potential and achieve self-actualization for a meaningful life in the face of any challenge. The model includes 6 dimensions: positive relationships, autonomy, self-acceptance, environmental mastery, sense of personal growth, and purpose in life14,15. Since the dimensions of positive relationships with others, reflecting trust and intimacy in interpersonal relationships, and autonomy, reflecting the feeling of being able to make one’s own decisions, are related to manipulative processes16, the term gaslighting, which focuses on relationships, can be considered similar to these dimensions. Similarly, it can be said that the dimensions of self-acceptance and environmental mastery, which reflect the individual’s positive evaluations of themselves and their past life, and their ability to express their desires and needs, refer to emotion regulation; and sense of personal growth, which emphasizes development and growth after positive or negative life events, refers to psychological resilience. In this context, gaslighting, emotion regulation, and psychological resilience may be related to psychological well-being, and this perspective may contribute to explaining mental well-being. Therefore, this study was planned to determine the linking of gaslighting exposure with mental well-being, through the lens of emotion regulation and psychological resilience.

Gaslighting and mental well-being

Positive or negative experiences in romantic relationships can predict individuals’ mental well-being, just as mental well-being can also be linked to the ability to form or maintain a romantic relationship. In this sense, it can be argued that the relationship between romantic relationships and mental well-being is bidirectional. According to a study reviewing research on romantic relationships and mental well-being, bidirectional romantic relationships and mental well-being variables were generally found to have stronger effects when relationships were the predictor and mental health the outcome17. The quality of romantic relationships formed in emerging adulthood enhances well-being18. Specifically, a smaller gap between the ideal level of inclusion of the other in the self and the actual level of inclusion of the other in the self—meaning that the individual is actually experiencing the relationship they desire to feel authentic and fulfilled—contributes to relational well-being and mental health19. However, situations such as the individuals being more prone to depression by alienating them and suppressing their own beliefs and feelings in the relational process4, having low self-esteem due to their wishes and desires being ignored in relational processes20, developing dependence on the partner and experiencing anxiety disorder due to the feeling that he/she cannot continue without him/her21 show that negative experiences of individuals in their romantic relationships can negatively predict mental well-being.

The term gaslighting, which emphasizes negativity in relational processes, has gained significant attention in various fields, from the workplace22 to social media23, and particularly in romantic relationships24,25 and mental well-being3,6. Gaslighting became a popular term with the 1940 s film adaptation of Patrick Hamilton’s 1930 s play Gas Light. The film depicts a husband who adopts a manipulative relationship style and tries to convince his wife that she is mentally unstable. In this context, the term gaslighting means convincing someone that they are mentally unstable through false claims and persuading them that they need help24. Gaslighting is a form of psychological manipulation that causes the victim to experience emotional and mental instability and a lack of agency over time, as well as leading them to doubt their perception of reality26. After a while, the victim of gaslighting feels insecure and experiences confusion. During this process, the victim develops dependence on their partner and the relationship, surrendering themselves to this authority8. This can negatively predict their mental well-being over time6, threatening their well-being. Indeed, mental health is generally considered a psychological prerequisite for pursuing any situation of well-being27.

In the relevant literature, it is observed that the negative impact of gaslighting on individuals’ mental health and well-being is related to the decrease in self-esteem and increase in anxiety levels as individuals encounter manipulation and control behaviors at the interpersonal and intrapersonal levels3,28. Romantic relationship quality is an important factor in Mental Well-being, which was developed to guide individuals on how to achieve and maintain mental well-being from a collective and holistic perspective29. It has been observed that problem-solving skills in romantic relationships are associated with mental well-being, and that feeling satisfied in a relationship without perceiving oneself as under pressure facilitates coping, thereby contributing to the maintenance of mental well-being30. Furthermore, some researchers have suggested that individuals who are victims of gaslighting in close relationships are prone to mental illness31 and have argued that exposure to gaslighting is significantly associated with a decrease in well-being28. The underlying mechanisms of this relationship have not been fully determined, but emotion regulation and psychological resilience have came forward as explanatory factors in the relationship between exposure to gaslighting and mental well-being.

The mediating role of emotion regulation

One of the possible mechanisms for mental well-being by reducing the effects of gaslighting exposure can be emotion regulation. Emotion regulation is the process by which individuals experience and express their positive and negative emotions32. According to previous studies, emotion regulation has a positive and predictive relationship with well-being33,34. Researchers also suggest that emotion regulation skills reduce depression and anxiety35,36, are a focal point in improving well-being37, and are a key influencing factor for mental well-being38.

The probable mediating role of emotion regulation on gaslighting exposure and mental well-being is consistent with Ryff’s model of Psychological Well-being13. In this theory, the impact of emotional factors, emotional balance, and healthy emotion management in social and interpersonal relationships on mental well-being is emphasized. A person’s exposure to gaslighting can shows a predictive relationship with mental problems by causing them to surrender to the perpetrator’s control and ignore their own desires and needs. In this process, emotion regulation skills reduce psychological problems such as pressure and control that may be experienced in negative relationships39, which can provide an opportunity to increase mental well-being.

Emotion regulation should not be considered as an experience solely related to positive life events or as involving only positive phenomena. Generally, emotion regulation refers to the experience and expression of negative as well as positive circumstances40. Holley et al.41 revealed that difficulties with emotion regulation are linked to a harmful set of communication, such as withdrawal or avoidance, within the demand/withdraw pattern, which is associated with dissatisfaction in those experiencing psychological violence or partner pressure in close relationships. Therefore, using adaptive emotion regulation to reduce mental illness following gaslighting victimization is crucial. This type of emotion regulation not only reduces the difficulties and problems experienced by the individual but also enhances relationship quality, providing profound outcomes for well-being and longevity42. Adaptive emotion regulation can serve to produce positive outcomes by reducing negative consequences such as psychological disorders and aggression43. In this context, the current study may provide insight into the extent to which emotion regulation can explain the mental well-being of those exposed to gaslighting.

The mediating role of psychological resilience

Psychological resilience is defined as the effort to recover or cope despite negative experiences and is crucial for achieving positive outcomes44. It involves not becoming overwhelmed by adverse conditions, resisting difficulties, and transforming negative factors into positive ones45. Moreover, psychological resilience is positively associated with less hopelessness and higher well-being46. Patterns of psychological resilience in response to negative experiences in close relationships can also contribute to well-being. Satisfying and consistent romantic relationships encourage functional coping behaviors, which increase well-being by creating a sense of security that contributes to psychological stability and acts as a buffer against stress47. Conversely, negative experiences in romantic relationships can negatively be associated with efforts to overcome difficulties, increasing distress.

Studies on workplace gaslighting, psychological resilience, and positive outcomes have been studied more in the literature48–50, while manipulative and narcissistic behaviors in romantic relationships, psychological resilience, and well-being are quite limited51. Many studies have reported that responding to challenges functionally and being resilient in the face of adversity are linked to mental well-being52,53. In an integrative review, Damas et al.54 emphasized psychological resilience as a way of coping with gaslighting. Furthermore, some studies have emphasized that psychological resilience mediates the effects of gaslighting-victimization on positive psychological outcomes. For example, Güleç and Özbay48 showed a negative relationship between exposure to gaslighting and psychological resilience, assessed as the adaptive use of positive adaptation strategies to adversity, and life satisfaction. Based on relevant research, the current study examined whether psychological resilience mediates the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being.

The chain mediating role from emotion regulation to psychological resilience

Possessing protective factors such as emotion regulation strategies and skills is important for psychological resilience55. Emotion regulation contributes to psychological resilience by facilitating problem- and emotion-focused coping56. In other words, it can strengthen psychological resilience by helping individuals make sense of the victimization caused by psychological violence in their relational experiences. While no studies have examined gaslighting exposure together with emotion regulation and psychological resilience in the literature, it is known that individuals who experience conflict in romantic relationships exhibit more adaptive patterns in their relationships through emotion regulation and psychological resilience strategies57. Therefore, it may be possible for gaslighting victims to recognize their experiences through cognitive reappraisal, which is one of their emotion regulation skills, and to construct their psychological resilience by resorting to adaptive coping strategies in response to this negative situation. A meta-analysis of resilience training programs has shown that cognitive reappraisal and problem-focused coping strategies are protective factors for mental health58. Therefore, individuals’ ability to recognize and regulate their emotions through reappraisal rather than suppressing them may contribute to psychological resilience by employing coping mechanisms against gaslighting in their future relationship processes. Previous studies have shown remarkable relationships between emotion regulation and psychological resilience34,55. A systematic review by Polizzi and Lynn56 found that emotion regulation contributes to psychological resilience by facilitating emotion- and problem-focused coping. In this context, we hypothesize that emotion regulation can predict psychological resilience.

Cognitive reappraisal, which involves interpreting a situation positively as a regulation strategy in romantic relationships, is known to contribute to well-being59. Furthermore, responding to a negative situation with reappraisal is considered an important force contributing to psychological resilience60. In this context, reappraisal can be considered a factor that enhances psychological resilience in situations of pressure or psychological manipulation experienced in relational processes. Therefore, reappraisal during gaslighting victimization can serve as a positive coping strategy for psychological resilience because it contributes to cognitive restructuring.

On the other hand, young people who suppress or are unable to express their emotions may resort to maladaptive coping strategies. This, in turn, can negatively impact their psychological resilience. Maladaptive coping strategies, in turn, may vary depending on the type of psychological violence experienced in romantic relationships. Young adults who cope with the difficulties experienced in their relational processes face more positive life experiences later in life61. The relevant literature has found that individuals’ levels of psychological resilience can mitigate the negative impact of gaslighting on life satisfaction48. In other words, emotion regulation and psychological resilience can enhance recovery and coping efforts following the negative emotions resulting from gaslighting victimization.

Based on the information presented, we believe this study will contribute to the literature by providing insights into how exposure to gaslighting predicts mental well-being. In this context, we are going to test the protective effects of emotion regulation and psychological resilience against gaslighting victimization. Furthermore, we put forward that emotion regulation and psychological resilience enhance mental well-being.

The present study

While most studies on gaslighting address psychological manipulation in the workplace22, there are limited studies addressing the well-being of those gaslighted in romantic relationships25. Previous studies have generally focused on the relationship between gaslighting and the mental health of individuals in any occupational group62,63. While evaluating mental health, a psychological prerequisite for any well-being27, within the context of gaslighting’s predictive relationship is necessary, it may be insufficient to explain mental well-being. In this context, assessing mental well-being in romantic relationships, in particular, could contribute to the literature.

On the other hand, there are limited studies that separately examine the mediating roles of emotion regulation and psychological resilience in the relationship between gaslighting exposure and well-being47,48. Unlike other studies, this study examined the predictive relationship with gaslighting on mental well-being through a sequential mediation of emotion regulation and psychological resilience. This suggests that there will be a greater need for interventions to promote more adaptive emotion regulation and strengthen psychological resilience in those exposed to gaslighting.

Moreover, conducting the current study during young adulthood, a critical period for establishing close relationships, may provide profound insights into the well-being of individuals in romantic relationships during this period. Gaslighting can negatively impact a young adult’s confidence in their own judgment and self-esteem, particularly impairing identity formation, another critical task during this period3. During this developmental period, individuals continue to both format their identities and engage in close relationships, and these two developmental processes are particularly interconnected1,64. Therefore, the current study may provide new perspectives on gaslighting and mental well-being during this period, when an individual’s self-perception is structured and close relationships are central.

Based on the theoretical explanations presented, the current study aims to analyze how emotion regulation and psychological resilience, as two mediators in a series of mediation models, work together in the relationship between gaslighting and mental well-being in a sample of Turkish young adults. In this context, we formed our hypotheses as follows:

H1: Gaslighting exposure is negatively related to mental well-being.

H2: Emotion regulation mediates the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being.

H3: Psychological resilience mediates the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being.

H4: Emotion regulation and psychological resilience sequentially mediate the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being.

Method

Study design

Our cross-sectional study was conducted with data from young adults living in various regions of Turkey between September 2025 and October 2025. The current study was approved by the Atatürk University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (document no: E-88656144-000-2500257561, as a result of decision no. 305, session no. 13, dated August 5, 2025). Before data collection, all participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and their informed consent was obtained. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

The study participant size was calculated using power analysis. For this reason, the “a priori analysis” of G*Power 3.1 software was used65. For the regression analysis, it was determined that the calculated sample size should be 95 people by selecting four predictor variables, an Alpha level of 0.05, a power ratio (1-β) of 0.85, and a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15)66. Following the sample size calculations, the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study participants were determined. The study inclusion criteria were determined as individuals over the age of 18, who agreed to participate in the study, had no communication problems, and had experience in a romantic relationship. Exclusion criteria included individuals who did not meet the specified inclusion criteria. Data collection was conducted considering these criteria, and at the end of the process, 340 participants were reached. Four of these participants were excluded from the study because they had no previous romantic relationship experience. Based on the information above, it can be said that the study’s sample size of 336 participants provides evidence of a sufficient sample size. The majority of participants were female (81.5%), high school graduates (62.2%), and from a low socioeconomic background (53.9%). Data collection process

Permission was first obtained from the ethics committee of Atatürk University to conduct the research. Permission for the use of the instruments used in the study was obtained from the relevant researchers. After obtaining the necessary permissions, the measurement instruments were converted into an online form. The prepared form began with an informed consent form containing detailed information about the study. It was followed by a personal information form, the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), the Gaslighting Relationship Exposure Inventory (GREI), the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), and the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS). Participants completed the data collection form in approximately 15 min.

Measurement instruments

Personal information form

It was developed by the researchers and consists of questions about the gender, age, socio-economic levels, and education levels of the participants.

Gaslighting relationship exposure inventory (GREI)

The Gaslighting Relationship Exposure Inventory was developed by Tager-Shafrir et al.67 to assess the level of gaslighting that individuals are exposed to by a romantic partner, and its adaptation to Turkish culture was conducted by Demir Kaya and Kaya68. The scale is scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = often, 5 = always) and consists of 11 items (e.g., ‘My partner makes me feel like I’m in the wrong’). High scores on the Gaslighting Relationship Exposure Inventory indicate a high level of exposure to gaslighting behaviors. As a result of the reliability studies of the scale, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.96. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient obtained as a result of the adaptation studies was reported as α = 0.94. As a result of the reliability analyses performed on the data obtained within the scope of this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.94.

Emotion regulation questionnaire (ERQ)

The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire was developed by Gross and John69 to assess individuals’ emotion regulation levels and was adapted to Turkish by Eldeleklioğlu and Eroğlu70. The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire consists of 10 items and is scored on a 7-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The scale consists of two dimensions: suppression (e.g., ‘I keep my emotions to myself’) and reappraisal (e.g., ‘When I’m faced with a stressful situation, I make myself think about it in a way that helps me stay calm’), and there is no total score. In the original form, the internal consistency coefficients of the scale ranged from 0.73 to 0.76 for the suppression dimension and from 0.80 to 0.82 for the reappraisal dimension69. The internal consistency coefficients of the Turkish form of the scale were calculated as 0.73 for the suppression dimension and 0.78 for the reappraisal dimension70. In this study, the Cronbach alpha coefficients of the scale were determined as 0.70 and 0.84, respectively.

Warwick-Edinburgh mental well-being scale (WEMWBS)

The Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale was developed by Tennant et al.71 to assess the mental well-being levels of individuals, the short form was created by Haver et al.72, and its Turkish adaptation was carried out by Demirtaş and Baytemir73. The mental well-being scale consists of 7 items (e.g., ‘I’ve been feeling useful’) and is evaluated as a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = Never, 5 = Always). High scores obtained from the measurement tools mean that individuals have high levels of mental well-being. As a result of the reliability studies of the scale, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as α = 0.84 in the Norwegian sample and α = 0.86 in the Swedish sample. As a result of the adaptation study, the internal consistency reliability coefficient was found to be α = 0.86 for the first sample and α = 0.84 for the second sample. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was determined as 0.86 in this study.

Brief resilience scale (BRS)

The Brief Psychological Resilience Scale was developed by Smith et al.74 to assess the psychological resilience levels of individuals and adapted to Turkish culture by Doğan75. The psychological resilience scale consists of 6 items (e.g., ‘I tend to bounce back quickly after hard times’) and 5-point Likert-type items (1 = Not at all applicable, 5 = Completely applicable). High scores obtained from the measurement tools indicate that individuals have high levels of psychological resilience. Reliability analyses revealed that reliability coefficients ranged from 0.80 to 0.91. In studies conducted to test the reliability of the scale, the internal consistency reliability coefficient was found to be α = 0.83, indicating a high level of reliability. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was calculated as 0.84.

Data analysis

The data obtained within the scope of the study were evaluated in accordance with the suggestion of Field76. In this context, outliers, linearity, and normality were first examined.). First, the data was tested for outliers, and the transformed standardized z values of the variables were determined to be between ± 3. Since these values were within this range, it was determined that there were no outliers in the data set77,78. The normal distribution properties of the variables in the data set were examined using skewness and kurtosis values. The values obtained being ± 2 indicate evidence of a normal distribution79,80. The hypotheses of the study were tested with Model 80, a regression-based mediation using Process Macro. Before testing the model, the assumptions of the regression analysis were addressed. First, the Durbin-Watson coefficient for independence of errors was checked, and this value was found to be 2.00. This value is expected to be between 1 and 381,82. Another assumption, multicollinearity, was tested using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. It has been reported that VIF values should be less than 5, and tolerance values should be greater than 0.2083,84. As a result of the analysis, it was observed that the VIF values of the model were between 1.05 and 1.14, and the tolerance values were between 0.88 and 0.95. According to the results, it was determined that there was no multicollinearity problem among the variables in the model. To ensure that the variables tested in the study empirically represent different constructs, discriminant validity was examined. The discriminant validity of the variables considered in the study was evaluated using the Heterotrait–Monotrait Ratio (HTMT); the condition was that the ratio between the variables should be less than 0.85 for each latent variable to be sufficiently distinguishable from the others85,86. In addition, Harman’s one-factor test was applied to evaluate potential common method bias (CMB). This test determined whether the explained variance was below 40%87,88. The results of Harman’s one-factor test showed that the first factor explained only 25.8% of the variance, which is well below the traditional 40% threshold. This result suggests that common methodological bias is unlikely to be a serious problem in this study.

Additionally, Cook’s D value was examined to determine the absence of multivariate outliers. Considering that Cook’s D values greater than 1 are considered outliers, it was determined that there were no multivariate outliers in the data set83,84,89,90. The study also examined multivariate normality using Mahalanobis values91,92. If the data have a multivariate normal distribution, comparing Mahalanobis distances with chi-square percentiles produces an approximately linear line. According to the resulting graph, the data formed a linear line and were found to meet multivariate normality.

The bootstrap method is recommended to test the significance of the indirect effects obtained in the model conducted within the scope of the study93. In this method, a resampling technique was used with 10,000 samples at a 95% confidence interval, and the significance of the indirect effect was determined by whether the bias-corrected confidence interval included zero94,95. If the indirect effect is found to be significant, this is considered successful mediation96. In this case, the direct effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable may either disappear or remain significant. If it disappears, this indicates full mediation. However, if the direct effect remains significant, this indicates partial mediation96,97.

To test the research hypotheses, Model 80 was tested using the PROCESS Macro after controlling for gender. As a result of the model analyses obtained, the total effect of gaslighting exposure on well-being was found to be negative and statistically significant (β = −0.401, t = −8.190, p <.001). When the direct effects from the model findings were examined, it was determined that gaslighting exposure positively predicted the suppression dimension of emotion regulation (β = 0.180, t = 3.365, p =.001) and negatively predicted the reappraisal dimension of emotion regulation (β = −0.278, t = −5.291, p <.001) and statistically significantly. In addition to these findings, gaslighting exposure (β = −0.124, t = −2.249, p =.025) and the suppression dimension (β = −0.160, t = −2.988, p =.003) negatively predicted psychological resilience levels, while the reappraisal dimension (β = 0.164, t = 3.015, p =.003) positively and statistically significantly predicted psychological resilience levels. When the direct effects of the model continued to be examined, gaslighting exposure (β = −0.245, t = −5.281, p<.001) and suppression dimension (β = −0.129, t = −2.870, p =.004) predicted well-being levels of individuals negatively; It was determined that reappraisal dimension (β = 0.290, t = 6.316, p <.001) and psychological resilience (β = 0.302, t = 6.607, p <.001) predicted positively and statistically significantly.

When the standardized indirect effects were examined, the 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals did not include zero, indicating that the mediating effects of both emotion regulation dimensions and psychological resilience levels were significant in the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being. When these mediating effects were examined, firstly, the indirect effect of gaslighting exposure on mental well-being through the reappraisal dimension (a1b1 = −0.081, SE = 0.021, 95% CI [−0.124, −0.043]) was determined to be significant. Secondly, the indirect effect of gaslighting exposure on mental well-being through the suppression dimension (a2b2 = −0.023, SE = 0.012, 95% CI [−0.050, −0.005]) was also determined to be significant. When the third indirect effect path was examined, the indirect effect of gaslighting exposure on mental well-being through psychological resilience (a3b3 = −0.038, SE = 0.017, 95% CI [−0.074, −0.007]) was also determined to be significant. Discussion

According to the findings regarding the first hypothesis of the study, gaslighting exposure negatively predicted mental well-being, and Hypothesis 1 was confirmed. The findings reveal that gaslighting exposure reduces mental well-being. This result is similar to the results of studies in the relevant literature that address the relationship between gaslighting and mental well-being and mental well-being3,6.

Manipulating individuals’ emotions, controlling their feelings and thoughts, and causing them to doubt their beliefs and perceptions during romantic relationships increases the risk of mental illness and decreases well-being6,28,31. Individuals who are gaslighted during a relationship experience depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem3,4,20. In line with the cited studies, the first hypothesis of the study suggests that gaslighting exposure is associated with mental well-being.

Studies on gaslighting and its psychological consequences within the Turkish cultural context show similar results44,98. However, in more traditional structures like Turkish culture, significant differences have been found between female and male participants in the application of emotional manipulation99. For example, in the context of the potential effects of gaslighting on female victims, blaming the victim in a relational problem reflects a situation frequently encountered, especially in the Turkish context100. These gender inequality tendencies may predict a negative relationship with well-being by causing individuals to experience more negative cognitive distortions and self-doubt.

In another finding of the study, we found gaslighting exposure not only directly predicts mental well-being but also partially and indirectly predicts it through the mediating role of emotion regulation. These findings supported hypothesis 2 and are consistent with Ryff’s model of Psychological Well-being, which suggests that building positive relationships with others and being able to be autonomous without manipulation increases well-being. Cognitive reappraisal of emotion regulation, in particular, contributes to mental well-being, while the suppression dimension is negatively linked to mental health101. Previous studies have shown that emotion regulation is an important mediator of general health through experiences in close relationships102. Furthermore, the fact that reappraisal of negative situations is considered an adaptive coping strategy and that this enhances well-being highlights the mediating role of emotion regulation103.

Emotion regulation encourages individuals to be aware of what their romantic partner is experiencing and feeling during the relationship process104. Levenson et al.42 suggested that emotion regulation contributes to mental well-being by reappraising negative emotions experienced during the relationship process. Kwon and Kim105 demonstrated that suppression reduces well-being by increasing negative emotions. Similarly, Polizzi and Lynn56 also stated that emotion regulation contributes to individuals’ well-being by increasing adaptive coping strategies.

Because cognitive reappraisal is related to increasing an individual’s awareness of negative experiences, it is considered an active coping style, while suppressing emotions rather than expressing them is considered a maladaptive coping style106. Therefore, in relational processes, cognitive reappraisal can help counteract gaslighting, avoid doubting perceptions and thoughts, and make sense of what to do next. However, dysfunctional beliefs held by individuals during relational processes can cause relational problems by suppressing emotions39. At the same time, exposure to gaslighting can also impair cognitive reassessment through mechanisms such as a decrease in epistemic trust, self-doubt, and cognitive confusion100. It is known that self-efficacy in coping with these negative effects is related to perceived burden107. Therefore, emotion regulation skills can be considered both a coping mechanism to prevent the harm gaslighting has on the individual and a factor contributing to positive outcomes.

The findings obtained within the scope of the third hypothesis of the study indicate that psychological resilience mediates the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being. This finding supports Hypothesis 3. No studies have been found in the relevant literature examining gaslighting, psychological resilience, and mental well-being in romantic relationships together. However, studies investigating the mediating role of psychological resilience between exposure to workplace gaslighting and life satisfaction are similar to the findings of the current study48. Furthermore, a study by Walia and Joshi47 found positive and significant relationships between romantic relationship commitment, psychological resilience, and psychological well-being. The finding that experiences of psychological manipulation in romantic relationships were negatively associated with psychological resilience and well-being in the current study is consistent with the relevant literature.

Most studies examining the relationship between difficulties experienced in romantic relationships and psychological resilience consider psychological resilience as a functional coping strategy following negative experiences108. Shousha51, in a study conducted on young women, concluded that women vulnerable to gaslighting tend to doubt their rationality, jeopardizing their psychological and social well-being. In addition, this study suggested that women resort to psychological resilience resources such as social networks and spirituality to maintain their well-being during this period. In this context, it can be argued that individuals exposed to gaslighting increase their psychological resilience by using various variables as coping mechanisms, thereby strengthening their well-being. In addition, psychological resilience is considered as a important variable contributing to well-being in the relevant literature46,53,58.

Gaslighting can occur in either biological sex at any developmental stage. However, because romantic relationship formation is seen as a more central developmental task during young adulthood, young people may be more likely to be gaslighted by their partners during this period. Young adults, in particular, are more likely to experience the negative consequences of gaslighting when they lack the psychological resilience necessary to cope with gaslighting. This highlights the need for youth to be more informed about how to increase their psychological resilience to gaslighting in romantic relationships.

According to another finding, emotion regulation and psychological resilience play the sequential mediating role between exposure to gaslighting and mental well-being, supporting Hypothesis 4. This finding indicates that gaslighting exposure predicts emotion regulation, emotion regulation predicts psychological resilience, and psychological resilience predicts mental well-being. As Karimi and Esmaeili57 noted, as individuals encountering difficulties in their close relationships improve their emotion regulation skills, they also develop psychological resilience strategies and patterns of overcoming these difficulties. Zarotti et al.60 reported that reappraising problems rather than suppressing them increases psychological resilience.

Furthermore, psychological resilience is associated with an individual’s well-being52. Coping strategies exhibited within the scope of psychological resilience serve a protective function against mental illnesses, enabling individuals to feel better58. Coping strategies employed are crucial for maintaining mental well-being in the face of gaslighting in romantic relationships. According to Ryff’s model of Psychological Well-being, the feeling of constantly developing and growing as an individual following experiences contributes to an individual’s well-being. Therefore, effective emotion regulation can facilitate the development or maintenance of resilience over time. In the context of gaslighting exposure, individuals who can cognitively re-evaluate manipulative relational experiences can reduce emotional dysregulation and exhibit more adaptive coping responses, which can strengthen psychological resilience. In this context, the findings of the current study can be said to be consistent with this theory.

Additionally, numerous studies have shown that emotion regulation is a significant predictor of psychological resilience34,56. In a study conducted by Mestre et al.55 on adolescents, the fact that reappraisal, an emotion regulation strategy, predicted psychological resilience was interpreted as a shield protecting adolescents from risky behaviors common in their developmental stage. Polizzi and Lynn56, in a systematic review of cross-sectional, comparative, and longitudinal studies on emotion regulation and psychological resilience, found that emotion regulation increases psychological resilience by supporting coping strategies.

In line with the information provided above, it can be predicted that young adults who can cognitively reappraise the difficulties related to psychological manipulation in romantic relationships and who have a psychological resilience pattern by using coping mechanisms will have higher levels of mental well-being.

Implications and limitations

The current study investigated the mediating roles of emotion regulation and psychological resilience in the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being among Turkish young adults. The findings revealed that emotion regulation, psychological resilience, and the sequential mediation effect from emotional regulation to psychological resilience mediate the relationship between gaslighting exposure and mental well-being. Therefore, enhancing emotion regulation and psychological resilience during gaslighting exposure are important ways to enhance mental well-being. These findings not only reveal the mechanisms underlying the predictive value of gaslighting on mental well-being in romantic relationships, but also provide implications for enhancing emotion regulation and psychological resilience in preventing victimization by gaslighting in relationships and enhancing mental well-being.

While the study has practical and theoretical implications, there are some limitations in the current study. Firstly, the data collection process in this study was conducted online. This may have resulted in only individuals with internet access, mostly living in urban areas, being included in the data collection process. This could have led to the exclusion of individuals with limited digital literacy or those living in rural areas, creating socio-economic and cultural bias in the sample. Second, convenience sampling was used. While convenience sampling facilitates data collection, it can limit the sample’s ability to represent the population. Inclusion of individuals with specific characteristics in the convenience sampling method may have led to overrepresentation in the sample, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Considering these points, it is recommended that the findings be interpreted carefully for different age groups and socioeconomic levels. To mitigate these limitations, future studies should incorporate face-to-face data collection methods and probabilistic sampling processes to increase the generalizability of the findings. Third, the participants were young adults in romantic relationships. However, since married individuals can experience empty nest syndrome in middle adulthood109, they may experience problems such as control and pressure on each other in their relationships. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies also include gaslighting and related variables with participants in middle adulthood. Fourth, the majority of the participants in the study were women (81.5%). While gender was statistically controlled for to address this limitation, the findings may predominantly reflect women’s experiences and perceptions of gaslighting exposure, emotion regulation, psychological resilience, and mental well-being. Therefore, future studies could achieve gender balance. Fifth, the use of a cross-sectional design in the study may limit the conclusions regarding the direction of the effect of changes in variables over time. Therefore, longitudinal studies can be conducted to determine whether the relationship between individuals’ exposure to emotional manipulation in close relationships and mental well-being changes over time. Sixth, some of the measures used in the study regarding the mediating variables may be insufficient to explain the mediating role between the independent and dependent variables. For example, although psychological resilience is suggested to be related to the effective adaptive coping strategies, future studies could examine the mediating role of coping strategies and determine emotion- and problem-focused strategies separately. Finally, the current study was conducted within the framework of quantitative research. Given that previous studies have addressed gaslighting within the context of narrative-based research110, future studies could utilize qualitative research designs on gaslighting and mental well-being.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals that exposure to gaslighting predicts not only individuals’ psychological well-being but also their emotion regulation skills and subsequent psychological resilience skills. The partial mediating effect of emotion regulation and psychological resilience in this process indicates that individuals experiencing gaslighting exposure need to develop both their emotion regulation skills and psychological resilience skills in order to improve their psychological well-being. Psychologists, counselors, and psychiatrists working in the field of mental well-being can use cognitive restructuring techniques to identify the distorted self-perceptions of gaslighting victims. Specifically, mental well-being professionals can analyze gaslighting incidents in writing with clients. Following these analyses, functional interpretations can be developed, and “evidence-counterevidence” studies can be conducted. In addition to these practices, emotional awareness exercises can be implemented. Instead of suppressing emotions, the therapy process can focus on identifying and accepting them. This helps clients cope with feelings such as guilt, shame, and self-doubt that may arise after gaslighting. Furthermore, psycho-educational programs can be developed for gaslighting victims. These programs can clearly address what gaslighting is, typical manipulation patterns, and the psychological predictive relationship with gaslighting victims, thereby increasing their level of awareness.
