ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The global opioid use problem presents a complex public health challenge characterized by increasing overdoses, addiction rates, and fatalities. Personal factors such as cognitive traits, distress tolerance, and decision-making styles play a crucial role in influencing opioid use trajectories.Aim:This study aimed to investigate decision-making styles, magical ideation, and intolerance of uncertainty among current and past opioid users and healthy controls to contribute to the literature on opioid use disorder.Methods:Three groups were involved: current opioid users (n = 94), past opioid users (n = 93), and healthy controls (n = 100). Participants completed self-report scales assessing magical ideation, intolerance of uncertainty, and decision-making styles. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, analysis of variance, correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression.Results:Current opioid users exhibited lower vigilance decision-making styles and higher magical ideation scores than past users and controls. Decisional self-esteem correlated positively with vigilant decision-making in current and past opioid users. Magical ideation scores correlated positively with maladaptive decision-making styles across all groups. Intolerance of uncertainty did not differ significantly between groups.Conclusions:This study emphasizes that decision-making styles and magical thinking might have significant roles in opioid use disorder. These results contribute valuable insights to tailor interventions and support systems for individuals struggling with opioid use disorder.Keywords: Decision-making, magical thinking, opioid, uncertainty

BODY
INTRODUCTION

The global opioid use problem has become an alarming and complex public health crisis, particularly escalating into an opioid crisis in the United States, transcending geographical, social, and economic boundaries. A worrying increase in opioid-related overdoses, addiction rates, and fatalities has marked this epidemic.[1] Understanding the factors driving opioid use is cardinal to managing its devastating impact.

Individual cognitive traits, tolerance toward distressing situations, and decision-making styles are among some of the personal factors that could influence the initiation, continuation, and development of dependence on opioid use.[2]

Magical thinking refers to a cognitive tendency in which individuals believe that their thoughts, actions, or rituals can influence unrelated events or outcomes through supernatural means, despite lacking any logical or causal connection.[3] This psychological phenomenon often involves attributing extraordinary powers to certain behaviors or objects, leading to irrational beliefs and behaviors.[4] Eckblad and Chapman (1983) defined magical thinking as the tendency of an individual to establish causal relationships between concepts that are not widely accepted within their culture.[5] When it comes to substance use, magical thinking can manifest in various ways, such as believing that a specific substance has mystical properties that can bring about desired outcomes or alter reality. Individuals might engage in substance use rituals with the belief that these actions can enhance the effects or ensure a positive experience, even though the actual mechanisms of the substances are rooted in biological and chemical processes.[6] This intertwining of magical thinking and substance use can contribute to the development of superstitions, risky behaviors, and unrealistic expectations, potentially impacting how individuals perceive and engage with psychoactive substances.[7]

Intolerance of uncertainty is the discomfort with ambiguous situations.[8] Opioid users with this trait might use opioids to alleviate uncertainty-induced anxiety, seek temporary relief, and perpetuate a cycle of use and addiction.[9] Addressing intolerance of uncertainty alongside the physical aspects of addictions can inform more effective interventions.[10]

Decision-making styles incorporate various approaches individuals employ when making choices, with adaptive and maladaptive variations.[11] Adaptive decision-making involves a balanced assessment of information, risks, and benefits, leading to informed and rational decisions.[12] On the other hand, maladaptive decision-making styles often deviate from rationality. They can include impulsive decisions, avoidance of decisions due to fear of uncertainty, or reliance on nonrational thinking.[12] In the context of opioid users, their decision-making styles can significantly impact their trajectory of use.[13] Those with adaptive decision-making skills might be more inclined to seek treatment and adopt healthier coping mechanisms. At the same time, those with maladaptive styles could exhibit impulsive drug-seeking behaviors, avoid seeking help, or resort to opioids to manage emotional distress.[2] Understanding the interplay between decision-making styles and opioid use is essential for tailoring effective interventions and support systems for individuals struggling with opioid addiction.

People may feel anxious in uncertain situations and may struggle to tolerate this anxiety, attempting to manage it with supernatural beliefs. At this point, magical thinking can be one of their coping mechanisms.[14] In particular, substance use could increase uncertainty, such as the uncertainty of finding the substance and the possibility of being caught by the police. Although the relationship between magical thinking and maladaptive decision-making styles is studied in gambling disorder, unfortunately, studies regarding this relationship in substance use disorders are lacking in the main literature.[15] Increased magical thinking has been related to uncertainty-filled activities such as gambling.[16] In gambling disorder, there is a phenomenon known as “control illusion” that makes the patients gamble more.[16] Likewise, some data indicate that intolerance of uncertainty is associated with risky decision-making.[17]

There are studies in the literature showing that former opioid users may also show some cognitive impairments despite their discontinuation of opioid use, which may alter decision-making styles.[18] Some studies suggest that former opioid users may demonstrate better functional decision-making compared to current opioid users; however, there are also studies indicating no difference.[19,20]

This study aimed to investigate decision-making styles, magical ideation, and intolerance of uncertainty of current opioid users, past opioid users, and healthy controls and contribute to the literature in this field. Our first hypothesis is that magical thinking and intolerance of uncertainty are higher in current opioid users than in former opioid users and healthy controls. Another hypothesis is that magical ideation scores are lower in the former opioid users compared to the current opioid users but higher than healthy controls. Our study also hypothesizes that in the current opioid users, the vigilance decision-making style is lower compared to the former opioid users and control groups. Our last hypothesis is that magical thinking and intolerance of uncertainty impact the vigilant decision-making style.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Model of the study

This research was conducted on individuals who are currently using opioids (detoxification group), individuals who have used opioids in the past but are now in remission (remission group), and individuals without any psychiatric disorders or substance use disorders, including alcohol (control group). The study aimed to examine the levels of magical thinking and intolerance of uncertainty as well as decision-making styles among individuals in the detoxification (n = 94), remission (n = 93), and control (n = 100) groups, while also identifying various sociodemographic variables. We employed a quantitative approach using a correlational research model to achieve this objective.

Study sample

The inclusion criteria for the detoxification group were meeting the criteria for opioid use disorder according to DSM-5 and testing positive for opioids in the urine screening test. For the remission group, the criteria were having met the diagnostic criteria for opioid use disorder in the past but not meeting them for at least 1 year according to DSM-5 and testing negative for opioids or any other substance metabolites in the urine screening test. For the control group, criteria included not having any current psychiatric disorders and no history of alcohol/substance use disorders in the past or present. Additionally, all participants must be literate and willing to participate in the study and provide informed consent. Individuals using any substance other than opioids were not included in the study due to the potential impact of this substance on the outcomes of the MIS, IUS-12, and MDMQ scales. All participants’ exclusion criteria were illiteracy, current or past psychosis, and mental retardation. The detoxification and remission group participants were recruited from a Drug Addiction Treatment Center outpatient clinic and healthy controls from a similar sociocultural area as the detoxification and remission groups.

Our clinic, which provides inpatient and outpatient clinic services, receives an average of 25,000–30,000 outpatient clinic applications annually, and the health insurance of the patients covers both examination and treatment fees. On the other hand, although the examination fees of patients without health insurance are covered by the state, they have to cover their own treatment expenses.

Ninety-three of the patients in our study (past opioid users: n = 93) were currently applying to our clinic regularly at 28-day intervals and had been under regular partial agonist buprenorphine/naloxone combination treatment for at least 1 year. Ninety-four of them (current opioid users: n = 94) consisted of patients who applied to our clinic for outpatient treatment due to their current active opioid use. For the control group, relatives of the patients and hospital employees were invited to participate.

Nine of the patients in the detoxification group, three of the patients in remission, and two of the participants in the control group were excluded from the study due to their past or active psychotic disorder diagnoses.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the XXX Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (date: x, approval number: x). The study was conducted following the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant included in the study.

Data collection instruments

All participants included in the study were assessed by researchers using The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID 5-CV).[21] Additionally, participants were administered four self-report forms/scales as explained below:

Sociodemographic data form: Using this form prepared by the researchers, information was collected from participants, including their age, gender, educational level, marital status, employment status, parental loss or divorce, parental education level, initial substance used, age of substance initiation (any kind of substances), and duration of opioid use disorder.

Magical Ideation Scale (MIS): This scale, developed by Eckblad and Chapman, identifies magical thinking styles in individuals, such as thoughts between unrelated events, astrology, psychic energy transfer, lucky charms, and psychological interactions. The scale consists of 30 true–false questions, and scores between 0 and 30 can be obtained. Higher scores indicate a greater tendency toward magical thinking styles.[22] The adaptation of the scale into Turkish and its validity reliability study was conducted in 2003.[23]

Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale-12 (IUS-12): This scale, developed based on the 27-item long form,[8] consists of 12 items and employs a 5-point Likert scale. The scale allows for scores between 12 and 60, with higher scores indicating increased levels of intolerance of uncertainty. The scale comprises two subdimensions: prospective anxiety and inhibitory anxiety. This scale was adapted into Turkish in 2014.[24]

Melbourne Decision-Making Questionnaire (MDMQ): This scale, developed by Mann and colleagues in 1998, aims to assess individuals’ self-esteem and decision-making styles. The scale consists of two sections. The first section comprises six items measuring decisional self-esteem. The second section includes a total of 22 items examining four different decision-making styles: vigilance, buck-passing, procrastinating, and hypervigilance decision-making styles. The items of the scale use a 3-point Likert scale; correct answers correspond to 2 points, sometimes to 1 point, and incorrect to 0 points. The vigilance style is evaluated within 0–12 points, the buck-passing style within 0–12 points, the procrastination style within 0–10 points, and the hypervigilance style within 0–10 points. Higher scores are interpreted as indicative of the use of the relevant decision-making style. Unlike other decision-making styles, the vigilance decision-making style is recognized as the rational decision-making style.[11] The scale was translated and adapted into Turkish.[25]

Statistical analysis

In data analysis, descriptive statistical measures (frequency and percentage for categorical variables; mean and standard deviation for continuous variables) were used to depict participants’ sociodemographic information. Chi-square, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), and independent samples t-tests were employed to compare demographic variables between groups. ANCOVA was conducted to compare scale scores among participants. Gender was taken as the control variable. Nevertheless, some researches indicate that there is no substantial variance in magical thinking, intolerance of uncertainty, or vigilant decision-making based on educational status.[26,27,28] Correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationships between measurement tools used in the study for each group. Last, multiple linear regression analyses with the enter method were conducted with vigilance decision-making style as the dependent variable. SPSS software (version 25) was utilized for data analysis. A significance level of 0.05 was considered for statistical significance.

DISCUSSION

In this study, where we compared current opioid users, past opioid users, and healthy controls, it was determined that the detoxification group had lower vigilance decision-making style scores than the control group. Additionally, a positive correlation was observed between decisional self-esteem and vigilance decision-making style in both the detoxification and remission groups. Furthermore, it was found that magical ideation scores were positively correlated with maladaptive decision-making styles. Notably, decisional self-esteem emerged as a predictor of vigilance decision-making style in both the detoxification and remission groups.

The impact of opioid or other substance use on decision-making styles has been a topic of interest. In a study comparing individuals who use amphetamines, opioids, and controls with no substance use, it has been observed that individuals using opioids or amphetamines exhibited more disadvantaged decision-making performance than the control group.[29] In a different study, comparing individuals using opioids, cocaine, and amphetamines with controls who did not use substances, it was discovered that those using cocaine and amphetamines had lower vigilance decision-making style scores than those using opioids or healthy controls, while amphetamine users displayed higher procrastination decision-making style scores.[30] Another study conducted on the general population via online platforms has found that decisional self-esteem and decision-making styles play a role in individuals’ relationship with cannabis use and their attitudes toward seeking help.[31]

In a study specifically examining the effects of opioid use on decision-making styles, heroin users were compared to those using prescription opioid medications, and it was observed that heroin users exhibited more impulsive decision-making styles.[32] Our study found that individuals with active opioid use had lower vigilance decision-making styles compared to individuals with past opioid use history or healthy controls, which is consistent with the existing literature data. Since decision-making styles can influence individuals’ risky behaviors or help-seeking and receiving behaviors,[33,34] it appears to be an important topic that needs to be emphasized in substance use disorders.

Another noteworthy finding of our study is that while the vigilance decision-making style was not associated with decisional self-esteem in the control group, this relationship was observed in the detoxification and remission groups. This relationship could be significant for active opioid users and individuals with a history of opioid use during the treatment process; by enhancing decisional self-esteem, efforts can be made to encourage individuals to make more vigilant decisions.[35]

One of the results that contradict the hypothesis of our study is the similar levels of magical thinking in the detoxification group compared to the remission and control groups. In magical thinking, an individual believes that their thoughts, actions, words, symbols they use, or even a living or inanimate object possesses powers.[5] Within this framework, individuals may continue their relevant substance use by attributing omnipotent magical properties to the substances they use, transforming the phenomena of nature into objects of their desires. Although magical thinking has been shown to be higher in gambling disorder,[16] the fact that we did not see the same relationship in our study after controlling gender may be related to our sample size.

In numerous studies involving individuals with gambling disorder, classified under non-substance-related disorders in DSM-5, it has been observed that these individuals exhibit behaviors such as the illusion of control, defined as a belief in having control over uncontrollable situations, and associated ritualistic behaviors like talking to slot machines or blowing on dice. Additionally, they have been found to attribute various magical qualities to specific gambling tables or machines, believing that they bring luck, within the context of which rituals such as blowing on dice or engaging in superstitious behaviors are performed.[36,37] In a study investigating the relationship between cannabis use and magical thinking, it was found that individuals who use cannabis regularly or occasionally exhibited higher levels of magical thinking compared to nonusers in the control group.[38]

Although primarily associated with childhood by Piaget, it has been reported that in later stages, even adults, particularly during periods where they perceive a loss of control and experience increased levels of anxiety, may resort to magical thinking systems and associated rituals to regain a sense of control and reduce anxiety.[39] Disregarding the literature, in our research, it has been found that similar levels of magical thinking were observed in the detoxification group as the remission and the control groups.

Intolerance of uncertainty is associated with trait anxiety and impulsive decision-making in opioid users.[9] Even if our study did not reveal significant differences in IUS scores among the detoxification, remission, and control groups, it was observed that the IUS is positively correlated with maladaptive decision-making styles in all three groups. In this context, interventions, such as cognitive behavioral therapy or pharmacotherapies,[40,41] could be implemented for opioid users to reduce their intolerance of uncertainty, thereby enhancing their vigilant decision-making.

Certain limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting the results of this study. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between opioid use and decision-making styles. Longitudinal studies could provide a more detailed understanding of how these factors evolve. Second, the study relied on self-reported measures, which might be subject to recall bias or social desirability effects. Future research could incorporate objective measures of decision-making and substance use behaviors.

Additionally, the study focused only on opioid users, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to other substance-use populations. Broader inclusion of different substance users could offer a more comprehensive view of decision-making styles across various addictive behaviors. Furthermore, other clinical variables such as comorbidity, psychiatric and medical, and treatment details were not considered, which is a limitation. Last, the study did not examine the potential underlying neurobiological mechanisms that might contribute to the observed cognitive differences. Exploring the neural correlates of decision-making styles in the context of opioid use could provide deeper insights. So, these findings need to be interpreted cautiously as the study design was cross-sectional. We conducted the study with volunteer patients and the patients who were unwilling to participate might have different cognitive features, and this also limits the generalizability.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes valuable insights into decision-making processes in current or past opioid use disorders, suggesting adjusted interventions to enhance decision-making styles and improve the tolerance of uncertainty.

Key messages

Current opioid users tend to have a less vigilant decision-making style.

Current opioid users have similar magical ideation as past opioid users and healthy controls.

Decisional self-esteem positively correlates with vigilant decision-making in current and past opioid users.

Magical ideation scores seem to be related to maladaptive decision-making styles.
