ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveExperiencing multiple relationships with intimate partner violence (IPVRs) in one's lifetime is linked to poorer mental health outcomes. However, associations among multiple IPVRs, depression symptoms, and posttraumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) have primarily been tested with individual women. The present study examines whether experiencing multiple physical IPVRs, compared to a singular physical IPVR, is associated with greater severity of depression symptoms and PTSS among a sample of romantic couples. We also explored whether gender or current physical IPV victimization severity moderates these associations.MethodsA subsample of 116 romantic partners reporting at least one physical IPVR was drawn from a larger study of dyads reporting alcohol use disorder and IPV in their current relationship. Two multilevel models tested associations among multiple physical IPVRs, gender, and current IPV victimization severity with depression symptoms and PTSS while controlling for the interdependence of the dyadic sample and alcohol problem severity.ResultsExperiencing multiple physical IPVRs, compared to a singular physical IPVR, was related to greater depression symptoms and PTSS, respectively. Alcohol problem severity was also related to greater PTSS. There were no significant associations among gender or current physical IPV severity with depression symptoms or PTSS, nor any significant interactions between these variables and single versus multiple physical IPVRs with depression symptoms or PTSS.ConclusionThese findings emphasize the value of examining physical IPV and psychological outcomes in a dyadic context. Prevention and intervention efforts for those who experience multiple physically violent relationships may be valuable for both men's and women's psychological health. Clinical Trial Registration: NCT03046836 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03046836).

BODY
Introduction

Relationships with intimate partner violence (IPVR) negatively impact mental health (Coker et al. 2002; Lagdon et al. 2014; Spencer et al. 2019a). Certain characteristics associated with IPVRs (e.g., violence severity and type, repeated exposure) may also exacerbate psychological distress for survivors (Lagdon et al. 2014). Experiencing multiple IPVRs compared to one IPVR is one characteristic that has garnered attention. Some studies have found that individuals who experience multiple IPVRs may be at greater risk for poorer mental health outcomes (Bogat et al. 2003; Coolidge and Anderson 2002; Matlow and DePrince 2013). Still, this body of work is limited and has primarily focused on women IPVR survivors (Ørke et al. 2018). The present study extends the literature by examining whether a history of multiple physical IPVRs, compared to a singular physical IPVR, is associated with the severity of psychological distress for men and women in romantic relationships.

Relational Context of Physical IPV

IPV victimization includes sexual violence, physical violence, stalking, and psychological aggression perpetrated by a current or former romantic partner (Leemis et al. 2022). Physical IPV victimization is prevalent for both men and women, with more than two out of five men (42.3%) and two out of five women (42.0%) reporting being slapped, pushed, shoved, or severe physical violence (e.g., beaten, choked) in their lifetime (Leemis et al. 2022). Men and women also exhibit similar rates of physical IPV perpetration, with bidirectional IPV, or perpetration by both partners, being the most common pattern of physical IPV (Hines et al. 2020; Straus 2011). However, IPV research often focuses on unilateral violence with men as the perpetrator and women as the victim (Dokkedahl and Elklit 2019). Given the relational context of physical IPV, studies would benefit from incorporating both partners' experiences.

Consequences of Physical IPV

Physical IPV victimization is particularly pervasive, with clear, negative impacts across different domains of functioning. Physical IPVRs are associated with a range of negative outcomes for survivors, including poor physical and mental health, substance use, chronic illness, and injury (Afifi et al. 2009; Coker et al. 2002; Lagdon et al. 2014; Spencer et al. 2019a). Specific to mental health, depression symptoms and posttraumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) are among the most common and salient sequelae of physical IPVRs for men and women (Coker et al. 2002; Lagdon et al. 2014; Spencer et al. 2019a). Although some studies have found women are more likely to report psychological distress, other work suggests that men and women who experience physical IPVRs are equally at risk for depression symptoms and PTSS (Afifi et al. 2009; Lagdon et al. 2014; Próspero 2007; Spencer et al. 2019a). However, not everyone who experiences physical IPV reports violence‐related psychological symptoms or distress, which emphasizes the need to identify those at greater risk for poorer outcomes (Leemis et al. 2022).

Multiple Physically Violent Relationships

Severity of IPV victimization is a well‐established risk factor for psychological distress. The literature suggests a dose‐response effect, where repeated IPV exposure is associated with the development of depression symptoms and PTSS (Golding, 1999; Lagdon et al. 2014; Próspero 2007). Experiencing multiple IPVRs is one type of repeated exposure consistent with this dose‐response effect. Indeed, Coolidge and Anderson (2002) found that women with exposure to multiple violent relationships endorsed significantly higher rates of PTSS (36%) compared to women reporting violence in one relationship (15%) and women with no history of IPV (17%). Bogat et al. (2003) also found that women who reported violence in both their current and previous relationships endorsed higher PTSS and depression symptoms compared to women reporting violence with only one partner. Moreover, Matlow and DePrince (2013) found that type of repeated exposure was related to differential PTSS, where repeated victimization from multiple partners was associated with several symptoms across the re‐experiencing, arousal, and emotional numbing clusters, and repeated victimization from the same perpetrator was associated with only one avoidance symptom (i.e., avoiding thoughts and feelings about the event). Thus, multiple physical IPVRs may be a critical risk factor for depression symptoms and PTSS. Still, the literature has rarely distinguished multiple IPVRs from multiple IPV episodes with the same partner (Ørke et al. 2018). Furthermore, to our knowledge, these associations have yet to be tested with both men and women in a dyadic, or relational, context.

The Present Study

The primary aim of the current study was to examine whether experiencing multiple physical IPVRs, compared to a singular physical IPVR, was associated with the severity of depression symptoms and PTSS for men and women in romantic relationships. Using dyadic data from a larger study examining co‐occurring IPV and alcohol use disorder (AUD), we hypothesized that participants with a history of multiple physical IPVRs would report (H1) greater severity of depression symptoms and (H2) greater severity of PTSS compared to participants reporting a single physical IPVR. Given the uniqueness of this dyadic sample and the limited work including men and partners in intact IPVRs, we also conducted two exploratory analyses that examined the attenuating effects of gender and physical IPV severity in the current relationship on the associations among multiple IPVRs, depression symptoms, and PTSS. Lastly, we controlled for the severity of alcohol use problems, given that the current sample included couples in which at least one member met criteria for AUD.

Methods

Participants

The current study comprised a subsample of participants enrolled in a pre‐registered randomized trial assessing the effects of oxytocin on alcohol craving and intimate partner violence (Flanagan et al. 2022). The main outcomes for this trial, as well as full inclusion and exclusion criteria, study procedures, and participant characteristics, have previously been published (Flanagan et al. 2022). Dyads were screened for physical IPV using the Revised Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS‐2; Straus et al. 1996), and included in the parent study if either partner endorsed at least one instance of physical IPV at some point in their relationship. Additionally, one or both partners within the dyad had to meet DSM‐5 (2013) criteria for current AUD. Participants who endorsed severe and unilateral IPV were excluded from the study for safety; however, no participants needed to be excluded for this reason. For the current analyses, the sample was limited to participants who endorsed physical IPV victimization, either in their current or a previous relationship, as indicated by the Traumatic Life Events Questionnaire (TLEQ; Kubany et al. 2000).

Demographics

A total of 116 adult romantic partners were included in the current subsample, with 55.9% (n = 66) of participants identifying as women and 43.1% (n = 50) identifying as men. No participants reported a gender identity different than their sex assigned at birth. Participants were on average 36.33 (SD = 10.64) years old, with ages ranging between 21 and 67. The majority of participants identified as white (n = 86, 74.1%) and non‐Hispanic (n = 108, 93.1%). The sample also included participants identifying as Black/African American (n = 23, 19.8%), Native American or Alaskan Native (n = 2, 1.7%), Asian (n = 1, 0.9%), and multi‐racial (n = 3, 2.6%), with 6% of the sample identifying as Hispanic (n = 7). Most participants were not married, but cohabitating with their current partner (n = 61, 52.6%). Participants reported a mean relationship length of 77.48 months (SD = 82.06) or ~6.5 years, with the minimum being 6 months and the maximum being 33 years. Within the subsample, 10 participants identified as belonging to a same‐sex couple (8.6%). There were 38 couples (65.52%) where both partners reported physical IPV victimization in their current or a previous relationship on the TLEQ, and 9 couples (15.52%) where both partners reported experiencing multiple physical IPVRs.

Procedures

All study procedures received approval from the Institutional Review Board and were conducted in accordance with its guidelines. Prospective participants were recruited through outpatient clinics at an academic medical center and a VA hospital, flyers, and online advertisements (e.g., Craigslist, Facebook). Participants from previous studies who consented to future research were contacted via email and text. Interested participants completed an initial phone screen, followed by a private informed consent (separate from partner) and baseline assessment to determine eligibility. The psychosocial measures used in this study were drawn from the baseline assessment of the parent trial.

Measures

Physical IPV Victimization and Multiple Physical IPVRs

There are currently no gold standard assessments to measure multiple physically violent relationships. However, selecting individual items from validated measures is commonly used and is associated with less bias than newly developed measures that have yet to be validated (Matlow and DePrince 2013; Ørke et al. 2018). The Traumatic Life Events Questionnaire (TLEQ; Kubany et al. 2000) was used to measure participants' exposure to multiple physical IPVRs. The TLEQ is a validated inventory that asks about lifetime exposure to 23 potentially traumatic events. Participants are asked to indicate whether they have experienced an event (yes/no) and answer follow‐up questions if they endorse exposure. This study focused on items 14 and 14D, which measure participants' exposure to physical IPV. Item 14 was used to identify which participants had a history of IPV, “Have you ever been slapped, punched, kicked, beaten up, or otherwise physically hurt by your spouse (or former spouse), a boyfriend or girlfriend, or some other intimate partner?” and item 14D was used to measure participants' exposure to multiple physical IPVRs, “Has more than one intimate partner physically hurt you?”. Both lifetime exposure to physical IPV and multiple physical IPVRs were dichotomous responses (0 = no, 1 = yes).

PTSS

The 20‐item PTSD Checklist for DSM‐5 (PCL‐5; Weathers et al. 2013) was used to measure the severity of participants’ PTSS. Using a 5‐point Likert scale (0 = Not at all to 4 = Extremely), participants are asked how much they were bothered by their PTSS within the last month. An overall score of PTSS severity was calculated by summing the items, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 80. The PCL‐5 demonstrated strong internal consistency in the current sample (α = 0.95).

Depression Symptoms

The 9‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9; Kroenke et al. 2001) was used to measure the severity of participants’ depression symptoms. Using a 4‐point Likert scale (0 = Not at all to 3 = Nearly every day), participants are asked how often they were bothered by different problems (e.g., feeling tired or having little energy) in the past 2 weeks. Severity of depression symptoms was calculated by summing the items, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 27. The PHQ‐9 demonstrated strong internal consistency in the current sample (α = 0.86).

Current Physical IPV

The physical assault subscale from the Revised Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS‐2; Straus et al. 1996) was used to determine the severity of physical IPV in the current relationship. Using an 8‐point Likert scale, (0 = This never happened, 1 = Once, 2 = Twice, 3 = 3–5 times, 4 = 6–10 times, 5 = 11–20 times, 6 = More than 20 times, 7 = Not in the last 6 months, but it did happen before), the CTS‐2 asks participants to report on victimization and perpetration of violence that occurred within the last 6 months. The physical IPV subscale contains 12 items ranging from mild to severe, including behaviors such as throwing something, grabbing, kicking, choking, and using a weapon. For this study, response categories were rescored based on standard procedure outlined in the CTS‐2 handbook (i.e., 3–5 times (recoded to 4], 6–10 times [recoded to 8], 11–20 times [recoded to 15], and more than 20 times [recoded to 25]), with scores for the physical assault subscale ranging from 0 to 300 (Straus et al. 2003). We also applied the maximum scoring method to minimize potential self‐report bias (see Taft et al. 2010; Taft et al. 2016). The physical assault subscale demonstrated strong internal consistency in the current sample (α = 0.87).

Alcohol Problem Severity

The 10‐item Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Saunders et al. 1993) was used to measure the severity of participants' alcohol problems. Using a 5‐point Likert scale, the AUDIT asks participants to report on past‐year frequency of hazardous and risky alcohol use behaviors. Severity of AUD symptoms was calculated by summing the items, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 40. The AUDIT demonstrated strong internal consistency in the current sample (α = 0.90).

Analytic Plan

Preliminary analyses were conducted to inspect means, standard deviations, and bivariate correlations among key study variables. Correlations among multiple physical IPVRs, PTSS, depression, and sociodemographic variables, including age, relationship length, race, and income, were also examined to identify additional control variables. However, no significant associations emerged. Due to the use of secondary data, a power analysis was not conducted; rather, recommended procedures for reporting statistics were used (Dziak et al. 2020). All primary analyses were conducted within a multilevel mixture modeling framework using SPSS version 28. Multilevel mixture modeling was used to account for the nonindependence of the dyadic data by nesting individual partners within couples (Kenny et al. 2006). Two models were tested, both including lifetime experience of multiple physical IPVRs (effect coded as −1 = singular physical IPVR or 1 = multiple physical IPVRs), severity of physical IPV victimization in the current relationship, alcohol problem severity, and gender as predictors. Each model also included two interactions: one for multiple physical IPVRs * physical IPV severity, and another for multiple physical IPVRs * gender (effect coded −1 = male, 1 = female). Interaction terms were created by multiplying variables. Associations of these predictors were assessed separately for PTSS and depression symptoms. We planned to probe any significant interactions to better understand the direction of the effects. Continuous predictor variables were mean‐centered prior to running the models for ease of interpretation. Both standardized and unstandardized coefficients are presented. Although the models account for interdependence in the estimates of the unstandardized coefficients, standardized coefficients rely on variance estimates that assume independence and thus should be interpreted with caution.

Gender and Current Physical Violence

Our two exploratory analyses tested the attenuating effects of gender and severity of current physical violence with the associations between multiple physical IPVRs and depression symptoms, and multiple physical IPVRs and PTSS. Our models did not find any significant main effects of current physical violence or gender with PTSS or depression symptoms. We also did not find any significant interactions between these variables and singular versus multiple physical IPVRs with psychological symptoms (see Tables 2 and 3 for full results).

Discussion

The current study examined whether experiencing multiple physical IPVRs, compared to a singular physical IPVR, was associated with depression symptoms and PTSS in a sample of romantic partner dyads. It also explored whether these associations were qualified by participants’ identified gender or the severity of physical IPV in the current relationship. Previous literature has demonstrated a strong association between the severity of IPV experiences, depression symptoms, and PTSS (Lagdon et al. 2014; Próspero 2007). Still, few studies have tested relationships among the experience of multiple physical IPVRs, depression symptoms, and PTSS, and those examining multiple IPVRs have primarily focused on individual women (Ørke et al. 2018). This study expanded on previous work by offering critical insight into the experience of physical IPV victimization across multiple relationships, compared to a singular relationship, and associated mental health outcomes for romantic partners.

Multiple Physical IPVRs, Depression, and PTSS

Consistent with our first and second hypotheses, multiple, compared to singular, physical IPVRs were associated with greater severity of depression symptoms and greater severity of PTSS. These results were also found while controlling for ongoing stressors in participants’ relationships (i.e., AUD, current physical IPV), which emphasizes the salience of multiple physical IPVRs with survivors' psychological distress. These findings align with prior research that has found a dose‐response effect, where greater ‘dosage’ to interpersonal violence is associated with adverse mental health outcomes (Charak et al. 2020; Rees et al. 2016). Indeed, the dose‐response effect of multiple physically violent relationships may be linked to survivors' depression and PTSS. Still, future studies that test whether an incremental increase in the number of physical IPVRs is associated with greater risk for psychological distress will be particularly important, given that the current study was limited to secondary analysis of cross‐sectional data that were not originally intended to capture a range of physically violent relationship experiences. These results are also of particular importance given that psychological distress, including depression and PTSS, is linked to physical IPV victimization, including repeated victimization by multiple partners (Cheng and Lo 2022; Spencer et al. 2019b). Thus, interventions targeting depression symptoms and PTSS may be valuable for partners with exposure to multiple physically violent relationships. It will also be important for future work to consider whether sociodemographic factors (e.g., age) or other experiences that are known risk factors for both IPV and distress (e.g., childhood abuse; Coker et al. 2002) alter these results and to further explore the onset of PTSS and depression symptoms in relation to the timing of the multiple physically violent relationships, given the interwoven nature of IPV victimization and psychological distress (Cheng and Low 2022; Spencer et al. 2019b).

We also found that participants' alcohol problem severity was significantly and positively associated with PTSS. Although severity of alcohol use was included as a control variable, it is unsurprising that multiple physical IPVRs and AUD symptoms emerged as unique predictors, given that both have links to PTSS (Matlow and DePrince 2013; Smith 2018). Moreover, relationship violence has been associated with problematic alcohol use behaviors, and alcohol use has also been associated with experiencing violence in multiple relationships (Cafferky et al. 2018; Vatnar & Bjorkly 2008). Given these parallel and bidirectional associations, future work would benefit from examining the intersection and temporal ordering of problematic alcohol use, relationship violence, and PTSS for survivors.

The exploratory analyses revealed that severity of physical IPV in the current relationship did not have a significant effect on the associations among multiple physical IPVRs, depression symptoms, and PTSS. This was unexpected, given that previous work has found current IPV of any type (physical, psychological, or sexual) to be associated with adverse physical and mental health outcomes (Romito et al. 2005). One possible explanation is the measures used in this study. The CTS‐2 asks about physical violence in the current relationship over the past 6 months. Given that the average relationship length was over 6 years for the couples in this study, this may not have captured the full extent of physical violence in the current relationship. Due to the wording of the multiple physical IPVR item (i.e., Have you ever been slapped, punched, kicked, beaten up, or otherwise physically hurt by your spouse (or former spouse), a boyfriend or girlfriend, or some other intimate partner?), it is also possible that participants’ self‐report included any violence from past or present, including their current relationships. Thus, it may be that one's entire history of physical IPV has a more salient association with depression symptoms and PTSS than an isolated snapshot of physical violence in the current relationship. A second possible explanation may be the inclusion criteria for the parent study, which required at least one instance of physical IPV with the participant's current partner. Given this requirement, there may not be enough variability to detect an effect with the current physical IPV variable. These results may look different in a sample of couples with more diversity in IPV experiences with their current partner, which will be important to test in future studies. Even still, these findings emphasize the value of considering the entirety of one's relationship history when attempting to understand IPVR survivors’ mental health outcomes.

Gender Effects

Similar to current physical IPV, our second exploratory aim revealed that gender did not significantly qualify the associations between multiple physical IPVRs and participants' mental health outcomes. Still, this nonsignificant result has important implications. The lack of men in IPVR studies has made it difficult to understand if there are unique gender differences in mental health outcomes, particularly in the context of multiple physical IPVRs (Laskey et al. 2019; Ørke et al. 2018). The findings from this study begin to help fill these gaps, highlighting that both men and women may experience adverse mental health outcomes associated with the experience of multiple physical IPVRs. This emphasizes the need for future studies to consider the representation of men in IPV samples. It also highlights the importance of a thorough violence assessment for both men and women presenting to treatment. Lastly, it suggests the value of developing treatments that consider the unique lived experiences men face in their survivorship in order to best support their healing and recovery from IPVRs.

Limitations and Future Directions

Although the study adds meaningful contributions to the literature, the current findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the sample is cross‐sectional, which prevents directionality and causality from being inferred. Future work would benefit from longitudinal and prospective studies to determine how multiple IPVRs and mental health outcomes are associated over time. Second, while the sample size is sufficient to address the current research questions, a larger sample size would be preferable to increase confidence in the findings. Third, participants' (e.g., race, ethnicity) and couples' (e.g., sexual orientation, gender identity) diversity was limited, thus limiting the generalizability of these results. The data for this study also came from a larger clinical trial focusing on couples reporting AUD and IPV in their current relationship; as a result, generalizability is also limited to couples who share some similar characteristics. Moving forward, these associations should be tested with more diverse samples, both in regard to participants' identities and the types of samples recruited (e.g., community, treatment‐seeking for PTSS and depression). Fourth, although the measures used were valid and reliable, the data were limited in that PTSS scores were not anchored to a specific trauma event, PTSD Criterion A was not required to be met, and the measure for multiple IPVRs was a dichotomous, self‐report item. To contextualize the associations found in this study, future work would benefit from careful consideration of measurement selection and assessment procedures, such as anchoring PCL‐5 scores to survivors' experiences of physical IPV and gathering a more detailed history of participants' violent relationships (e.g., number of relationships, timing), as well as the consideration of other factors that may confound or impact these results (e.g., age, childhood abuse). Lastly, the exploratory findings on gender and violence in the current relationship should be replicated in future work.

Research Implications

The findings from this study offer preliminary evidence of the associations among multiple physical IPVRs, depression symptoms, and PTSS for men and women in romantic relationships. Given the dearth of studies on this topic (Ørke et al. 2018), the field would benefit from continued exploration of how experiencing multiple IPVRs intersects with survivors' recovery and wellbeing. In addition to mental health outcomes, researchers should consider overlapping constructs such as stigma, resources, social support, and coping strategies. Moreover, expanding the focus to include different types of violence (e.g., psychological, sexual) in multiple relationships will be a particularly important next step (Sullivan et al. 2012). Although prior work focusing on women is of the utmost importance, our results also point to the value of including men in IPV research. Despite the prevalence of men's IPV (Leemis et al. 2022), there has been a particularly wide gap in studies that have either included men or focused solely on men's victimization (Laskey et al. 2019). Similarly, gender and sexual minorities have often been underrepresented in the IPV literature (Laskey et al. 2019). Although our study included sexual minority couples, future work would also benefit from planned comparisons to ensure these findings translate to all survivors of IPV.

Clinical Implications

The results from this study have several important clinical implications. For men and women alike, prevention of re‐engagement with physically violent relationships is potentially relevant. Our findings also point to the value of detailed assessments of relationship violence for both men and women, as well as assessment of alcohol use behaviors, to better inform the biopsychosocial processes that may be associated with survivors' mental health. Lastly, the development and testing of psychotherapy interventions for people experiencing IPV victimization have primarily been limited to women (Arroyo et al. 2017). Our findings suggest that connecting people with multiple physically violent relationship experiences to evidence‐based treatments and services may help support their psychological health. Still, more work is needed to determine whether these same treatments and services are equally effective and available for men and women.

Conclusion

In sum, this study found evidence of associations between multiple physical IPVRs and depression symptoms, as well as multiple physical IPVRs and PTSS in a unique sample of high‐risk romantic partner dyads. We also found that problematic alcohol use behaviors were associated with PTSS, yet we did not find that gender or current physical IPV qualified these associations. These results not only illuminate the negative links between multiple physically violent relationships and partners' mental health, but they also begin to establish these associations for both men and women in romantic relationships.
