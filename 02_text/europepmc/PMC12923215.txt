ABSTRACT
Background:Schizophrenia impacts not only patients but also their intimate partners. Sexual dysfunction (SD) among spouses is a critical yet underexplored consequence of this caregiving burden.Aim:To compare sexual functioning in spouses of individuals with schizophrenia versus control spouses.Materials and Methods:A case-control study was conducted involving 120 heterosexual couples—60 with one partner diagnosed with schizophrenia and 60 control couples. Sexual functioning was assessed using the Changes in Sexual Functioning Questionnaire (CSFQ), while symptom severity in patients was measured using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). Statistical analyses included t-tests, Chi-square tests, and correlation analysis.Results:SD was significantly more prevalent among spouses of schizophrenia patients (50% males, 70% females) versus controls (33% and 32%, respectively). Spouses of patients had lower CSFQ scores across all domains. Negative symptoms in patients showed a moderate negative correlation with sexual desire in female spouses. Gender differences were significant, with female spouses reporting greater impairment.Conclusions:Spouses of individuals with schizophrenia experience higher rates of sexual dysfunction, which are influenced by the clinical profile of the patient and caregiving burden.

BODY
Sexual dysfunction (SD) in the spouses of individuals with schizophrenia is an often overlooked but important aspect of the broader impact of schizophrenia on intimate relationships. Schizophrenia, a severe and chronic mental health disorder, characterized by impaired thought processes and poor emotional response, not only affects the individual diagnosed with the condition but also places significant strain on their relationships with family members, particularly their spouses. This strain can manifest in various forms of sexual dysfunction among spouses, including decreased libido, emotional disconnect, and challenges in achieving sexual satisfaction.[1] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are currently 24 million people worldwide livings with schizophrenia (January 2022) and for a significant period after the onset of schizophrenia, one or more major areas of functioning, such as work, interpersonal relationships, or self-care, are significantly below pre-disease levels.[2]

SDs are characterized by disorders of sexual desire and psycho physiological changes associated with the cycle of sexual response in men and women[3] and can present with anorgasmia, delayed or retrograde or premature ejaculation, erectile dysfunction, low desire or poor sexual drive, and pain during sexual activity.[456] Age, psychiatric disorders (schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety disorders), medical disorders, and drugs are some of the factors that interfere with sexual functioning.[7]

Research indicates that sexual dysfunction is prevalent among patients with schizophrenia, with studies reporting rates ranging from 25% to 85%.[891011] These dysfunctions can be attributed to several factors, like disease-related factors (negative symptoms of schizophrenia, such as reduced initiative and motivation, can lead to diminished sexual desire and activity),[51213] psychosocial factors, somatic health, and antipsychotic medications.[561415] Additionally, in female patients, SD was also attributed to factors such as estrogen imbalances and polycystic ovarian syndrome.[16]

Spouses with schizophrenia are not only caregivers but also intimate partners. When romantic expectations are not met, frustration is attributed to the spouse, which directly leads to decreased relationship as well as sexual satisfaction and subsequently burnout and poor role functioning.[171819] The responsibilities associated with caring for a partner with schizophrenia can lead to stress and fatigue, further impacting the marital relationship.[20] Additionally, schizophrenia patients generally have poor communication skills which may further contribute to SD in spouses.[1] Despite the various factors associated with poor sexual functioning in patients of schizophrenia, there is scarcity of data available for sexual functioning of the spouses of schizophrenic patients, pointing toward a need for a multifaceted approach for addressing SDs in spouses. Furthermore, although there are studies assessing sexual functioning in spouses,[1719] we could not find any study that utilized the Changes in Sexual Functioning Questionnaire (CSFQ),[21] a comprehensive tool that assesses various aspects of sexual functioning.

This research article seeks to explore the extent of SD in the spouses of individuals with schizophrenia and also fill the data gap regarding the correlation between the clinical profile of schizophrenic patients and the sexual functioning of their spouses.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This analytical case-control study was conducted in the inpatient department of Psychiatry at Shyam Shah Medical College, Rewa, Madhya Pradesh, spanning over a period of 18 months.

Sample size

Convenience sampling was employed to select a total of 120 couples (n = 240) for the study, comprising 60 couples with one individual diagnosed with schizophrenia and 60 control couples from the general hospital population.

Study participants

Participants consisted of spouses of schizophrenia patients diagnosed as per International Classification of Disease 10 Diagnostic Criteria for Research (ICD-10 DCR).[22] Control couples were selected from the married general hospital population after the selection of cases. Both cases and controls were between 18 and 50 years of age and gave informed written consent. If the spouses of schizophrenia patients or controls had any pre-existing medical or psychiatric disorder, they were excluded from the study.

Study procedure

After selecting the study participants, data were recorded in a semistructured sociodemography proforma. To assess the clinical profile and severity of schizophrenia symptoms, the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)[23] was administered to both male and female patients. It is a 30-item observer-rated scale, with a score range from 30 to 210. It is divided into positive scale, negative scale, and general psychopathology scale. There is 7-point scoring where 1 = absent and 7 = extreme. The interrater reliability of PANSS is 0.80.[24]

Assessment of sexual functioning

Sexual functioning was assessed using the CSFQ, a standardized, reliable, and valid 14-item instrument designed to evaluate multiple domains of sexual functioning. The CSFQ provides five subscale scores related to sexual desire, interest, pleasure, arousal, and orgasm, with separate scoring formats for males and females.[21] The questionnaire was administered to the spouses of both case and control participants, who were asked to reflect on their sexual functioning over the past 12 months.

As the CSFQ was originally developed in English, a culturally adapted Hindi version was created to ensure suitability for the target population. The translation followed a standardized forward and backward translation procedure conducted by two independent bilingual experts. This was followed by pilot testing on a sample of 10 participants to evaluate clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension. Based on the feedback, necessary modifications were made, and the finalized version was administered by the principal investigator. When needed, real-time verbal explanations were provided in Hindi to facilitate understanding and ensure the accuracy and reliability of responses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS)-version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The quantitative variables were described as means, standard deviation, and P value. Student’s t-test for parametric data and Chi-square test for nonparametric data were employed. The quantitative variables were compared with independent t-test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the level for statistical significance was 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The present study aimed to explore and compare sexual functioning among male and female spouses of patients with schizophrenia, alongside their respective control groups. SD in schizophrenia is an established comorbidity with a prevalence of around 55% in male schizophrenics[8] and as high as 96% in female patients with around 70% as average.[910] However, only a handful of studies have commented upon the SD of their spouses.[1719]

The findings indicate a significant difference in sexual functioning, as measured by the CSFQ, between spouses of individuals with schizophrenia and those in the control group and the overall prevalence of SD was 66.6% in male patients (CSFQ total score <47) and 80% in female patients (CSFQ total score <41), both of which were significantly higher than the prevalence observed in the control groups (30% for males and 33% for females). The high prevalence of SD in patients with schizophrenia is consistent with findings from previous studies.[8910] Similarly, the prevalence of SD in the control group aligns with data from several general population surveys.[262728] These findings highlight the complex interplay between chronic psychiatric illness, caregiving burden, and intimate partner dynamics.[141517] This is consistent with existing literature suggesting that the chronicity, unpredictability, and functional impairments associated with schizophrenia can impose considerable psychological and relational strain on partners.[1517] Gender differences observed in this study further underscore the differentiated impact of caregiving roles. Female spouses reported significantly lower sexual functioning than male spouses, particularly in domains related to sexual desire and satisfaction. This aligns with prior research indicating that women in caregiving roles often experience higher levels of emotional and psychological burden.[29] Additionally, cultural factors may play a role; in societies where discussing sexual issues is taboo, women may be less likely to report or seek help for sexual dysfunctions.[1516] Moreover, gender norms around emotional expression and relational expectations may make female spouses more vulnerable to the relational and sexual consequences of long-term caregiving in the context of severe mental illness.

Overall, there were statistically significant differences between the spouses of individuals with schizophrenia and the control group across domains of the CSFQ, which includes sexual desire/frequency, sexual desire/interest, sexual pleasure, sexual arousal, and orgasm.

The mean age of individuals with schizophrenia (both male and female, n = 60) in our study was 37.21 ± 5.12 years, while the mean age of their spouses was 35.58 ± 7.11 years. These findings are consistent with previously reported data.[17] A significant difference was observed in sociodemographic variables and general sexual history between the spouses of individuals with schizophrenia and those in the control group, aligning with the findings of Sahu et al.[20] Further analysis revealed that when sociodemographic variables and general sexual history were correlated with the total CSFQ score, no significant associations were found among either male or female schizophrenia cases. In contrast, within the control population, significant correlations were identified between the total CSFQ score and variables such as education, occupation, socioeconomic status, family type, locality, and current sexual functioning.

There were significant differences between spouses of individuals with schizophrenia and those in the control group with regard to several aspects of sexual behavior and expression. Specifically, disparities were observed in the frequency of engaging in sexual thoughts, initiation of sexual activity, presence of foreplay and afterplay, verbal communication during sex, age of initiation of masturbation, and premarital sexual experiences. These findings suggest that schizophrenia may exert a profound impact on intimate relational dynamics.

Conversely, both groups showed comparable responses in terms of emotional experiences following sexual activity, sources of sexual knowledge, frequency of masturbation, experiences with dating and kissing, number of sexual partners, first sexual encounter with their current spouse, incidence of extramarital sexual activity, and post-marital masturbation.

Some of the observed differences—particularly in initiation of sexual activity and verbal expression during intercourse—can be plausibly explained by the presence of negative symptoms in schizophrenia, such as diminished motivation, emotional expressivity, and social engagement, as documented in previous research.[14] Additionally, cultural attitudes toward sexuality may account for certain similarities across groups, particularly in more socially sensitive or stigmatized behaviors.[30]

Regarding the comparable rates of extramarital sexual activity, it is likely that broader sociocultural factors contribute to this finding. As noted in previous literature, extramarital relationships are often influenced by increased opportunity—such as time spent apart from one’s partner—as well as premarital sexual habits, including patterns of multiple sexual partnerships that may carry over into marriage.[31] These factors may be equally present in both groups, accounting for the lack of significant difference in this domain.

Limitations

The study included only spouses of inpatient schizophrenia patients, and there was a lack of data on outpatients of spouses of schizophrenia patients. Furthermore, only one assessment method was used for sexual functioning. Also, the sample size was small. Future research should aim to further elucidate the mechanisms linking caregiving burden, gender roles, and sexual functioning, ideally through longitudinal and qualitative studies.

CONCLUSION

Our study emphasizes the broader psychosocial impact of schizophrenia beyond symptomatology. While pharmacological and psychosocial interventions primarily focus on the patient, the intimate partners of individuals with schizophrenia also face significant challenges that can affect their mental health and quality of life. SD, in this context, may not only reflect relationship strain but also serve as an indicator of caregiver burnout, depressive symptoms, or unresolved emotional distress. Clinically, these results suggest the importance of adopting a systemic and family-centered approach in psychiatric care.
