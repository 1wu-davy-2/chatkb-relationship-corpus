ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundReligious leaders (RLs) form an influential group in Eswatini. This study explores their understanding and acceptance of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), with a view to improve PrEP service delivery for those who would benefit from PrEP the most.MethodsWe conducted online and in-person in-depth interviews with (n=26) purposefully selected RLs (n=12 female and n=14 male) from Eswatini. Data were managed using NVivo V.12 and coded inductively. We used thematic analysis to analyse our data and structure our findings across the individual, interpersonal, community and institutional levels of an ecological framework.ResultsRLs have the willingness, potential and influence to promote PrEP in their church communities. They are aware of the community need for HIV prevention, but describe a conflict between their professional role as a pastor and their personal commitment to the well-being of their church community. Participants expressed a dilemma between their pastoral ethics— which oppose sex outside of marriage—and the conflicting challenges of HIV stigma and evidence-based health promotion among church members. Discussing PrEP could compromise their credibility and expose them to potential censure from religious authorities and the church community.ConclusionRLs can play an important role in the community acceptance and implementation of PrEP but are conflicted between community health and their professional role. Addressing the sources of this conflict and offering tailored training about HIV prevention and PrEP is essential to reduce stigma and benefit from RLs’ influence in the church community.

BODY
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Religious institutions play a significant role in HIV and AIDS prevention and care in sub-Saharan Africa.

Religious institutions are also considered to fuel HIV-related stigma and the spread of misinformation.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Religious leaders (RLs) experience a conflict between their personal beliefs and professional responsibilities, which limits their willingness and capacity to promote pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).

Using an ecological framework, we identify factors across multiple levels influencing this conflict that are closely tied to the stigmatisation of HIV and sexuality.

We make recommendations on how this conflict can be addressed and where RLs can be leveraged to best support PrEP programmes.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Integrating RLs and their institutions into HIV and PrEP policy is essential for recognising their influential role and to reduce barriers that may limit their support for community acceptance of new HIV prevention methods.

Evidence-based training, tailored to the working context of RLs, is crucial to equip and empower them to discuss PrEP and sexuality with their church community.

Religious authorities, theologians and other community leaders must work collaboratively with other practitioners to foster a stigma-free and supportive environment that encourages RLs’ active involvement in PrEP programmes.

Introduction

Religious leaders (RLs) are a trusted and integral part of communities in sub-Saharan Africa16 and can influence the health and sexual risk behaviour of their church members.3 6 7

As RLs know their community members personally, are highly respected, are based within the community and have a unique and wide community reach, RLs qualify as influential allies in overcoming the AIDS epidemic.2 6 8

With 62% (2020) of the population in sub-Saharan Africa identifying as Christians,9 Christianity plays an essential social role in many communities.10 11 Faith-based health organisations are providers of health and HIV services in sub-Saharan Africa11215 and churches in sub-Saharan Africa are known to have one of the most far-reaching social and organisational networks globally.10 12 13 16

Evidence suggests that the role of the church in the HIV response in sub-Saharan Africa is ambivalent.10 On the one hand, Christian values highlight religious responsibility to love and care for the sick.10 Churches provided comprehensive support for orphans11 and palliative care to people living with HIV (PLHIV) who had progressed to the stage of AIDS.12 17 Christian churches and church organisations provide health advice and education7 10 18 and can offer spiritual and emotional support for people living with and affected by HIV.1117 1921 On the other hand, some churches are seen as the main drivers of spreading HIV stigma and misinformation by linking HIV to immoral and sinful behaviour,2 10 11 21 22 negatively influencing medication adherence,2 and distorting risk perception.13 Many churches limit their prevention messages to abstinence and fidelity and stigmatise sexual relationships outside of marriage.10 This has in some contexts led to a reduction in condom use.10 23 24

In their scoping review, which analysed 29 studies primarily located in African countries and the USA, Vigliotti et al show a positive influence of religion, faith and spirituality on HIV prevention activities,25 including condom use.24 25 However, they include only one study about pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for the prevention of HIV25 and highlight the need for further research on the influence of religion on PrEP uptake and use.6 25

RLs have not been included in supporting the rollout of PrEP, which remains suboptimal in terms of uptake and adherence in many settings.26 27 In Eswatini—the country with the highest HIV prevalence globally28—evidence demonstrates that community approaches are essential for the dissemination of information about PrEP that is accessible and appropriate.27 29 30 This qualitative study examines the role that RLs in Eswatini play in HIV prevention, and factors limiting and enabling their involvement in PrEP rollout within their church communities.

Methods

Study setting

Our study took place in the Kingdom of Eswatini, formerly Swaziland, which borders Mozambique and South Africa.31 Eswatini has approximately 1.09 million inhabitants (2017)32 and the majority identifies as Christians.32 33 Eswatini has three main church bodies: the Council of Swaziland Churches, the Eswatini Conference of Churches and the League of African Churches.34 Most of the churches in Eswatini support conservative Christian values such as abstaining from sex before marriage, avoiding contraceptives and opposing abortion.33 Besides Christianity, ‘Swazi Religion’,33 which stems from various African traditional religions and traditions, dominates the religious landscape in Eswatini.33 Eswatini has met the UNAIDS 95-95-95 antiretroviral treatment targets;35 however, in 2021/22, only 50.9% of the female and 46.5% of the male young population (15–24 years) demonstrated a comprehensive understanding of HIV prevention methods.36

Study design

We employed a formative qualitative design using in-depth interviews (IDIs). Our study follows the qualitative reporting guidelines as detailed in the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research.37

Data collection

From October 2021 to February 2022, we conducted 26 IDIs with purposefully selected male and female RLs from Eswatini. Participants were recruited by the first (HG), third (MM) and last (KB) authors and aimed to capture perspectives from RLs of different genders, age, years of experience and community reach working in a church that is affiliated with one of the main church bodies in Eswatini. Our author from Eswatini (MM), fluent in English and SiSwati, conducted 21 IDIs in-person and if requested by the participant, via a phone call,38 following COVID-19 prevention guidelines.39 40 The first author (HG) conducted the remaining five IDIs via online calls in English. The interview tools included a participant information sheet, an informed consent form and an interview guide in both SiSwati and English. We obtained written informed consent prior to the outset of interviews, which were conducted in a private location and audio-recorded. We conducted the IDIs in SiSwati and English and pseudonymised all personal information. We collected socio-demographic information such as age, gender and education status. The interview guide included open-ended questions about the participants’ personal perspectives and experiences with HIV prevention and PrEP. We made reflexive and observational notes during every interview, transcribed the interviews verbatim, and then—where required—translated them into English. All documents were pseudonymised and, together with the recordings, saved in password-protected devices. We conducted debriefing sessions after every interview.41 We endet the data collection phase after we had agreed with the research assistant and author from Eswatini,that we had reached enough ‘information power’.42

Data analysis

We used reflexive thematic analysis4345 to analyse the transcripts and debriefing, observational and reflexive notes. KB and HG who were both part of the data collection team completed the analysis. KB and HG discussed the content of the debriefing notes during the data collection phase. The content of the debriefing notes generally matched with the final results.

Once full transcripts were available, we began a process of reading and re-reading the transcripts to build data familiarisation (phase 1). Extensive notes were made during this phase. After this, we generated codes of the transcripts, and observational and reflexive notes (phase 2). We then analysed and organised the codes into themes (phase 3). After discussing the themes, KB and HG agreed on the most prominent themes (phase 4)45 and began to see that many resonated with an ecological framework.46 47 Hence, we adapted our themes to the framework and conducted another round of code allocation of the entire dataset to the themes we felt linked to social ecology (phase 5). Considering the literature, we built our storyline (phase 6)45 and organised our results within our final organising framework and shared this with the author team. To minimise analysis bias,48 we only did a targeted literature research after completing phase 3. Data were managed using NVivo V.12.49

Reflexivity statement

The motivation for this research was a previous study2 where community leaders repeatedly emphasised the influence of RLs on the community and the importance of involving RLs in community-based PrEP promotion.2 Therefore, in response to the findings from Goymann et al,2 we selected RLs based within communities that are confronted daily with HIV and health issues among their church members.

The first author is a clinical doctor by training. During her studies, she lived and worked in urban and rural hospitals in sub-Saharan Africa, India and Brazil. She completed a fellowship with UNAIDS Brazil and is currently working as a research associate and finalising her medical PhD. In her youth, she was an active member of the protestant church community, but left church as an adult. The last author, an atheist, was part of the original data collection team from the Eswatini PrEP demonstration project, and as such has analysed the data that led us to conduct these additional interviews. The third author conducted the in-person interviews with the RLs and was also part of the original Eswatini PrEP demonstration project.

The first, second and last authors have experience of Christian-led and non-Christian-led healthcare facilities and health organisations in high-prevalence HIV settings and noted the positive and negative impacts of Christian religion and RLs on patients and their health behaviour in terms of support and care, stigma and misinformation. The first and last authors have worked in a number of settings where they feel they have seen people stigmatised as a result of RLs’ stance on sexual well-being and education. The second author collaborated with programme implementers in the past who equipped churches for an evidence-based response to HIV and has also seen many positive examples in addition to RLs who stigmatised PLHIV. Alongside a project team with diverse views and experiences, we ensured groundedness in the local context and understanding by holding regular debriefing sessions with our author from Eswatini to compare our impressions, to discuss rising challenges and context-related questions.

Strategies employed to ensure rigour

Lincoln and Guba define four criteria for achieving rigour in qualitative research: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.50 Credibility involves accurately capturing the phenomena under study, transferability refers to the extent to which the findings can be generalised to other contexts, dependability emphasises the use of reliable research methods that yield consistent findings and confirmability ensures that the findings are shaped by the participants’ responses rather than the researcher’s biases or interests.50 Throughout each phase of the research process, these criteria were systematically applied to ensure rigour throughout the study. This involved the revision of interview guides, thick description through rich interviews, building reoccurring themes in multiple data sources, including observational notes, and debriefings. Furthermore, we elicited feedback on our initial findings via member reflections51 with our colleagues in Eswatini and searched for negative cases to affirm our key finding. Analysis of observational and debriefing notes alongside our interview allowed us to triangulate several key themes. From each of these data sources, we revealed the conflicting and contradicting attitudes towards HIV prevention as the dominant theme, which persisted throughout the debriefing sessions and was evident in the final data analysis.

Ecological framework

We organise our results following an adapted ecological framework of health behaviour as described by Glanz and Rimer47 and McLeroy et al.46 In their framework, McLeroy et al describe health behaviour as being influenced by ‘intrapersonal factors, interpersonal processes, institutional factors, community factors, and public policy’.46 These factors are dynamic and operate not in isolation but in a system whereby each factor is influenced by the other, that is, ‘behavior both affects, and is affected by, multiple levels of influence; second, individual behavior both shapes, and is shaped by, the social environment’ (Glanz and Rimer, p. 10).47 Glanz and Rimer47 refer to this as ‘reciprocal causation’.

We apply a modified four-level framework (figure 1) which includes the individual, interpersonal, community and institutional levels. Individual factors are based on the individual beliefs and experiences of the RLs. We frame the relationship between RLs and their church members as interpersonal factors. Both individual and interpersonal factors, including the knowledge, beliefs, experiences and attitudes of the individual and their social relationships, are linked to the perspectives on sexuality, HIV and PrEP, and with whom, how and where these topics should be discussed. The community level is represented by the church community and social network in which the RLs live and work. Here, social norms are influenced by a stigmatising and conservative perspective towards sexuality, HIV and PrEP. The institutional level describes how their behaviours are shaped by the church authorities, pastoral ethics and the bible.

The four-level framework outlines the individual, interpersonal, community and institutional levels that shape religious leaders’ conflicting attitudes towards pre-exposure prophylaxis promotion. Each level is impacted by stigma and reciprocal influence.

Stigma, defined as a process, ‘when elements of labeling, stereotyping, separation, status loss, and discrimination occur together in a power situation that allows them’ (Link and Phelan, p. 377),52 is present throughout every level of the framework and we consider this to be a key factor that is influenced and influencing how information is shared between each level.

We describe ‘reciprocal causation’47 as ‘reciprocal influences’, in relation to the personal beliefs of the RLs, their social relationships, community norms and institutional rules. In some instances, there are influences that overlap, so we have allocated these to levels where we see the most relevance.

Results

We conducted in-depth interviews with 12 female and 14 male RLs from Eswatini. Most of the interviewed RLs were above 50 years of age and had a community reach of 100 to 500 members. One RL had a community reach of more than 10 000 members. 11 of the RLs were in position for 1–10 years and 4 RLs were working as a RL for over 20 years. Almost all interviewed RLs completed tertiary education and the vast majority worked for a Pentecostal-type church. Only one RL did not know about PrEP before the interview and half of the RLs interviewed had already promoted PrEP in their community (table 1). We organise our results by firstly presenting the RL’s conflicting roles towards community health and PrEP promotion. We then describe the factors influencing the RLs’ behaviour towards PrEP promotion following an ecological framework of individual, interpersonal, community and institutional levels47 as described in our framework. We use an * to highlight where the quote comes from one of four participants who were most supportive of PrEP, as some of the quotes act as ‘deviant cases’53 and contradict some of the themes we present here. These cases are considered in the Discussion section.

Demographics of participants

To mantainmaintain confidentiality of our participants, we do not present the specific church names.

RLs who, in addition to regular counselling as RLs, have been or are actively involved in providing HIV services to their community, working as an expert client, a nurse and midwife, in an Non-governmental organizationNGO offering HIV services and in an HIV- specific role with the Council of Churches.

The conflicting role towards PrEP

Most RLs support the use of PrEP and are aware that church community members are at risk of HIV and could benefit from PrEP. Since they know that many members are at risk of acquiring HIV, they would like to promote PrEP and advise married and unmarried members who are at risk to use PrEP. One RL explained that “personally, I like that people are protected from HIV” but she can only “tell them to use the pill as a private person, not a pastor” (female RL #20). Their pastoral role is seen as a hindrance to the promotion of PrEP:

If I wasn’t a pastor, I would be telling them about condoms, contraceptives, and PrEP. But I preach abstinence to the youth as HIV prevention methods should not be for them as they are not supposed to have sex (male RL #8).

One RL told us that “I can freely talk about PrEP behind closed doors like when counseling partners that want to marry or those that have found their HIV statuses are different” (male RL #5). As a pastor, however, some RLs felt that even counselling the married couple about PrEP is problematic as it could imply one partner was being unfaithful.

Some RLs felt that their pastoral role collides with reality and that this is “a huge conflict” (female RL #18):

So, you can see life of a pastor is difficult because people are already having sex yet you are busy telling them not to have sex. The fire is already burning so you must have an idea how to put it out not asking how it started (male RL #10).

HIV was seen as a major health challenge in their church communities and RLs felt “very passionate that people in the community must be in good health” (male RL #14). In general, almost all interviewed RLs approved PrEP as “a good HIV prevention way” (male RL #14) and “a game changer in HIV prevention as it will greatly reduce HIV infections” (female RL #16). Only one RL stated that she is not “going to be an agent for that [PrEP]” (female RL #17).

Individual factors

Personal belief around what constitutes sinful behaviour was common when discussing PrEP. Abstinence before marriage influenced the perception that only a “married couple who knows that their HIV status is different” (male RL #14) should use PrEP. RLs tended to label sex outside marriage as sinful behaviour:

That person is sinning against God, and I am going to continue speaking about sin until they see that sin is sin. I won’t be like because you have sinned now, I am going to give you PrEP so that you cannot get HIV. NO and NO and NO (female RL #17).

Some RLs described PrEP as giving members a “green light to have [unmarried] sex” (female RL #18) which would be against “my values as a pastor” (female RL #12) and could be seen as leading members to sin. RLs felt this could have negative spiritual consequences for the church community. Concerning sexually active youth, the majority believed that “they are not supposed to have sex” (male RL #8) and hence, mainly promote abstinence. Otherwise, it would “feel like I would be diverting from the mandate of delivering spiritually” (female RL #16).

The RLs’ personal beliefs and attitudes towards sexuality and HIV prevention were strongly influenced by Christian values and ethical standards, shaped on an institutional level. One RL was concerned that if not sticking to these values, “I feel like I would be moving away from preaching people the word of God” (female RL #16).

At the same time, most of the RLs stated that HIV is real and has a negative impact on the church community, “having so many parents die due to less knowledge about HIV thus not knowing how to manage the disease” (male RL #14).

Many RLs firmly believed that people living with and affected by HIV should not be stigmatised and that evidence-based prevention and treatment of HIV are key for a healthy community. One male RL highlighted that “we should remove the stigma on HIV positive people and this virus must be treated like any virus or disease we know” (#4).

This sentiment was particularly clear when speaking with RLs who have worked in the area of HIV and found themselves “immersed in the real situation and working with people suffering or have been affected directly by HIV/AIDS” (female RL #25)* had less stigmatising beliefs and values. They did not see people who acquired HIV “as a sinner” (male RL #24)* and felt that “you cannot judge them as having been immoral or having been careless in their behavior” (female RL #25)*. In their understanding, “people are people and they are made in the image of God. So, they should be respected and they should be given the respect that they deserve” (male RL #24)*. RLs who did not problematise promoting PrEP to the community explained that in the “area of sex, we [the RLs] normally over-emphasize that the person that you are supposed to have sex with is only the wife or only the husband. Not any other person” (male RL #24)*. They perceived sex outside of marriage as part of the church community reality and wanted to “give them [community members] some ideas on how to they can protect themselves from HIV and also we encourage those that are HIV positive already to disclose” (male RL #24)*.

Interpersonal factors

RLs spoke of long-term and close interpersonal relationships with church community members. Most RLs said they experienced challenges talking in detail about PrEP with their members because this would be incompatible with the attitudes and expectations their members have of them:

Talking about PrEP to everyone inside church when I am at the pulpit is not a good idea because the message will land on wrong ears. Do I need to tell a 60-year-old to use PrEP? Do I tell youth in front of their parents? NO (male RL #8).

Moreover, many RLs felt that they could not preach abstinence and promote PrEP in parallel as this would confuse the members and would negatively impact their credibility and relationship with them:

Just imagine a pastor telling people in public ‘please use PrEP to protect yourself from your cheating spouse’. Instead of solving the issue of cheating we are telling spouse to continue cheating and on our side we will protect ourselves (male RL #10).

One RL stated “say I tell you to use PrEP but on Sunday I also tell them to abstain from sex until they get married” (male RL #3) to demonstrate the confusion mixed messages could cause. Consequently, they feared that they would lose church member’s trust and not be taken seriously if promoting something that was against the assumed word of God:

They cannot explain the gory details of some HIV prevention programs like condoms and PrEP because they will feel like they are telling their members to have sex but protect themselves yet on the other hand preach about abstinence and only have sex after marriage (male RL #9).

Overall, RLs seemed to understand the church community members’ realities and difficulties that they are facing regarding HIV prevention. Equipped with HIV knowledge and immersed in the church community, they were able to tailor their advice to individuals or specific risk groups such as women, knowing that this might cause conflicts with other members:

(…) PrEP mostly is used by women so sometimes one thing that can bring fear into your life [as a RL] is the husbands of the wives that you help. That you have spoken to about PrEP. Because some, they [the husbands] might threaten you, some, they want to quiet you, and some, they can. (…). It is only in church where I don’t fear to talk about PrEP because I will have a womens service where at the end, I will tell them that this is not information to talk about with your husband or no. What you can tell their husbands is only trying to make them aware that there is this PrEP that can do A, B, C, D, (…). But it is a matter of making sure he understands (male RL #24)*.

Community factors

Many RLs described stigmatising norms and beliefs within the church community which limited their capability to promote PrEP. Some RLs felt that community HIV stigma is still very present:

It’s [stigma] full there, it is full there. Especially in churches. I think that I have said earlier, that people they are capable to come to their pastor and declare status. But they are not capable to declare their status among the other congregates. Because of the stigma issues, people still have them (male RL #22)*.

Moreover, people “still stigmatize HIV-positive people, they feel like they should not be closer to them” (male RL #5). However, most RLs felt that they can influence their church community and described themselves as role models. Some RLs explained that they care about both the spiritual well-being of their members and an holistic approach to well-being which includes health, economic, crime and community development issues. They spoke broadly about community issues and “invite the police to talk about crime, health workers about health, business people about starting businesses, social welfare to talk on family issues and others on different issues” (female RL #20). Participants also explained that the church provides education and sometimes financial support to the church community.

RLs explained that they are involved in HIV education and invite HCWs to talk about HIV, give marriage counselling which includes HIV education, or talk about HIV during church services or funerals.

A male RL (#3) highlighted the church as an important platform to provide information to its community:

Good for the fact that us or churches have a wider reach in the community and people believe in their man of God which are the pastors so if we spread the word people would listen. I think they would listen more than they would to a nurse. A church is known as a holy place and it’s a direct conduit to God through the pastor, so anything that is said by the pastor or church they will take or believe it. In some cases, pastors are role models.

Institutional factors

RLs described a working environment that, although physically removed from higher church authorities, remained heavily influenced by their authority, pastoral ethics and biblical interpretation. Some RLs explained that they counsel their members on “how to overcome problems they [members] are facing based on the bible and (…) subjects that I was doing as part of my diploma at the Theology college” (female RL #12). Their theological training and awareness of church hierarchies limited the content and way RLs could discuss PrEP with their church community. Some RLs described that it will be difficult for them to advise PrEP to their members without disagreeing with their institution. Two RLs stated very clearly that they feared receiving censure from the governing church bodies if talking about PrEP:

Some might take my message and twist it to their gain saying pastor [name] said we must use the PrEP pill which would be interpreted as saying I am allowing everyone to go about have sex as long as they take the pill which will be against the bible principles and the church (male RL #3).

Additionally, two RLs refused to be recorded as they feared that, if they were advising people against their pastoral ethics, they would be censured by the authorities. For this reason, one participant felt uncomfortable giving personal information due to the interview topic. A male RL (#14) summarised “I don’t know how we can do it so that we are able to acknowledge that and talk more about HIV prevention in the church without expecting much rebuke from church members and our governing bodies”.

The overall sentiment was that the pastoral ethics and the biblical interpretation of their church are against “talking more about sex or encouraging sex” (male RL #14). RLs expressed concern about talking about HIV prevention and sexuality in church since “the Bible doesn’t allow that [HIV prevention]” (female RL #19). The RLs described the church members would follow and trust in the Bible text as “God’s word” (male RL #15) where sex outside marriage is mostly interpreted as sinful behaviour.

Promoting PrEP bore the danger for RLs of harming the relationship with their church members on an interpersonal level and losing influence and trust in their church community.

Moreover, some RLs explained that the “pastoral ethics don’t allow [talking about HIV prevention] either” (female RL #19). Some RLs feared negative consequences from the institution by disrespecting the institutional ethics as stated by one RL:

The best way would be to use people who are not pastors because we are tied between instilling bible principles that are God’s words and then also following pastoral ethics (male RL #9).

Moreover, one RL living with HIV (self-reported) and having worked as an expert client (a person living with HIV supporting another person who is living with HIV), emphasised the importance of sharing detailed information about HIV prevention:

What works is when a pastor has deep knowledge of what really is HIV prevention and the methods used. What also works is straight talk about HIV prevention unfortunately for us pastors we can’t always do straight talks because it may clash with our pastoral ethics (female RL #6).

A conflict arose for many RLs when they wanted to follow the word of God and HIV prevention simultaneously, as PrEP is “not scriptured in the Bible; a book that us pastors must teach people because it is God’s word” (male RL #21). One RL even stated that it is “so unfortunate that we cannot change what the bible says that young people should abstain from sex until they are married” (female RL #16). The pastoral ethics and biblical understanding not only had an impact on RLs’ PrEP promotion behaviour but also influenced their values, beliefs and attitudes:

I am a pastor so my job is to instill God’s principles to everyone who cares to listen and nowhere I am mandated to promote condoms or telling people either married or youth to use condoms (female RL #13).

Promoting PrEP “would be against what I am here for or what God sent me to preach” (female RL #13).

Some RLs felt that HIV stigma is still very present in the churches and needs to be addressed to improve prevention services:

If you are HIV positive you find that people, they are running away from you, even in churches. So the stigma is still there. So I think this is an issue that we still need to work on especially (male RL #22)*.

One RL highlighted the importance to actively involve RLs in HIV education and programmes to overcome stigma and the dissemination of misinformation:

I think that can be a good move because looking back at first when this issue of HIV started, I think most of the church leaders they were left behind and with them being left behind, they have a wrong information about HIV. (…) So I think bringing them on board can be a great move. In short: I support that (male RL #22)*.

It is noteworthy that our data show contradictive statements by RLs about HIV prevention and PrEP. While young people are viewed as a primary target group for PrEP since they are perceived to be at risk, many RLs cannot reconcile this with the understanding of their work that highlights “abstinence” as “the way to go according to the Bible” (male RL #14). Some RLs explained that they can only advise married couples in a private setting about PrEP. But in another part of the interview mention, “if single I would talk of abstinence but because I know they have sex anyway I would then advise on using PrEP” (male RL #8).

My reaction as a pastor would be the same that the young person needs to abstain from sex or get married first and explain the dangers of having sex before marriage. I would then try and win him or her by saying what is in the bible and then mention that because some youth can’t control themselves. PrEP is another form of HIV prevention that those eligible can use to protect themselves from HIV (female RL #18).

Furthermore, RLs described groups such as truck drivers, sex workers, the youth and people in polygamous marriages or with unfaithful partners as eligible for PrEP, but when face-to-face in an advisory role, they could only advise married sero-discordant couples to use PrEP.

Four RLs explained that they had no problem talking about PrEP with any church member and giving advice on its use. One told us that they “do not feel it is something bad or it is something that we cannot do” (male RL #24)*. Another RL (male #22)* noted that “I don’t have a problem with that [talking about PrEP during a religious event], I can do that with pleasure”. Three participants work or have worked in HIV-specific roles and two belong to the Council of Swaziland Churches, a national body that unites different churches and denominations in the spirit of ecumenism and a strive for justice and peace.54

Discussion

We present findings from 26 RLs who have the potential, willingness and influence to support the rollout of PrEP in their church community. Our participants expressed a profound personal wish to protect and educate their members, are aware of the sexually active church community—married and unmarried— and hence, the necessity for HIV prevention. However, their professional role as a pastor and beliefs about sex and HIV limit their desire and capabilities to promote PrEP to all members. This ambivalence and insecurity are evident through various contradictions within the interviews. In specific contexts such as dealing with youth, the interviewed RLs told us that they see PrEP as a good prevention method but later—within the same interview—the same RLs explained that abstinence is the main or only prevention approach they teach to the youth.

Contradictory attitudes towards PrEP are observed in different settings and contexts5558 in relation to condom use,58 same-sex relationships56 57 and the use of contraceptives.55 In a South African study, RLs described challenges in talking freely about HIV because they fear conflicts with their church members, authorities and personal values, while acknowledging that the youth is sexually active and needs information on condom use. Still, the main prevention message was abstinence and only some RLs promoted condom use to sexually active youth.58 PrEP implementors from South Africa noted PrEP community acceptance as a challenge as the community feared that PrEP would—as an indirect consequence—promote sex among the youth.59 This resonates with our work, where advising HIV prevention and PrEP outside of marriage was perceived as promoting promiscuity, which equated to endorsing immoral and sinful behaviour.

In their review, Ochillo et al conclude that sex education provided in church settings does not reflect the sexual health needs of sexually active youth and recommends that a more holistic health approach is required.13 As in our study, the general support of PrEP was also observed among RLs from Malawi6 and Kenya.3 Studies from sub-Saharan Africa concur that RLs care about the health of their church members6 and want to engage in HIV care3 6 because they felt that a person can only be healthy if receiving both biomedical and spiritual support.6 Still, receiving the support of religious organisations is seen as a major challenge in the PrEP scale-up.60 In South Africa, PrEP implementors felt that RLs may negatively influence PrEP interventions by disseminating misinformation.59 One possible approach at the public health policy level could be to exclude RLs from PrEP promotion, given the stigma many experience, express and reinforce may hinder efforts to improve uptake. However, since RLs serve as key providers of health information2 and HIV remains such a challenge to community health, it is essential to offer training59 and build HIV literacy61 among RLs and church authorities. Strengthening their understanding and acceptance of HIV and evidence-based prevention strategies can foster more supportive engagement in HIV interventions.

We use an ecological framework to present the RLs’ conflicting perceptions towards PrEP. This includes stigmatising PLHIV and branding sex outside of marriage as immoral as well as the assumption that by seeing ‘sexuality as a gift of God’11 and by sending positive, empowering messages,62 the RLs would in some way abandon Christian and community values. In the Eswatini context, the RLs are located in a community environment where stigmatising people living with and being affected by HIV is very present.2 63 This stigmatising environment negatively impacts the well-being of PLHIV63 and the implementation of HIV prevention methods.27 30 This resonates with our framework where reciprocal influence is evident as stigma and theological convictions towards HIV and sexuality is a driving, limiting factor for PrEP promotion at every level. In our case, the individual conflicting beliefs and attitudes of the RLs towards PrEP and sexuality are supported and perpetuated by the institutional level, including the pastoral ethics and biblical scriptures.11 This runs in parallel to the community norms and expectations that shape the RLs’ attitudes towards HIV prevention and PrEP.

Four participants stood out in their opinions regarding the promotion and advice for PrEP use. When coding, these four interviews constituted our ‘negative’ cases, and we revisited them to see if we could identify what made them different. Three had face-to-face and hands-on experience with PLHIV, and two were members of the Council of Swaziland Churches. While it is not possible for us to identify if these experiences override the morality conflicts that other RLs describe, we can assume that their work and affiliations required training and education that others had not received.

The ecological perspective highlights that a change in health behaviour is complex and can only be achieved via a multilevel approach, considering that behaviour is influenced by and influences the social environment.47 64 Hence, educational interventions to change individual behaviour are more effective and sustainable, if supported by the social environment and appropriate policies.47 65 This is evident in our data where four RLs who had extensive HIV and AIDS education and had previously worked in positions in relation to HIV prevention seemed to have fewer personal conflicts in promoting PrEP and to be more open to evidence-based approaches.

Education programmes by the International Network of Religious Leaders Living with or Personally Affected by HIV and AIDS (INERELA+),66 a network by RLs who live openly with HIV, have shown that educating RLs can enable stigma-free and safe environments to talk about HIV prevention and empower RLs to be open about their HIV status in their church community. The World Council of Churches Ecumenical HIV and AIDS Initiatives and Advocacy (WCC-EHAIA)67 and INERELA+68 have been effectively providing education and advocacy and connecting (HIV-positive) RLs across all levels of the church hierarchy—from the grassroots to church authorities and other stakeholders—to establish and support evidence-based HIV approaches in religious communities.68 69 Given this success, these organisations could be consulted for future interventions focused on PrEP promotion and conflict resolution within the church. It would be important to ascertain the number of RLs who could be engaged in such programmes and if this engagement reflects the priorities of the RLs and societal expectations in Eswatini.

Moreover, programmes that promote new analytical tools for interpreting Biblical texts can lead to a deeper understanding of issues regarding sexuality and HIV.6971 Such programmes scrutinise long-held beliefs and offer a new perspective where HIV is seen as a punishment of God due to sinful sexual behaviour.11 71 Learning to read the Bible differently and coming to a different theological understanding of sexuality and HIV is an important first step in leading to a transformation in churches’ HIV prevention work11 as exemplified by WCC-EHAIA70 and INERELA.18

While the community level is important, for the interviewed RLs, the institutional level of pastoral ethics, their biblical understanding and church authorities greatly impacted their capabilities and willingness to promote PrEP. Involving and educating religious authorities and theologians are crucial for creating a safe and supportive environment for the RLs. Christian networks such as the WCC,72 INERELA+73 and the Churches United to Stop HIV (CUSH)74 could be recruited to co-create contextual, faith-sensitive HIV prevention by working closely with religious authorities, theologians and RLs. These networks can support church authorities in adapting their local church policies in a way that promotes Christian values and evidence-based HIV prevention, reflecting the reality of the church community and combating stigma. Implementing a church-based health ministry75 can bring together members, RLs and the authorities to leverage the church as a safe space of health education, empowerment and advocacy, which promotes the well-being of its members.75

Actively involving other highly respected community stakeholders such as community leaders2 29 30 or traditional healers2 76 will allow for strong, sustainable partnerships between the community, health institutions and churches.

Limitations

Our study participants were limited to RLs from Eswatini, who generally represent a relatively conservative Christian perspective.33 The participants predominantly belonged to Pentecostal churches that tend to interpret Biblical texts literally and hold a more conservative view on HIV prevention. It did not include many perspectives of RLs from other church denominations, nor the perspective of church authorities. We therefore emphasise the need to engage with all levels of the church hierarchy to learn more about their perspectives around PrEP and HIV prevention and identify opportunities for interventions.

Five interviews were conducted online in English. This was to limit researcher bias by using more than one data collector and to mitigate the challenges with travel during the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite some challenges with an unstable internet connection, our participants were fluent in English, were rich in information and complemented the in-person interviews well.38

Our purposive selection of participants inevitably shaped the perspectives captured in this study. We sought to engage RLs who were representative of the general population in Eswatini, both in terms of their lived experiences within their communities and their diverse religious affiliations. The strong support for PrEP observed among this conservative group reflects this selection. The RLs uphold the restriction of HIV prevention and sex to married couples, yet they are also conscious of serving communities where HIV prevalence—a highly stigmatised, sexually transmitted infection—exceeds 25%. This creates a profound tension between their religious teachings and the pressing public health realities they face. Rather than viewing their support for PrEP as only contradictory, it underscores the adaptive role RLs play in responding to the needs of their communities. In this context, unwavering opposition to biomedical HIV prevention would be more surprising and concerning, as it would suggest a complete detachment from the lived experiences of those they serve. It highlights that there is room to leverage support for PrEP at this level, if approved by church authorities.

Conclusion

RLs could play a pivotal role in the successful rollout of PrEP. However, their ability to provide information on HIV prevention is shaped by complex, multilevel factors. On an institutional level, these factors include the pastoral ethics and a biblical mandate enforced by church authorities. This creates tensions between their role as spiritual and moral guides and their capacity to openly discuss evidence-based prevention methods. Addressing these barriers is critical to reducing the spread of stigmatising beliefs and fostering greater acceptance of biomedical HIV prevention strategies. Additionally, engaging church authorities and theologians is essential for cultivating a stigma-free environment that empowers RLs to actively support HIV prevention within their church communities.
