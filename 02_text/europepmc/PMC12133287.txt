ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on a nationally representative survey of the Canadian population conducted in 2019/2020, this study examined the prevalence and determinants of nonphysical intimate partner violence (NP-IPV). NP-IPV was defined as experiences of emotional abuse, controlling behaviors, or economic abuse during the past 5 years. Women (17.3%) and men (16.9%) were equally likely to report NP-IPV, often without co-occurring physical/sexual IPV. For both genders, the risk of NP-IPV decreased with age and increased with financial strain and having a disability. Childhood maltreatment (sexual abuse, emotional abuse, and exposure to emotional IPV for women and sexual abuse and emotional abuse for men) was associated with an increased likelihood of reporting NP-IPV in adulthood. Other risk factors included heavy episodic drinking (self and/or spouse/partner) for women and living in a neighborhood with high levels of social disorder for men. Having confidence in the police was a protective factor for both genders. These associations generally persisted in regression analyses controlling for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV. Future research should focus on methods of preventing NP-IPV and the development of gender-specific interventions to reach and support those who experience NP-IPV. Furthermore, there is a need to improve and standardize measures of NP-IPV.

BODY
Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is defined as any “behaviour by an intimate partner or ex-partner that causes physical, sexual or psychological harm.”1 Determinants and outcomes of physical and sexual IPV have been studied extensively.2-5 Compared with men, women are at increased risk of severe and frequent physical and/or sexual IPV and more serious outcomes, including psychological consequences, injuries, and death by homicide.6-8 Although the definition of IPV includes nonphysical IPV (NP-IPV), relative to physical/sexual IPV, knowledge about the determinants and outcomes of non-physical IPV is limited.9-11

Among studies examining NP-IPV, most have focused on emotional abuse, controlling behaviors, and economic abuse. Emotional abuse is typically defined as acts and comments aimed at undermining a partner’s self-respect and sense of worth;9,12-14 controlling behaviors include acts aimed at restricting a partner’s access to friends and family and demands for accountability of actions and whereabouts;14 economic abuse is defined as acts aimed at making a partner economically dependent through acts of controlling finances (even if the partner earns an independent outcome).15

A meta-analysis of health correlations of NP-IPV found associations with suicidal ideation, posttraumatic stress symptoms, anxiety and depressive symptoms, borderline personality disorder, psychological distress, anger, poor physical health, and somatic symptoms.11 Moreover, longitudinal studies have found that NP-IPV is a precursor of physical/sexual IPV, further increasing the risk of negative health outcomes.16,17

Population-based studies on NP-IPV with large sample sizes are rare, data about men’s experiences of NP-IPV are limited,18-20 and consensus is lacking on the definition and its measurement.12,14,21-23 Numerous scales have been proposed and used in NP-IPV research.24-27 Furthermore, there is debate about whether NP-IPV should be considered a single construct or if subtypes should be examined separately.9,12,14,21,22

One of the challenges with measuring experiences of NP-IPV and delineating any independent effects is its strong association with physical and sexual IPV.28-31 Research on demographic correlates of NP-IPV, such as gender and socioeconomic status, has yielded inconsistent findings.9,12,22,29,30,32-46 In general, evidence indicates that the prevalence of NP-IPV decreases with age,9,22,28,35,45,47 but there are exceptions.14,37,44 There is some evidence that financial stress/strain31,34 and being an immigrant28 increase the risk of NP-IPV.

Risk factors for physical and sexual IPV that have been studied extensively, such as alcohol consumption, having a disability, and childhood maltreatment, have rarely been examined in relation to NP-IPV, even though such factors may increase risks.28,29,31,34,41 Community and societal factors48,49 may also act as risk factors for IPV. There is a growing awareness that the neighborhood environment in which an individual resides is related to a number of health behaviors and outcomes, including IPV.48,49

Research on protective factors for NP-IPV is particularly lacking, although necessary to inform prevention strategies. There is some evidence that social support (from friends, families, and neighbors) and feelings of social cohesion may lower the risk of NP-IPV.9,34,39,41,45 Individuals experiencing IPV often feel reluctant to disclose abusive experiences to police or health care providers due to embarrassment or fears of disbelief;43,50,51 trust in these groups may act as a protective factor.

Inconsistent results in identifying determinants of NP-IPV likely arise from the variations in definitions and measurement.10,14,52-54 Additionally, many previous studies did not account for the co-occurrence of NP-IPV with IPV that is physical and/or sexual. There is growing awareness of the need to disentangle the impacts of NP-IPV from physical/sexual IPV. It is rare for physical/sexual IPV to occur without NP-IPV, but most people exposed to NP-IPV experience it in the absence of physical/sexual IPV.10,29,42,53 This implies the existence of a subpopulation whose risk and protective factors and service needs are largely unknown. This study examined the prevalence and correlates of the experience of NP-IPV among women and men aged 15 years or older in Canada. In this article, the phrase “experiencing” IPV means “being a victim” of IPV. We did not examine IPV perpetration.

Based on a large representative survey of the Canadian population conducted in 2019/2020, the research objectives were to:

Examine the prevalence of NP-IPV and co-occurring physical/sexual IPV

Assess the risk and protective factors associated with NP-IPV

Investigate if associations between these risk and protective factors and NP-IPV persisted when controlling for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV

We expand on previous research by examining NP-IPV in relation to established risk and protective factors for experiencing IPV in general1,3 as well as to items included in the survey that are understudied.

Methods

Data source

We conducted a population-based cross-sectional study with nationally representative data from Statistics Canada’s 2019 General Social Survey (GSS)–Canadians’ Safety.55 The GSS target population was household residents aged 15 or older living in the 10 provinces and 3 territories in Canada. Residents of institutions were not included. Two samples were selected (one for the provinces; one for the territories) and combined for our analysis. The sampling frame for the provinces was based on census data and landline and cellphone numbers. The sample for the territories was selected from an area frame of dwellings. For each occupied dwelling, 1 household member aged 15 or older was randomly selected to complete the GSS questionnaire. GSS data were collected using a combination of self-completed online questionnaires and telephone interviews. Proxy responses were not permitted. Data were collected from April 2019 through March 2020. The response rate was 36.4% for the provinces and 57.0% for the territories. The total responding sample was 22 412 individuals.

The GSS asked gender of the respondent with response categories of male, female, or a free text field third response option. We refer to the gender response categories as “men” and “women” in this article. Due to inadequate sample size, those who indicated they were other than male or female were excluded from the study; a binary definition of gender was used in the analyses.

Measures

Dependent variable—NP-IPV

The primary dependent variable was NP-IPV. The 2019 GSS included 9 NP-IPV items (Table 1). Five of these items1,5-8 were developed for Statistics Canada’s 1993 Violence Against Women Survey (VAWS).26 Items 2 and 4 were first introduced in the 1999 GSS and 3 and 9 in the 2014 GSS. These additions were made based on recommendations from stakeholders (academics, researchers, clinicians, federal and provincial partners) that additional questions on emotional abuse and coercive control were required.

Items and definitions.

Continued

For each item, respondents were asked about experiences during the past 5 years. Three separate sets of questions were asked: for current spouses/partners, for former spouses/partners, and for dating relationships. IPV data from all 3 types of relationships were combined for this study. NP-IPV was defined as acknowledging occurrence of at least 1 of the 9 items.

The co-occurrence between NP-IPV and physical/sexual IPV was examined. The 9 physical IPV items (Table 1) are based on the Conflict Tactics Scales56 with rewording introduced in the 1993 VAWS to respond to criticisms of the scale.26 The first sexual IPV item is from the 1993 VAWS,26 and the second item was first introduced in 2014 in response to comments made during the GSS consultation process. Like NP-IPV, respondents were asked about experiences during the past 5 years for the 3 types of relationships, and physical/sexual IPV prevalence was defined as indicating the occurrence of at least 1 item in the 2 scales.

Analysis

All analyses were stratified by gender. Frequency estimates and cross-tabulations were used to describe the prevalence of NP-IPV and the extent to which it co-occurred with physical/sexual IPV vs the extent to which NP-IPV occurred in isolation.

Multiple logistic regression was used to examine NP-IPV in relation to risk and protective factors and sociodemographic variables. In the final models, physical/sexual IPV was added as a control variable to identify risk and protective factors that were uniquely associated with NP-IPV, independent of other forms of IPV. The percentage of records with a missing value for 1 or more dependent/independent variables was 16.0%. Since analyses based on records with only complete information can lead to biased results,57 we used multiple imputation in our regression models to address missing data. All variables included in the logistic regression models were included in the imputation procedure. The MI procedure in SAS was used to impute 5 data sets specifying the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method.

All analyses (descriptive and multivariable) were based on weighted data. Weights created by Statistics Canada ensured that the data were representative of the Canadian population in 2019. To account for the survey design effect of the GSS, standard errors, coefficients of variation, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the bootstrap technique.58 All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Among women, in the regression model (not controlling for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV), those who reported childhood sexual abuse (odds ratio [OR] = 1.4; 95% CI, 1.1-1.9), childhood emotional abuse (OR = 1.5; 95% CI, 1.2-2.1), or childhood exposure to emotional IPV (OR = 1.3; 95% CI, 1.0-1.8) were more likely to report NP-IPV. In supplementary analyses considering the number of types of childhood maltreatment experienced, a gradient emerged with the likelihood of reporting NP-IPV increasing by the number of types of childhood maltreatment experienced. NP-IPV was reported by 10.9% of women reporting no childhood maltreatment, 20.0% of those reporting 1 type, 25.6% of those reporting 2 types, and 35.1% of those reporting 3 or more types. This gradient persisted in the full model. Women were at increased risk of NP-IPV if they engaged in heavy episodic drinking (OR = 1.4; 95% CI, 1.1-1.8) or had a spouse/partner who did so (OR = 1.8; 95% CI, 1.3-2.6). NP-IPV was more common among women with a disability, those who perceived financial strain, and those having no confidence in the police. Among women, number of friends was a protective factor; compared with those who reported having 10 or more friends, women with no friends were at increased risk of NP-IPV.

When physical/sexual IPV was added to the model for women, it was strongly associated with NP-IPV (OR = 41.1; 95% CI, 23.4-72.2). Compared with the model not controlling for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV, in this model, the strength of associations between some of the significant risk factors was reduced. However, in all cases, the ORs remained elevated (greater than 1).

Among men, in the regression model (not controlling for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV), those who reported childhood sexual abuse (OR = 1.7; 95% CI, 1.1-2.6) or childhood emotional abuse (OR = 2.0; 95% CI, 1.5-2.7) were more likely to report NP-IPV. The gradient that emerged between the number of types of childhood maltreatment experienced and experiencing NP-IPV was not observed.

Other significant risk factors for men included living in a neighborhood with social disorder, financial strain, and having a disability.

The protective factors that were significant for men were the number of close relatives/friends and confidence in the police. Compared with men reporting 10 or more close relatives/friends, those reporting only 1 or 2 had an increase in the odds of NP-IPV (OR = 1.8; 95% CI, 1.3-2.6). Also, men with no confidence in the police had 2.1 times the odds of NP-IPV (95% CI, 1.0-4.7) compared with those who had a great deal of confidence in the police. When we added physical/sexual IPV to the model, we found that it was highly associated with NP-IPV among men (OR = 26.0; 95% CI, 13.4-50.5). Nonetheless, the associations with the other risk and protective factors generally persisted.

In the final regression models (controlling for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV), the sociodemographic factors significantly associated with increased risk of NP-IPV were age and marital/dating status for both genders and being heterosexual for men.

Discussion

Based on data from the 2019 GSS–Canadians’ Safety, women and men aged 15 or older who had an intimate partner in the past 5 years were equally likely to report experiencing NP-IPV in the past 5 years. NP-IPV often occurred in the absence of physical/sexual IPV, but the reverse was not true. When considering all those who had an intimate partner in the past 5 years, 11.0% of women and 12.2% of men experienced NP-IPV without co-occurring physical/sexual IPV compared with 0.7% of men and women reporting physical/sexual IPV but no NP-IPV.

In regression models (without controlling for physical/sexual IPV), NP-IPV was more common among men and women with a disability, those experiencing financial strain, and those with no confidence in the police. Among women, childhood sexual abuse, emotional abuse, and exposure to emotional IPV were independently associated with NP-IPV. A gradient was observed, with the likelihood of NP-IPV increasing with the number of types of childhood maltreatment reported. Heavy episodic drinking increased the risk of NP-IPV for women. This was also the case for women whose spouse/partner engaged in heavy episodic drinking. Among men, NP-IPV was more likely among those who had experienced childhood sexual and/or emotional abuse and those living in a neighborhood with high levels of social disorder. When controls were added for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV, these associations generally persisted.

However, to understand the determinants of NP-IPV, it may not be appropriate to control for co-occurring physical/sexual IPV. Longitudinal studies have found that NP-IPV is a predictor of physical/sexual IPV.10,16,17 Therefore, it may be more important to examine all determinants of NP-IPV (regardless of co-occurrence with other types of IPV). Such data are needed to develop policies and programs aimed at reducing the prevalence of NP-IPV and the risk of physical/sexual IPV.

Similar to our results, previous studies found that the prevalence of NP-IPV was similar among women and men.32,34,38,41,42 However, important differences distinguish women and men in their experience of NP-IPV. While there is no consensus on how to measure severity of NP-IPV, it has been found that when severity and frequency are considered, women are more likely to experience a higher number of different types of NP-IPV acts and a higher frequency of these acts.59 The GSS did not measure the severity or frequency of NP-IPV. Measures of severity of NP-IPV and the degree to which IPV is reciprocal are needed to fully understand gender differences and the power differential in relationships.

Markers of low socioeconomic status (SES), including unemployment, low income, and low education, are identified risk factors for physical/sexual IPV.3,60 Some evidence suggests that low SES also increases the risk of NP-IPV,22,30 but our study, like others,36,37,44,45 found no associations with SES variables. The stigma associated with physical/sexual IPV may deter its occurrence and/or acknowledging its occurrence in a survey among high-income groups, but the stigma may not be as strong for NP-IPV, which is more subtle and less observable.36 Nonetheless, our results indicated that perceptions of financial strain increased the likelihood of NP-IPV, as reported in other studies.31,34 Financial strain may cause stress in relationships and thereby increase the likelihood of violence. Furthermore, people experiencing financial difficulty may feel unable to leave an abusive relationship. Financial literacy programs have been shown to increase self-efficacy and have had positive outcomes for those experiencing IPV.15

Our finding that having a disability increased the risk of NP-IPV for both women and men is concerning since people with a disability may depend on their partner for financial support and for personal care, making it difficult to terminate an abusive relationship.

Alcohol use has often been investigated in relation to physical/sexual IPV but rarely in relation to NP-IPV. A meta-analysis found stronger effect sizes for associations with perpetrator alcohol use for male-to-female IPV than for female-to male IPV.61 In our study, women whose partner engaged in heavy episodic drinking had significantly higher odds of experiencing NP-IPV, as did women who themselves engaged in heavy episodic drinking. Alcohol use reduces self-control and increases the likelihood of perpetrating violence against an intimate partner.61 The high odds of NP-IPV among women who engaged in heavy episodic drinking may reflect the use of alcohol as a coping strategy in an abusive situation.60

Although some studies have reported that childhood maltreatment increases the risk of IPV in adulthood,28,41 associations with NP-IPV have seldom been examined. We considered forms of childhood maltreatment analyzed less often such as neglect, emotional abuse, and exposure to emotional IPV. We found that NP-IPV was more common among women and men reporting childhood emotional abuse and among men reporting childhood exposure to emotional IPV. This is consistent with social learning theory, in that childhood maltreatment may serve as a model for future intimate relationships.62,63 Children who experience emotional abuse or who have a caregiver who is emotionally abused may learn to regard this as a normal and acceptable way of dealing with conflict. For both genders, we found evidence of an association between childhood sexual abuse and NP-IPV. Longitudinal research has found that childhood sexual abuse increases the risk of negative outcomes in multiple domains of life in adulthood, including victimization and perpetration of abusive behaviors within intimate partner relationships.64 For women, as the number of types of childhood maltreatment increased, so did the likelihood of experiencing NP-IPV in adulthood. Life span studies have found that people who experience numerous forms of family violence exhibit the highest levels of negative health outcomes.65,66

Ideally, IPV should be considered within a public health framework that stresses the importance of moving beyond individual risk factors of IPV to consider community and societal factors.48,49 In our study, some evidence of the importance of upstream factors emerged—men living in neighborhoods with high levels of social disorder and men and women with no confidence in the police were more likely to report experiencing NP-IPV. In general, there is a reluctance to report IPV to the police.67 It has been found that men, in particular, are more reluctant to disclose abuse to formal agencies.43 Other research has also found that higher levels of IPV are associated with lower levels of trust in police, with stronger associations being observed for non-White and sexual minority individuals.68 Those who trust the police may be more likely to seek help and may do so earlier in a relationship, before violence becomes more frequent or severe.

Social disorganization theory (which links crime rates to neighborhood ecological characteristics) may also apply to IPV48,49; living in a disadvantaged neighborhood disrupts social cohesion, reducing the likelihood of community members maintaining social control and intervening in situations involving family violence. Strategies aimed at reducing neighborhood disadvantage and disorder, which are antecedents to violence, may play a promising role in preventing IPV.49

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its large, nationally representative sample of the Canadian population. The 2019 GSS included multiple behavioral-specific items to measure IPV and an array of items to measure IPV risk and protective factors, including some community factors. Notably, the survey collected data on types of childhood maltreatment that have rarely been examined in relation to IPV in adulthood. Nonetheless, this study had several limitations that warrant attention.

GSS data were self-reported. People who have experienced IPV may be reluctant to report such information in a survey.69-71 A review of the literature concluded that IPV is underreported by both genders in surveys.72 Furthermore, some of the risk factors considered in this study (such as child maltreatment, financial strain, and heavy episodic drinking) are sensitive topics that are also underreported in surveys.73,74 Therefore, the errors introduced due to underreporting of the outcome variable (IPV) and some of the risk factors are in the same direction and may result in biased estimates of associations toward null findings.74

Another challenge is that there is no consensus on how NP-IPV should be measured, which makes it difficult to compare studies in a meaningful way. There is a need to improve and standardize measures.23 The GSS did not measure the frequency or severity of NP-IPV or the degree74 to which NP-IPV is bidirectional or unidirectional. This may have masked gender differences.

In this study, we treated NP-IPV as a single construct. Other researchers have used principal component factor analysis to confirm the existence of distinct forms of NP-IPV (emotional abuse, controlling behaviors, and economic abuse).12,14,22,75 Although the GSS contained items to measure these forms of NP-IPV, our ad hoc principal component factor analysis based on GSS data found no evidence of distinct components. Our failure to find distinct components may reflect the limited number of items in the GSS and question wording. The studies that reported distinct forms of NP-IPV had more items to measure NP-IPV than did the GSS.

The community variables included in this study were measured by aggregating individuals’ perceptions. Optimally, these variables should be based on more objective measures of community attributes.48

The GSS asked gender of partner only for those currently married or living common-law and not for those with ex-partners or dating partners. Therefore, we report on associations with sexual orientation as opposed to gender of partner.

The GSS does not include individuals who are homeless or living in institutions, populations among whom experiences of family violence tend to be more prevalent. Together, these factors have led to an underreporting of IPV experiences.

Because of the cross-sectional nature of the GSS, it was not possible to determine the temporal order of events.

The relatively low survey response rate may have influenced associations between risk and protective factors and NP-IPV.

Conclusion

Based on a 2019 nationally representative survey of Canadians aged 15 years or older, we found that 16.9% of men and 17.3% of women had experienced NP-IPV in the previous 5 years. Some studies have concluded that NP-IPV is as strongly or more strongly associated with certain health outcomes than physical/sexual IPV.12,38,76-78 In addition, longitudinal studies have found that NP-IPV often progresses to physical/sexual IPV, further increasing the risk of and exacerbating negative health outcomes,16,17 underscoring the importance of addressing NP-IPV as an intervention target. Many people experiencing NP-IPV do not recognize such experiences as abusive or the harm associated with such experiences.42,43,79 Even if recognized, seeking support is rare because of a lack of awareness or minimization of what is happening and therefore a reluctance to use services.50,51,67,80-82 There is a need to improve methods of preventing NP-IPV, develop gender-specific support programs, and identify and encourage those who experience NP-IPV to seek support. IPV is complex and multifaceted. Improving measures of perpetration, victimization, and risk and protective factors is necessary to accurately capture and understand the epidemiology of NP-IPV.
