ABSTRACT
AbstractIn two‐parent households, the interparental relationship and coparenting alliance are central to the well‐being of family relationships and young children. The current study examines whether participation in a brief, online, couple‐focused relationship intervention has collateral benefits to coparenting indirectly through improvements in couple relationship quality and conflict frequency, respectively. A community sample of couples with young children in Canada (N = 140 couples; 280 participants; 91.4% heterosexual; 61.1% White; 49.3% women) participated in a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The intervention group was taught conflict reappraisal strategies, whereas the control group received an inactive control task. Both members of the couple reported on perceived relationship quality and conflict frequency (at baseline and post‐intervention), and coparenting (at baseline, 1‐month, and 3‐month follow‐up). Controlling for baseline levels, a longitudinal, parallel mediation analysis including relationship quality and conflict frequency as mediators indicated that the indirect effect of random assignment to the intervention to coparenting via relationship quality was significant, but not through conflict frequency. There are positive cascading effects of a couple‐focused intervention onto the relationship between parents, which in turn enhances their ability to work together to raise their child. Findings help to bridge intervention research across relationship, family, and developmental science.

BODY
INTRODUCTION

Despite having the same goal—to benefit children and families—interventions targeting parenting, couples’ relationship quality, and child development are typically siloed (Cowan & Cowan, 2019). In two‐parent households, the relationship between parents, or the interparental relationship, forms an executive subsystem that is essential to family functioning and the well‐being of children (Cummings & Davies, 2010; Minuchin, 1985; van Eldik et al., 2020). Harold & Sellers (2018) provide an integrated theoretical framework through which interparental conflict impacts youth psychopathology, involvement children's neurobiological/ psychophysiological arousal, cognitive/emotional processing, and parent‐child relations. Indeed, programs that focus on reducing negative conflict interactions between coparents are effective at enhancing couple communication, relationship satisfaction, as well as child psychological well‐being (Harold & Sellers, 2018). Another identified pathway through which the interparental relationship influences children's well‐being is through the coparenting subsystem. Indeed, how partners coordinate their child‐rearing efforts, engage in supportive or undermining parenting behaviour, and manage child‐rearing conflicts (i.e., their coparenting relationship; Feinberg, 2003; McHale, 2007) has important implications for child social, emotional, and behavioural development (Holland & McElwain, 2013; Teubert & Pinquart, 2010). The current study examines whether there are secondary benefits of a brief couple's intervention to the quality of the coparenting relationship, and whether benefits operate through couples’ relationship quality, conflict, or both. Identifying the myriad familial benefits of couple's interventions will help to bridge traditionally siloed areas of relationship, developmental, and family science.

The interparental relationship

Although the interparental relationship has traditionally been viewed through the lens of marital discord, there is increased interest in distinguishing relationship quality from conflict (Davies & Cummings, 1994; Grych & Fincham, 1990; Kerig, 1996). Whereas relationship quality is defined as an aggregate construct that reflects dyadic satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, and love (Fletcher et al., 2000), interparental conflict refers to discord or aggression between parents (Grych & Fincham, 1990). Given the interdependency between family members and subsystems, the health of the interparental relationship lays the foundation for healthy coparenting, parent‐child relationships, and child adjustment (Cook et al., 2009; Feinberg et al., 2007; Goldberg & Easterbrooks, 1984; Talbot & McHale, 2004; van Eldik et al., 2020). It is therefore critical to identify contributors to healthy interparental relationships, as well as to better understand the interdependence between the interparental relationship and the functioning of other family subsystems.

STATEMENT OF RELEVANCE

Forming a high‐quality coparenting alliance (e.g., cooperative and supportive) is among the key tasks during the early years of parenting, with direct impacts on children's social‐emotional development. Teaching couples healthy conflict dynamics, strategically delivered during the developmental period of having young children, has the potential for positive “spill over” onto coparenting. Findings have implications for clinicians working with children, families, and couples, and further support taking a systemic lens to working with these populations.

The developmental period of having young children poses a risk to the interparental relationship given heightened levels of relationship stress during the transition to parenthood and years following (Kluwer, 2010; Madigan et al., 2017). Pruett & Pruett (2009) describe the “parenting partnership” as reflecting the changing nature of partnerships that comes with this phase of life, wherein the partnership must integrate marital (or relationship), parental, and child needs. Compared with childless couples, parenting couples experience steeper declines in relationship satisfaction over time (Keizer & Schenk, 2012; Lawrence et al., 2008). This decline in relationship quality may have been exacerbated by the COVID‐19 pandemic, which posed many challenges to families (e.g., social, economic, and health‐related; Overall et al., 2021; Prime et al., 2020). These added stressors and increased demands may have depleted the resources required for partners to be responsive to one another, and reduced the time needed to foster a healthy connection (Pietromonaco & Overall, 2022). Given the delicate developmental period of early child‐rearing for relationship quality, and the elevated risk for couples with children during the pandemic (Overall et al., 2021, 2022; Schmid et al., 2021), interventions to support the interparental relationship of couples with young children are essential.

Coparenting alliance

A related but distinct family subsystem to the interparental relationship is the coparental relationship (Feinberg, 2003). Forming a high‐quality coparenting relationship is among the key tasks during the early years of parenting (Schoppe‐Sullivan & Fagan, 2020). Initially, coparenting was studied within divorced or separated parent contexts (Ahrons, 1981); however, it has since been recognized as playing a critical role within intact two‐parent families (Feinberg, 2002; Margolin et al., 2001; McHale et al., 2015; Pruett & Pruett, 2009). This is especially true since, over the past 2 decades, traditional gender roles have shifted, with mothers pursuing lifelong careers and fathers becoming more actively involved in childcare and home responsibilities (Chung & van der Lippe, 2020). With both partners now assuming more responsibilities in childcare, greater coordination and support between partners is essential.

Coparenting is an important dynamic to understand given its central role in family functioning and its proximity to child outcomes relative to interparental couple functioning (Feinberg, 2003). In a meta‐analysis of 59 studies, positive coparenting relationships, characterized by high cooperation and agreement and low conflict, were associated with fewer child internalizing and externalizing problems and better child social functioning, even after accounting for interparental and parent‐child relationship quality (Teubert & Pinquart, 2010). This body of research emphasizes the importance of the coparental relationship to the functioning of individual family members, most especially children.

The interface between the coparenting alliance and the interparental relationship

Family systems theory posits that members within a family subsystem are interconnected, as are their relationships (Minuchin, 1974, 1988). Thus, the quality of a couple's relationship and their conflict dynamics (i.e., interparental relationship quality), will have implications for how they work together in their parenting roles (i.e., coparenting alliance). One mechanism that operates within this broader framework, wherein family members are part of an interconnected system, is spillover; the functioning of one subsystem can “spill over” via emotions, cognitions, and behaviours that transfer from one relationship to another (Easterbrooks & Emde, 1988; Erel & Burman, 1995; Krishnakumar & Buehler, 2000). This model implies, for instance, that stressors may give rise to fewer positive and more negative interactions between partners (e.g., argumentative and hostile behaviours), resulting in feeling less supported by one another (Neppl et al., 2016) and ultimately increasing the probability of negative coparenting interactions (Camisasca et al., 2019; Cox & Paley, 2003; Morrill et al., 2010). Given these interdependencies, couples interventions are likely to not only benefit couples’ relationships, but also lead to cascading effects on their coparenting alliance.

We next describe the interrelations between the coparental alliance and two facets of the interparental relationship—relationship quality and conflict, respectively. There is robust evidence for the association between relationship quality and coparenting dimensions (Chong & Mickelson, 2016; Don et al., 2013; Schoppe‐Sullivan et al., 2016; Van Egeren & Hawkins, 2004). Indeed, a recent meta‐analysis demonstrated a moderate association between relationship satisfaction and the quality of the coparental relationship (Ronaghan et al., 2023). Specifically, positive coparenting dimensions (e.g., cohesion, cooperation, support) are associated with relationship satisfaction (Favez & Frascarolo, 2013; Talbot & McHale, 2004), whereas negative dimensions (e.g., low cooperation, high competition) are related to relationship dissatisfaction (Christopher et al., 2015). Direct tests of the spillover hypothesis show that interparental couples who are satisfied with their romantic relationship have positive affection for each other (e.g., connectedness, warmth, love), which allows them to support each other as parents and work cooperatively in child‐rearing (McHale et al., 2004; Morrill et al., 2010). Likewise, couple negativity (e.g., frustration, anger, hurt) disrupts partners’ ability to establish a supportive coparental relationship (Kitzmann, 2000; McHale, 1995). Importantly, there is evidence that the coparenting alliance serves as the mechanism linking relationship quality and positive parenting practices (Bonds & Gondoli, 2007). These findings highlight the interconnectedness between couple relationship quality and coparenting in two‐parent families, with implications for the entire family and child well‐being.

With respect to couples’ conflict, Krishnakumar and Buehler (2000) theorized the process through which interparental conflict undermines coparenting by negatively influencing the family climate. For instance, it may be difficult for parents who are fighting to effectively coparent, interfering with the development of a healthy parenting environment (Margolin et al., 2001) that supports healthy child functioning (Shimkowski & Shroat, 2012; Teubert & Pinquart, 2010). Moreover, high levels of conflict from parents in distressed relationships spills over from the interparental to the coparental relationship. Problematic conflict between couples can manifest as low coparenting alliance (Kopystynska et al., 2020), undermining one another's parenting (Brown et al., 2010; Martin et al., 2017), coparenting disagreements (McHale, 1995; Sturge‐Apple et al., 2006), and perceiving a lack of coparental support (Cabrera et al., 2009; Schoppe‐Sullivan et al., 2004). Riina and McHale (2015) demonstrate that an increase in love and a decrease in relationship conflict predicted increased coparenting satisfaction. Thus, healthy conflict dynamics within the interparental relationship may have potential benefits to supportive coparenting.

Coparenting alliance and couple‐focused interventions

Couple‐focused interventions are typically relationship programs aimed at improving quality, satisfaction, conflict, and overall functioning (e.g., Baucom et al., 2006; Cummings & Davies, 2010; Doss et al., 2016; Halford et al., 2001; Loew et al., 2012; Neumann et al., 2018). Couple‐focused interventions have been shown to improve relationship quality, parenting practices, and child adjustment (Bodenmann et al., 2008; Cowan et al., 2009; Gattis et al., 2008; Zemp et al., 2016). Despite parenting couples presenting to couple‐focused interventions with difficulties in coparenting (Feinberg, 2003; Van Egeren & Hawkins, 2004), coparenting is not typically central to the programming of these interventions. Nonetheless, improving interparental relationship quality in the context of couple‐focused interventions also improves the quality of the coparental relationship (Cowan et al., 2005; Gattis et al., 2008; Ledermann et al., 2007). For instance, in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of an online program for relationship distress with parenting couples, the intervention group showed greater decreases in coparenting conflict and increases in relationship satisfaction compared to the control group (Doss et al., 2020). Furthermore, following a relationship education workshop for couples, improvements in couple relationship quality were significantly associated with improvements in coparental relationship quality (Adler‐Baeder et al., 2013), and post‐intervention levels of relationship satisfaction mediated long‐term improvements in coparental relationship quality, relative to a control group (Lavner et al., 2019). Finally, in a meta‐analysis of 32 studies (Hawkin et al., 2022), couple relationship education programs showed small but significant effects on coparenting behaviour for the treatment group compared to the control. Interestingly, programs that included coparenting education did not have significantly larger effect sizes than the programs that did not. Generally, the effects of these couple‐focused programs on coparenting are broadly comparable to the effects achieved by interventions that directly target coparenting itself (e.g., Feinberg et al., 2016). There are some notable exceptions, however, wherein effects of couple‐focused interventions did not have cascading benefits on coparenting (Hahlweg & Klann, 1997; Klann et al., 2011; Le et al., 2021). Overall, however, there is fairly consistent support for spillover effects of the couple‐focused relationship interventions to coparenting.

Love together, parent together: A brief, online, self‐directed couple intervention

One drawback of existing relationship education programs is their intensity—they are typically implemented in‐person, within the community, led by a trained clinician, and associated with large time commitments and high costs (e.g., Mercer, 2015; Nicholson et al., 2010; Pearl et al., 2012; Sanders et al., 2014). As a result, stressors associated with material hardship, such as holding multiple jobs and contending with a chaotic home environment, or the demands of having young children alone, can stand in the way of participation (Caldwell et al., 2005). Using brief, online, self‐directed interventions can help alleviate these accessibility and retention challenges, while also minimizing costs (Kanter & Schramm, 2018). There is evidence for the effectiveness of brief couple's interventions (see Kanter & Schramm, 2018); however, it is not known whether low‐intensity, self‐driven approaches to enhancing the interparental relationship have positive spillover effects onto the functioning of the broader family system—namely, the coparenting alliance.

Love Together, Parent Together (L2P2) is a brief, couple‐focused intervention program targeting parenting couples with young children (Prime et al., 2022, 2023, 2024). Adapted from the Marriage Hack intervention (Finkel et al., 2013), L2P2 targets problematic conflict dynamics as a method to preserve relationship quality (Finkel et al., 2013). The intervention is low‐intensity, inspired by research demonstrating that brief, theory‐driven psychological interventions are effective (Yeager & Walton, 2011). Specifically, partners within a couple independently participate in three 9‐min writing sessions, where they are taught, and given practice, to reappraise their disagreements with their partner from a neutral, third‐person perspective. In their original investigation, Finkel et al., 2013 demonstrated that participation in the Marriage Hack buffered against normative declines in marital quality over time, an effect that was mediated by reductions in conflict‐related distress. Subsequently, Prime et al. (2022; 2023) adapted the Marriage Hack for use in the pandemic context for couples with young children, and have since shown benefits to the relationship quality of participating couples (Prime et al., 2024). Thus, this couple‐centered intervention has shown promising effects in improving romantic couples’ relationship quality and conflict‐related distress (Finkel et al., 2013; Prime et al., 2024). However, the cascading benefits of this program on coparenting, a relational subsystem that was not the intended target of the intervention and which is closer to the parent‐child relationship and child outcomes, have yet to be explored. Thus, the current study examined possible spillover effects of the a brief couple intervention onto coparenting quality through the interparental relationship, based on theoretical and empirical support for the interdependencies between these domains of couple functioning.

Current study

The central goal of this study was to determine whether the L2P2 intervention produces collateral benefits to coparenting for parenting couples at risk for relationship difficulties, with risk operationalized as both the developmental stage of having young children, and the widespread stress imbued by the COVID pandemic. We operationalize the interparental relationship in two ways, as two possible mediators: conflict frequency and global relationship quality, given the ongoing efforts to distinguish multiple facets of the interparental relationship (Davies & Cummings, 1994; Grych & Fincham, 1990; Kerig, 1996). This approach permits a more precise assessment of the mechanisms (conflict vs. relationship quality vs. both) accounting for putative intervention benefits on coparenting.

Using a longitudinal RCT design, we collected parent questionnaire ratings of relationship quality, conflict frequency, and coparenting. Consistent with longitudinal mediation, we expected to find a significant indirect effect of the L2P2 intervention (Time 1; T1) on coparenting (1‐month and 3‐month follow‐up; T3 and T4) via both conflict frequency and relationship quality (1‐week post‐intervention; T2). The coparental relationship was examined at both 1‐month and 3‐month follow‐up to determine whether indirect effects through the interparental relationship were immediate, maintained, and/or delayed. We test these indirect effects through conflict frequency and relationship quality in separate models, as well as simultaneously in a parallel (competing) mediation model. As mediation pertains to questions of the mechanisms of change, we controlled for initial levels of conflict frequency, relationship quality, and coparenting in all analyses. As such, an indirect effect would suggest that random assignment to the L2P2 intervention contributes to change in conflict frequency and/or relationship quality, which in turn, are associated with subsequent changes in coparenting.

METHOD

Study design

This study was a secondary analysis from an online, parallel‐group trial of the L2P2 intervention conducted in Canada. The methods of the current study are described following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for reporting parallel group randomized trials (Moher et al., 2001) and the CONSORT extension to randomized pilot and feasibility trials (Eldridge et al., 2016). The larger study was pre‐registered on clinicaltrials.gov (#NCT05261022), and this specific manuscript was pre‐registered on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/6evm2). Study materials can be found on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/drzhx/).

Sample

Participants were recruited through local community organizations and agencies serving children, parents, and families from April to December 2022. Recruitment flyers invited couples for a chance to participate in a brief writing program to learn strategies to manage disagreements. Couples were eligible if they met the following criteria: they were 18 years or older, in a romantic relationship (with no history of separation or divorce), resided in the same household, and had at least one child under 6 years of age living in the home at time of enrollment. Both partners of a couple had to enroll and complete baseline to be eligible.

The sample consisted of both members of 140 couples (280 individual participants; 91.4% heterosexual) living in Ontario, Canada. Table 1 shows baseline and clinical characteristics. Detailed demographics and descriptive statistics for the overall sample and by group are outlined in the primary article (Prime et al., 2024). Most couples reported being married (n = 120, 85.7%), with the remainder in domestic partnerships (n = 20, 14.3%). On average, couples had been together for 10.76 years (SD = 4.33), and most had one (n = 64, 45.7%) or two children living in the home (n = 60, 42.9%), with fewer having three or more (n = 16, 11.4%). Parents were asked to select and report on one child closest in age (but not over) 6‐years‐old in the home. Target children were on average 3 years old (target child M age = 3.33 years, SD = 3.45), and almost half were female (45.7%). Around one‐third of couples reported a total yearly household income of $175,000 CAD or more (34.3%) and between $125,000–$174,999 CAD (28.6%), respectively. A small percentage of couples (14.3%) reported making less than $75,000 CAD.

Baseline and clinical characteristics.

Couple‐level variable.

Parents identified primarily as woman (n = 138, 49.3%) or man (n = 136, 48.6%), with six (2.1%) identifying with another gender (i.e., trans man, gender fluid, self‐specified). Most parents had attained a bachelor's degree or higher (n = 206, 73.6%), with others completing a college diploma (n = 32, 11.4%), a professional degree (n = 20, 7.1%), some college/university (n = 12, 4.3%), or less than or equal to a high school education (n = 10, 3.6%). The racial/ethnic composition of the sample was: 61.1% White, 8.9% Chinese, 8.6% mixed origins, 8.2% South Asian, 7.5% Central/South American, in addition to 4.6% self‐reporting as one of several other race/ethnicities (e.g., Arab, Black, Filipino, Korean, West Asian, and Southeast Asian).

In terms of attrition, we had 134 couples (95.7% of the initial sample) at T2, 128 couples (91.4% of the initial sample) at T3, and 124 dyads (88.6% of the initial sample) at T4.

Procedures

Approval was obtained from York University ethics board (#022–076) prior to commencing data collection. All components of the study took place online via Qualtrics, including eligibility screening, registration, survey distribution and data collection, survey reminders, and the intervention. After providing written informed consent, participants were invited to complete baseline surveys (see measures below). Following the completion of the baseline assessment (T1; week 0), participants were randomly allocated (1:1) at the couple level to intervention (L2P2) or control group using Randomize.net. Participants were invited to take part in three self‐directed writing sessions (W1; writing session 1, W2; writing session 2, W3; writing session 3) over the course of 9 weeks, followed by a 1‐week post‐intervention assessment (T2; week 10). Follow‐up assessments were conducted at 1‐month (T3; week 13) and 3‐months (T4; week 22) after the final writing session. Participant recruitment, randomization, and progress through the study are illustrated in the CONSORT flowchart in Figure 1. Each partner was compensated for all parts of the study they completed (up to $80, for a total of $160 per couple). For all measures, partner ratings were averaged and used as final couple‐level scores.

Consort diagram.

Intervention

The L2P2 writing intervention involves three writing sessions completed over the course of 9 weeks. During each session, all participants: (1) provide a fact‐based summary of a recent conflict interaction with their partner in the previous 4 weeks and report on conflict‐related distress associated with this disagreement; and for the L2P2 intervention participants, only, (2) watch a short psychoeducational video introducing the conflict reappraisal task and considerations to make when completing the subsequent writing exercise; and (3) respond to three conflict reappraisal prompts.

To elaborate on Steps 2 and 3, above, the conflict reappraisal writing intervention involves watching a 1–3‐min instructional video explaining useful conflict reappraisal strategies (e.g., taking a third‐party perspective), followed by answering three self‐evaluative questions, and a 9‐min writing task. The writing task involves reappraising the disagreement they previously reported in Step 1 using the strategies provided in the video and three guided prompts focusing on: taking a third‐party perspective, barriers to taking this perspective, and a plan for how this perspective can be applied in the future. The instructional video used examples related to typical problems faced by couples with young children (e.g., couple relationship, coparenting, division of labour). Between sessions, participants in the intervention group receive reminder emails prompting the use of the reappraisal strategy, whereas those in the control group received scheduling reminders. For additional information about the intervention, see the project page on Open Science Framework and Prime et al. (2024).

MEASURES

Coparenting (dependent variable)

At baseline, 1‐month, and 3‐month follow‐up, parents reported on the quality of their coparental relationship using the 14‐item Brief Coparenting Relationship Scale (CRS‐B; Feinberg et al., 2012). The CRS‐B measures coparenting across seven subscales: coparenting agreement, coparenting closeness, exposure to conflict, coparenting support, coparenting undermining, endorse partner parenting, and division of labour. Participants rated 12 items (five reverse coded) on a 7‐point Likert scale (0 = not true of us to 6 = very true of us) and 2 items (reverse coded; exposure to conflict subscale) on a 7‐point Likert scale (0 = never to 6 = very often), which included items such as “my partner and I have the same goals for our child” and “I believe my partner is a good parent.” Items were averaged to create a single composite at each time point for each parent (iα T1 = .87, α T3 = .87, α T4 = .88), with higher scores reflecting a more positive coparental relationship with their partner. Parents scores were positively correlated with one another at each time point (r T1 = .64, r T3 = .66, r T4 = .68, all ps = < .001), and subsequently combined.

Couple relationship quality (mediator variable)

At baseline and post‐intervention, parents reported on the quality of their relationship using a total of 18 items of the Perceived Relationship Quality Component Scale (PRQS; Fletcher et al., 2000), distributed across six subscales: satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, and love. Participants responded to statements such as, “how satisfied are you with your relationship?” and “how much do you trust your partner?” Responses were scored on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 7 = extremely) and averaged to create a single composite at each time point, with higher scores reflecting a more positive relationship with their partner (α T1 = .95, α T2 = .96). Parents scores were positively correlated with one another at each time point (r T1 = .65, r T2 = .55, all ps = < .001), and subsequently combined.

Couple conflict (mediator variable)

At baseline and post‐intervention, parents rated frequency of conflict with their partner using a single item adapted from the five‐item Conflict subscale of the Braiker–Kelley Partnership Questionnaire (Braiker & Kelley, 1979). Participants responded to the question, “Think about your experiences with your partner over the last 4 weeks. How often did you and your partner argue with each other?” Responses were scored on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = not very often to 7 = very often), creating a single score for each time point, with higher scores reflecting more frequent conflict with their partner. Parents scores were positively correlated with one another at each time point (r T1 = .62, r T2 = .66, all ps = < .001), and subsequently combined.

In addition, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to address limitations with our assessment of couple conflict (i.e., conflict frequency). Conflict‐related distress would be an appropriate mediator to examine as a primary analysis, based on theories of escalating emotionality in conflict situations (Gottman, 1998) and Finkel et al's (2013) finding that participation in the Marriage Hack program buffered against normative declines in marital quality over time, as mediated by reductions in conflict‐related distress. However, due to a predetermined assessment schedule, we only have conflict‐related distress measured during intervention sessions, rather than at baseline and post‐intervention. For sensitivity analyses, we examined the mediation models looking at couple conflict using an assessment of conflict‐related distress, instead of conflict frequency, looking at change from writing session one to writing session three. More information on this measurement can be found in Supplementary Material.

Random allocation assignment (independent variable)

Couples were randomly assigned at the level of the couple to the L2P2 intervention (coded as 1) or control (coded as 0).

Analysis plan

In line with an intent‐to‐treat approach, all randomized couples were included in analyses. Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0, and structural equation modelling (SEM) was performed using the Lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) in R statistical software (version 4.2.3; R Core Team, 2021).

Bivariate correlations were examined first to examine interrelations between random assignment, relationship quality, conflict frequency, and coparenting, over time. Then, path modelling using observed variables was used for a series of models to examine the hypothesized mediation effects of random assignment to the L2P2 intervention (predictor) on later coparenting (outcome) through couple relationship quality (mediator one) and conflict frequency (mediator two), respectively, controlling for baseline levels of the mediator and outcome. Following best practice guidelines to establish the most parsimonious model (Little, 2013), we planned to constrained paths that were (a) unnecessary for testing longitudinal mediation and (b) did not approach significance (p > .10); however, see results for an adjustment to this plan. A visual depiction of the hypothesized pathways for Model 1 can be found in Figure 2 as an example. Four fully saturated path models containing all possible direct effects were tested. Model 1 examined whether the L2P2 intervention predicted changes in coparenting at 1‐month follow‐up (T3) through relationship quality at post‐intervention, controlling for baseline levels of each. Model 2 examined whether random assignment to the L2P2 intervention predicted changes in coparenting at 1‐month follow‐up (T3) through conflict frequency at post‐intervention, controlling for baseline levels of each. Models 3 and 4 were similar to Models 1 and 2, but with coparenting at 3‐month follow‐up (T4). Sensitivity analyses for Models 2 and 4 (i.e., Model 5 and Model 6, respectively) were conducted with conflict‐related distress instead of conflict frequency. For Model 7 (1‐month follow‐up) and Model 8 (3‐month follow‐up), a parallel multiple mediation model tested the indirect effects of random assignment to the L2P2 intervention on later coparenting through relationship quality and conflict frequency at post‐intervention, included together in the same model as concurrent mediators.

Model fit is not reported given that all models are fully saturated. For direct effects, standardized path coefficients were calculated, and p‐values < .05 indicate statistical significance. The significance of the indirect effect was tested using bootstrapping procedures with bias‐corrected 95% confidence intervals (CI) based on 10,000 bootstrapped draws; unstandardized coefficients were calculated, and a CI not containing zero reflects a statistically significant indirect effect and was taken as evidence of mediation.

Missing data

Rates of missing data ranged from 0% (couple relationship quality, baseline ratings) to 11% (coparenting, 3‐month ratings). Of the 140 couples who initially participated, complete data were available for 134 couples at post‐intervention. A total of 128 couples provided complete data at 1‐month follow‐up, and 124 couples at 3‐month follow‐up. Little's missing completely at random (MCAR) test conducted on all study variables was not significant, χ 2 (7, N = 140) = 2.50, p = .93. This suggested that the likelihood of having missing data was not correlated with measures included in the study. Follow‐up analyses provided further support for this assumption: couples who remained in the study (n = 134) did not significantly differ from those who dropped out (n = 6) in terms of primary study variables (baseline assessments of couple relationship quality, conflict, coparenting: ps = .13–.98) and did not differ on baseline demographic variables. Missing data were therefore estimated under the MCAR assumption using full‐information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimated with robust standard errors (using the Lavaan package; Rosseel, 2012).

RESULTS

DISCUSSION

The current study is a secondary analysis of a RCT of the L2P2 program—an intervention promoting conflict reappraisal use in couples with young children. One feature of this program is its brief nature, wherein members of a couple independently engage in three, 9‐min writing sessions. A previous paper using the current dataset demonstrated benefits of the program to couples’ relationship quality (Prime et al., 2024), but secondary benefits to additional family outcomes have not yet been explored. Couple‐focused programs often provide collateral benefits to coparenting, with evidence that improving interparental relationship quality improves coparenting (Cowan et al., 2005; Gattis et al., 2008; Ledermann et al., 2007). These collateral effects have been shown to be comparable to the effects of interventions that directly target coparenting (e.g., Feinberg et al., 2016). The goal of the current study was to build on and extend this prior work to determine whether benefits to coparenting are present in a brief couple intervention—L2P2—as well as to further elucidate the associated mechanisms.

As reported in a primary paper (Prime et al., 2024), random assignment to the L2P2 intervention predicted change in relationship quality at post‐intervention, 10 weeks later, controlling for baseline levels. Further, higher relationship quality at post‐intervention predicted relative increases in coparenting in the follow‐up periods, controlling for baseline levels of coparenting. Previous research has found a robust association between coparenting and the quality of the interparental relationship in two‐parent families (Bonds & Gondoli, 2007; McHale, 1997; Morrill et al., 2010; Pedro et al., 2012; Schoppe‐Sullivan et al., 2004). When couples enter the coparental relationship, their existing relationship quality (e.g., support and respect, managing disagreements) may carried over into their new roles as coparents (Feinberg, 2003). Indeed, couples with higher relationship quality have more positive affection for each other, leading to supporting each other as parents and working together in child rearing (McHale et al., 2004; Morrill et al., 2010). The opposite pattern is observed for couples with lower relationship quality (Kitzmann, 2000; McHale, 1995). By controlling for baseline levels of coparenting and using an RCT design, this study provides causal evidence for the association between these constructs—that is, the L2P2 intervention led to changes in relationship quality which, in turn, led to changes in coparenting. Findings are theoretically consistent with the conceptualization that the couple subsystem (i.e., interparental relationship) and the coparental subsystem are distinct yet intimately connected (Feinberg, 2003). Further, this constitutes evidence in favour of the assertion that a brief couple‐focused intervention can serve as a mechanism of change for coparenting behaviour over time. Establishing effective strategies for enhancing the coparenting alliance is of considerable interest given that it is long‐lasting and influences children's adjustment and parents’ well‐being (Don et al., 2013; Le et al., 2016; Sobolewski & King, 2005).

In contrast, we did not find support for an indirect effect of the L2P2 intervention on coparenting through couple conflict frequency or conflict‐related distress, mainly since there was no benefit of participating in the L2P2 intervention on couple conflict. On the one hand, conflict frequency has been shown to be an important predictor for relational outcomes, with higher conflict frequency linked to lower relationship satisfaction (Bradbury et al., 2000) and increased risked of divorce by 1.5 times (Orbuch et al., 2002). However, interparental conflict is a multidimensional construct including not only conflict frequency, but also severity, content of disagreements, intensity, resolution (e.g., satisfactorily resolving conflicts), perceptions of effectiveness of partners’ problem‐solving abilities, and conflict strategies used in attempts to resolve conflicts (Zanella Delatorre et al., 2017; Kerig, 1996). For example, in a study by Fallahchai et al. (2017), participation in a couple relationship education program reduced the levels of global conflict, a single measure that included dimensions related to conflict, intensity, and resolution. Further, different conflict expressions have been shown to have distinct implications for the stability of the health of the family system (Christensen & Heavey, 1990; Gottman & Levenson, 1992). Indeed, distressed parenting couples experiencing high levels of destructive conflict often undermine each other in their parental responsibilities and disagree on child raising practices (McHale, 1995; Sturge‐Apple et al., 2006). However, conflictual discussions that are calm and met with social support from the other partner can serve as relationship‐enhancing (Cobb et al., 2001; Cummings et al., 2003; Pasch & Bradbury, 1998). Thus, the consequences of couple conflict depends on how it is managed, the relationship in which it arises, and the frequency in relation to positive interactions (Laursen & Hafen, 2010). Taken together, null findings related to conflict frequency and conflict‐related distress may be due to a narrow assessment of couple's conflict, without consideration of the nature or quality of the conflict. Focusing on additional dimensions of interparental conflict may provide a better assessment of the potential pathways linking brief, couple‐focused interventions to coparenting via conflict.

Alternatively, it is possible that the narrow focus on conflict reappraisal in the L2P2 intervention, without consideration of other important characteristics of the conflict environment, did not directly impact couple's conflict dynamics. Although conflict is typically a main focus in couple relationship education programs, these programs often integrate psychoeducational and skills‐training in other relevant domains, such as communication, emotional self‐regulation, and preserving relationship quality (i.e., sexuality and support; Dion, 2005; Wadsworth & Markman, 2012). Thus, such programs benefit a myriad of conflict resolution strategies (Babcock et al., 2013; Bradford et al., 2017; Jr & Shirer, 2009; Markman et al., 1993; O'Halloran et al., 2013). In contrast, the L2P2 intervention is considered a “wise intervention” (Walton, 2014) that focuses on targeting conflict reappraisal, precisely, rather than global attributes and behaviours related to conflict. This brief approach may not have been enough to alter problematic patterns of conflict during this phase of the family life cycle.

Importantly, couples’ responses to intervention have been shown to vary as a function of baseline conflict levels, where those with initially higher levels of conflict, only show improvements (Epstein et al., 2015). In this way, couple's programming may enrich relationships without being remedial, per se; that is, couples may not have needed support for their conflict dynamics. Thus, null findings may be due to how the conflict environment was assessed, or because the intervention was not effective for altering conflict patterns, or because of the nature of the low‐risk sample.

Coparenting takes on special meaning for families with young children as they attempt to navigate the array of caregiving tasks during this developmental period, while also maintaining their relationship quality (Kolak & Volling, 2007). Further, once the coparental relationship is established, couples may still need to fulfill their responsibility as coparents even if their romantic relationship is later terminated (Le et al., 2016). Thus, maintaining a satisfying and high‐quality coparental relationship is important for both couple and child functioning. Fortunately, the quality of the coparental relationship is modifiable, through many means, including through couple‐focused interventions. Although promoting coparenting is not always a central goal of couple‐focused interventions, the current study provides a better understanding of the myriad benefits of such interventions to subsystems beyond the interparental relationship.

Limitations and future directions

A primary limitation of this study was the reliance on caregiver ratings for all main constructs, which increases the potential for shared method biases and corresponding inflation of effect sizes. This is especially a concern given that participants were not masked to their randomly assigned allocation. It is important for future research to capitalize on multi‐method, multi‐informant designs to increase confidence in the robustness in these effects, including behavioural observations of couples (e.g., conflict discussion) and/or child reports of the coparenting environment. Similarly, the measure of conflict reflects caregivers’ frequency of conflict using one item developed for the study. Yet, interparental conflict is a multidimensional construct. As such, adopting a more nuanced approach to the assessment of this construct (e.g., Conflicts and Problem‐solving Scale [CPS]; Kerig, 1996), with particular emphasis on parsing apart the aspects of interparental conflict constitutes a critical next step.

Finally, results are not generalizable across sociodemographic or contextual gradients. For instance, the requirement of reading/writing proficiency in English can exclude newcomer and some immigrant families. Moreover, the study results are based on mostly White, middle/upper class, two‐parent, married families, similar to much of the current knowledge on the proposed associations discussed above (van Eldik et al., 2020), with a few exceptions (Fagan et al., 2016; Kopystynska et al., 2020). Ethnic, racial, and cultural characteristics, as well as contextual stressors and systemic barriers (e.g., economic hardship, unemployment, racism) may impact the engagement, relevance, and uptake of specific intervention content (Conger et al., 2010). Notably, programs designed to improve parenting and coparenting relations, including couple conflict, have been shown effective for a range of family outcomes in diverse, low‐income families (Cowan et al., 2022; Pruett et al., 2019). However, these programs are typically lengthier and integrate contextual factors into program design. For instance, in the TRUE Dads intervention, in addition to parenting and couple/coparenting curriculum, fathers from low‐income families were provided an employment program to support their work readiness skills, and other employment supports (Pruett et al., 2019). Findings demonstrated that this contextually embedded approach strengthened coparental relations, which had benefits for parent‐child relations and child functioning. In the future, we hope to assess the relevance of L2P2 to a broader sociodemographic of families, including making the appropriate contextual and cultural adaptations to meet the unique strengths and needs of diverse families.

Clinical implications

Findings have implications for clinicians working with children, families, and couples, and further support taking a systemic lens to working with these populations. It is important to consider the interconnectedness of family subsystems (i.e., individual members and relationships), and how improvements in couple functioning can reverberate throughout the family. Clinicians working with the interparental couple might consider the implications of their work for the coparenting alliance. Families presenting with interparental relationship and/or conflict problems may need support for their coparenting, and vice versa; being cognizant that problems in one can co‐occur within the other. Also, clinicians working with children might consider the well‐being of the interparental relationship, knowing that how couples interact and manage disagreements have implications for how they work together to parent their children. It is important to note that this was not a clinical sample, with only a minority of couples presenting with clinical levels of relationship distress. Thus, findings may not be generalizable to a clinical sample given that they may present with entrenched problematic relations requiring a more comprehensive approach.

CONCLUSIONS

This study adds longitudinal, causal evidence for the proposition that improvements in relationship quality mediate the association between participation in a couple‐focused intervention (L2P2) and later coparenting behaviour. In contrast, there was no support for conflict frequency or conflict related‐distress as mediators of this association. Findings inform the design of brief, specific, and self‐directed programming for couples, with the potential for cascading effects across the family. Teaching couples healthy conflict dynamics, strategically delivered during the developmental period of having young children, has the potential for positive “spill over” into other family subsystems; ultimately, serving to enhance the overall well‐being of families and improve outcomes for children.
