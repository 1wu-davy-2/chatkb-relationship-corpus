ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexual assault prevention is a priority for the military and is likely to be most effective when tailored to specific needs and individual experiences. Technology advances make it possible to integrate individualized programming into group education settings common to military training, but this approach is not without potential challenges. Prior to implementing and evaluating a novel prevention program, it is critical to conduct a feasibility study to assess the extent to which the program can be successfully implemented, is acceptable to participants, and can be rigorously evaluated. This paper presents the results of a large feasibility study of the Sexual Communication and Consent (SCC) program in Air Force Basic Military Training. The objectives were to understand (1) the suitability and acceptability of SCC and study procedures, (2) the research team’s resources and ability to manage and implement the study and SCC, and (3) the appropriateness of the data collection procedures and outcome measures.MethodsThe 26-week feasibility study involved more than 9000 trainees. At pretest, the sample was 73% male and 90% straight. Half of trainees (51%) reported not being in a romantic relationship, and 46% reported being in an exclusive romantic relationship. SCC involved 6 h of training over two sessions delivered 2 weeks apart. Sessions combined tablet-based training that was tailored to individual sexual assault risk (for revictimization, first-time victimization, or perpetration based on a screening instrument) with instructor-led classroom activities and discussion.ResultsTrainee, instructor, and observer data indicated that the program was feasible, implemented with high fidelity, and acceptable to trainees. Technology and data collection procedures worked well, but time constraints led to program changes, missed content, and missing survey data. Staff time and skill requirements for SCC implementation exceeded those of standard training.ConclusionsOur results suggest that high-quality implementation of tailored sexual assault prevention training is feasible in a military environment, and they inform recommendations for program revisions, future evaluation, and sustainability.Keywords: Sexual assault, Military, Prevention, Feasibility, Acceptability

BODY
Key messages regarding feasibility

What uncertainties existed regarding the feasibility?

It was unclear the extent to which a hybrid classroom and technology-delivered sexual assault prevention curriculum could be implemented with high fidelity and quality during a highly structured training window with large groups of military trainees. There was also uncertainty surrounding acceptability of such a program, which covered sensitive material and required asking trainees sensitive questions to assign them to the most appropriate tailored training content.

What are the key feasibility findings?

The SCC program was feasible and acceptable to trainees. The implementation and data collection procedures worked well. The main feasibility challenge was fitting all the content and assessments into the allotted class times. Classroom and technology management required significant instructor time.

What are the implications of the feasibility findings for the design of the main study?

Findings suggest that program content should be reduced and assessments streamlined to better fit into the time allotted prior to effectiveness evaluation. Minor revisions to content are also warranted to improve acceptability. It will be important in future research to test implementation approaches that can rely on typical military training infrastructure (i.e., staffing and technology).

Background

Sexual assault continues to be a problem with significant negative impacts despite the military’s considerable efforts to address it [1]. The 2021 Workplace and Gender Relations Survey showed that 35,875 active-duty service members across all branches had experienced some form of unwanted sexual contact in the past 12 months [2]. Victims of sexual violence may experience significant health and psychological sequelae, such as injury, depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), alcohol misuse, and repeat victimization [3, 4]. Sexual violence is also detrimental to the service and military readiness. It is estimated that at least 16,000 manpower years are lost due to sexual assault and sexual harassment each year, and the odds of separation from the military are twice as high for victims of sexual violence relative to non-victims [5]. Although victimization rates vary by service, the far-reaching consequences demand attention to evidence-based prevention.

Patterns of sexual assault found within the military suggest the need to tailor prevention approaches by gender and type of risk (e.g., for perpetration, first-time victimization, and revictimization) [2, 6]. Tailoring programs in this way may offer unique benefits to sexual assault survivors, women, and other individuals who are at greatest risk for victimization, because these subgroups often do not receive content that meets their needs in universal (i.e., one-size-fits-all, general population-focused) prevention [7–9]. Advances in technology make it possible to deliver individually tailored prevention content even in group training settings [10], but the feasibility and acceptability of hybrid (instructor-led and technology-delivered) approaches for sexual assault prevention programming in military settings have not been tested. Such feasibility testing is necessary for the safety and well-being of participants [11]. For example, interventions designed to prevent sexual assault victimization must avoid sharing information concerning risk reduction strategies with sexual assault perpetrators, or those at high risk of perpetrating, so that training does not inadvertently increase perpetration [12]. It is critical to ensure that participant privacy can be maintained so that training does not inadvertently reveal the identities of sexual assault survivors and to ensure that survivors feel comfortable receiving revictimization prevention content in a group setting. In addition, given the potential logistical challenges and investment of resources required to implement technology-delivered training [10], it is important to assess feasibility and acceptability of hybrid training in military settings before scaling up to full implementation.

The Sexual Communication and Consent Program

In response to concerns about cultures that enable sexual harassment and assault, and a dearth of evidence-based primary prevention programs to address these issues in the military [7], the Headquarters Air Force Office on Integrated Resilience (A1Z) developed a hybrid classroom and tailored technology-delivered set of interventions called Sexual Communication and Consent (SCC) [13]. SCC simultaneously addresses risk and protective factors for sexual assault perpetration, first-time victimization, and revictimization among different participants, depending on their responses to an anonymous screening instrument [14]. After trainees are confidentially assigned to receive the most relevant tablet-based content, they then move through an instructor-led curriculum, alternating between group classroom activities and individualized tablet training.

We developed SCC using a six-step intervention mapping approach [15–17], to ensure program content was developmentally and culturally relevant to the Air Force training environment. Following an initial needs assessment [18], we identified effective interventions (i.e., those that demonstrated significant reductions in sexual assault in rigorous randomized controlled trials) designed for college and high school students [19–22] and worked with original program developers and military subject matter experts to map out key theoretical mechanisms of each program and to systematically adapt content and integrate tablet- and classroom-based delivery [23, 24]. The adapted content aligns with the Department of Defense Policy on Integrated Primary Prevention of Self-Directed Harm and Prohibited Abuse or Harm [25], DoD policy on sexual assault prevention and response (SAPR) training requirements [26], and DoD’s Prevention Plan of Action [27]. Overview of SCC programs

In part because junior enlisted service members (salary grades E1–E4) experience the highest sexual assault rates [28], A1Z determined that the ideal setting for SCC was at Basic Military Training (BMT), where trainees typically receive universal classroom instruction on sexual assault prevention in large groups (including mixed-sex classes). Given the innovative format and technical demands of SCC, a feasibility study is a critical step toward conducting rigorous evaluation of program effectiveness [11, 29].

Framework and objectives for assessing feasibility

We used Orsmond and Cohn’s [29] feasibility study framework to develop the SCC study design and research questions. According to this framework, feasibility studies focus on the process of developing and implementing an intervention, in contrast to pilot studies which focus more on outcomes. Feasibility studies are iterative and can be conducted with flexible methodology. They are a particularly useful first step when implementing a novel intervention or when implementing an existing intervention in a new setting or with a new population. Indeed, this framework has been used to support recent feasibility studies of technology-based and hybrid interventions with civilian samples [30–32].

We focused on three key objectives from the framework. For each objective, we examined selected dimensions of feasibility and implementation fidelity associated with program efficacy and sustainability [33]. The first objective was to understand the suitability and acceptability of the intervention and study procedures. This objective seeks to assess adherence to study procedures, intervention attendance, and engagement, burden, participants’ acceptance of and satisfaction with the intervention, and safety and adverse events (such as psychological distress or sexual assault disclosure). Given the demanding and tightly controlled BMT environment and implementation challenges commonly reported in group-based prevention research [33], we expected that key threats to adherence or dosage would include time limitations and scheduling conflicts or competing priorities. Based on our extensive formative research with the target population [18], we expected that the format and content of SCC would be generally acceptable to trainees; however, we also expected that instructors could encounter challenges if trainees experienced strong emotions discussing sensitive content in the large classroom setting [34].

The second objective was to understand the research team’s resources and ability to manage and implement the study and intervention. This objective seeks to assess space, administrative capacity, expertise, skills, and time, as well as technology-related management and training needs. We examined facilitator skill and assessed the time and expertise (including organizational-level capacity) required to teach SCC. We expected implementation staff might experience challenges using the technology given the large scale of implementation and military information technology restrictions [35].

The third objective was to understand the appropriateness of the data collection procedures and outcome measures for the intended population and purpose of the study. This objective seeks to assess participants’ ability to complete the measures (e.g., comprehension), appropriateness of the amount of data collection, the relative completeness and usability of the data, and the appropriateness of the measures for the specific population and intervention. We expected that screening for sexual assault risk would be a feasible way to drive participant assignment to the tailored interventions, rates of missing data would be low given the electronic data collection approach and integration of assessment within a required class, and the measures would be acceptable to trainees.1

Current study

In sum, as a first step to developing evidence-based hybrid sexual assault prevention programs for the military, the current study examined the feasibility of the innovative SCC program in Air Force BMT. The objectives were to understand (1) the suitability and acceptability of the intervention and study procedures, (2) the research team’s resources and ability to manage and implement the study and intervention, and (3) the appropriateness of the data collection procedures and outcome measures. Thresholds for determining feasibility within each objective (Table 2) were developed based on implementation and evaluation of the original evidence-based programs from which SCC was adapted [19–22] and on our prior work adapting and testing classroom-based violence prevention content that demonstrates potential for efficacy [36]. Findings were intended to be used to further develop the program and inform future evaluation.

Methods

Feasibility study design and intervention participants

We conducted a three-phase feasibility study over 26 weeks, from September 2019 to March 2020 (Fig. 1). During Phase I (10 weeks), 25% of incoming flights2 at BMT were assigned by the BMT schedule manager to receive the SCC program; the remaining 75% completed existing group training material on professional interactions and SAPR. Phase II (10 weeks) scaled up to 50% implementation of SCC, and Phase III (6 weeks) included SCC implementation with 100% of flights entering BMT. This design fostered iterative improvements in both program implementation and measurement. A total of 206 flights received at least 1 day of the SCC program, and 172 flights completed SCC in its entirety. The study was unexpectedly ended early because of the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, so 2 weeks of incoming flights that began SCC were not able to complete it. At pretest, the sample was 73% male and 90% straight.3 Half of trainees (51%) reported not being in a romantic relationship, and 46% reported being in an exclusive romantic relationship in which they date or have sex with only one other person.

SCC intervention and implementation

The SCC intervention included both universal and tailored content delivered in a large training classroom. Instructors delivered universal content to trainees as a group, and tailored content was delivered confidentially to individual trainees on tablet computers with headphones and privacy screens. Consistent with evidence-based guidance for sexual assault prevention [37], the prevention content was dynamic and interactive. Universal classroom content included activities, games, and interactive discussions, and the tailored tablet content included videos of realistic risk scenarios, interactive quizzes, and problem-solving exercises. Tailored content was delivered through five tablet-based programs, which were equal in length and had distinct content for men and women: revictimization prevention (RVP), primary victimization prevention (PVP), or healthy relationships and bystander intervention (HRBI, focused on male perpetration prevention, healthy relationship communication and skills, and prosocial bystander intervention). Trainees were assigned to one of the tailored programs using a brief screening instrument and an automated computer algorithm that assessed their self-identified sex and sexual assault risk profiles. Risk profiles were defined by past experiences of sexual assault victimization [38], perpetration, or both, using several well-established measures [39, 40], as well as measures of theorized risk factors for male victimization (i.e., sexual minority status and PTSD symptoms as a proxy for trauma) [41–43], as delineated in the center column of Table 1. The full screening instrument and algorithm are included in Goldstein, Scaglione, and colleagues [14].

SCC was delivered over the 6 h already set aside in BMT for training on professional interactions (2 h in week 2 of BMT, hereafter referred to as Day 1) and SAPR (4 h in week 4, i.e., Day 2). The training was divided into six classroom sessions and five tablet sessions that took between 12 and 39 min each. Day 1 included classroom session 1 and tablet sessions 1 and 2; Day 2 included classroom sessions 2 through 6 and tablet sessions 3 through 5. The allotted time included tablet distribution, screening and program assignment, program delivery, breaks, transitions between classroom and tablet content, and all assessments. This implementation plan was designed to minimize burden on BMT staff, scheduling, and trainees. SCC was implemented in the same classrooms where trainees normally reported for their training. Flights were paired for training so group size was approximately 100 trainees per classroom; some classrooms had two male flights, and some classrooms had one male flight and one female flight.

Two trained instructors led each class and were supported by a BMT permanent party SAPR staff member. We recruited and hired instructors through a professional staffing agency; we trained a total of 18 instructor staff (13 women and 5 men) over the course of the study. Twelve were military veterans with relevant experience teaching SA prevention or working with SA survivors. One was a military spouse and parent with relevant teaching experience. The remaining five were civilians with advanced studies in social work and experience working with at-risk youth and their families or seasoned professionals with experience supporting service members and veterans. Each instructor training lasted 2 days and occurred in a classroom on base. Most of the training time was spent having instructors practice delivering the classroom content, setting up and breaking down the classroom, and using the tablets (including troubleshooting any issues trainees might experience). Instructors were also trained to follow all BMT protocols for responding to trainees who opted out of group training, disclosed prior sexual assault experiences, or exhibited distress at any point during the training. After each training, we asked instructors to practice delivering the content in pairs to ensure they were familiar with the material and classroom procedures before instructing trainees. We also encouraged instructors to observe active training sessions that were ongoing throughout the study.

Wireless Internet access was limited at BMT, and trainees were not allowed to maintain personal electronic devices, so trainees accessed the SCC tablet-based assessments and tailored interventions via classroom sets of 120 iPads with an offline application installed. Using the classroom roster, instructors set out the needed number of iPads before class started and returned them to locked charging carts at the end of class. More details about the technology used for SCC, including technology-related tasks the instructors needed to perform, can be found in Eckhoff et al. [35].

Data sources

The study used a multi-method approach that included self-report trainee assessments, instructor logs, classroom observations, tablet metadata, and informal group feedback sessions; the following paragraphs describe each of these in more detail. All study procedures were subject to rigorous DoD programmatic, legal, and human subjects protections review, following an initial review provided by the research team’s Institutional Review Board.

Trainee assessments

Trainees self-administered all assessments in the classroom anonymously on the tablets. They completed the screening instrument, feedback questions about the screener, and a pretest survey at the beginning of Day 1, acceptability surveys at the end of each day of training, and a posttest survey at the end of Day 2. We leveraged the incremental study design to make improvements to the screening algorithm (and therefore the acceptability of tailored content and appropriateness of outcomes assessed) before most trainees had completed SCC. Specifically, we removed items about unwanted touching and attempted assault from the screening algorithm, based on the performance of those items and feedback that trainees who only endorsed those items (out of all the prior SA victimization items) felt they were not receiving the appropriate training content, presumably because they did not see themselves as SA survivors. Thus, in the current study, we examined data from the subsamples of trainees that participated after the screener was revised. Screener and pretest results are based on the samples of trainees that completed these assessments on Day 1 (n = 5951). Acceptability results are based on the samples of trainees that completed acceptability surveys at the end of each day of training (Day 1 n = 5812; Day 2 n = 4213). Instructor and observer logs

SCC instructors completed a hard-copy log form after each class to document adherence to the classroom curriculum and other aspects of feasibility and acceptability. For approximately one-third of classes, trained observers completed a log that mirrored the instructor log. A total of 173 (96 Day 1 and 77 Day 2) instructor logs and 75 (40 Day 1 and 35 Day 2) observation logs were completed, representing about 92% and 40% of classes, respectively.

Tablet metadata and group feedback sessions

We supplemented these direct classroom-based assessments with passive collection of tablet metadata (automatically stored usage data, including length of time spent on each activity) and three post-implementation group feedback sessions. Two feedback sessions were conducted with a total of nine SCC instructors. One feedback session was conducted with the three BMT SAPR staff who often supported or observed instruction.

Measures

Objective 1: Suitability and acceptability of interventions and procedures

We adapted instructor and observer log questions from those used to assess implementation of a previously adapted version of Safe Dates [36], which is the evidence-based foundation for the SCC classroom content [19]. Questions measured adherence (e.g., proportion of classroom activities that were completed without changes, start and end times for each session) and whether any trainees opted out of group instruction, became distressed, or disclosed sexual assault victimization or perpetration. We developed guides for informal feedback sessions ad hoc in response to trends observed in the initial phases of the study; semi-structured questions asked instructors for their perceptions of needed adaptations to classroom activities. Measures calculated from the tablet metadata included the proportion of trainees who reached the end of each tablet session (suggesting completion) and the amount of time trainees spent in each session [44].

Questions assessing acceptability of the intervention from the trainees’ perspective were asked on the tablets at the end of each training day [45]. These individual items, developed for this study, assessed likeability of and comfort with the tablet and classroom content, perceived relevance of and emotional reactions to the tablet content, perceived balance between the tablet and classroom content, and the extent to which trainees perceived any victim-blaming language in either the tablet or classroom content.

Objective 2: Resources and ability to manage and implement the study and SCC interventions

Instructor and observer logs included questions developed or adapted for this study about ratings of the instructor’s own and their co-instructor’s performance on a scale of 1, indicating very poor, to 6, indicating outstanding [36], a checklist of potential problems with tablet functioning, and a checklist of potential environmental disruptions (such as fire drills or problems with classroom temperature). Discussion guides for group feedback sessions included questions about staffing capacity needed to deliver and sustain SCC.

Objective 3: Appropriateness of data collection procedures and measures

The trainee screening instrument included single items about self-identified sex and sexual orientation, items adapted from the Sexual Experiences Survey (SES) about sexual assault perpetration (10 items) [39, 40] and victimization (24 items) [38], and the 17-item Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) Checklist—Civilian Version, on which a score of 44 or above indicated probable PTSD [46]. We developed questions assessing acceptability of the screener; these asked about trainees’ honesty, comfort answering questions, and perceptions about privacy of responses, length of the screener, and clarity of questions. Given the sensitive nature of the questions and the high-pressure context in which we collected data, we also used a 9-item modified version of the short form of the Marlowe-Crowne Social Desirability Scale [47] to more objectively assess participant honesty in responding to the survey.

For the pre- and posttest, we adapted measures from those used to evaluate the original programs from which SCC was adapted; pretest descriptive statistics and Cronbach’s alpha for scale measures are shown in Table 4. Trainees in all tablet-based programs were asked questions assessing knowledge and attitudes. Knowledge items assessed general knowledge of sexual assault (16 items, e.g., “Rapes are likely to occur in isolated places”) [20], knowledge of effective consent for sex (10 items, e.g., “Consent must be given for each step of a sexual encounter”) [22], and legal knowledge of sexual assault (4 items, e.g., “Sexual assault always involves the use of physical force”) [22]. Knowledge items were scored dichotomously (correct/incorrect) and summed to create index measures; as indices do not assume item homogeneity, we did not calculate Cronbach’s alpha for these constructs [48]. We assessed date rape attitudes using a 10-item scale adapted from the Rape Attitudes and Beliefs Scale (e.g., “If a person wants to increase their chances of having sex, they should get the other person drunk”) [22, 49]. Responses were on a 5-point scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree, and items were summed.

Male and female trainees in the PVP and RVP programs completed a 6-item measure of self-efficacy to resist unwanted advances (e.g., “How confident are you that you could successfully avoid a situation in which you could be sexually assaulted?”) [21, 52]. We adapted items across measures to be gender neutral, specific to the Air Force (e.g., we modified legal questions to reflect the Uniform Code of Military Justice), and consistent with intervention content (e.g., to include males as potential sexual assault victims). For all screening and pretest measures that consisted of multi-item scales, a scale score was only calculated if at least 50% of items were answered and mean replacement was used for missing items.

Data analysis

To assess the first two objectives, we conducted univariate analyses (frequencies and percentages) on instructor, observer, and trainee responses. To assess the third objective (appropriateness of measures), we examined individual item descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages for categorical variables; means, standard deviations [SDs], and normality of distribution for continuous variables) and measures of internal reliability (interitem correlations, Cronbach’s alphas) for hypothesized scales. Across all objectives, we compared descriptive statistics to pre-established thresholds.

We took detailed notes during feedback sessions with instructors and staff, which we cleaned and compiled into a single file to be analyzed by category. We then conducted content and thematic analysis of the notes related to implementation feasibility and program sustainability. Team members who were present during the sessions discussed what we learned to come to a consensus on the recommendations being made by instructors and staff.

Results

Trainee sample characteristics

A small proportion of trainees (1.4% of males and 0.6% of females) reported having engaged in some form of sexually aggressive behavior (ranging from unwanted touching to sexual assault) before arriving at BMT. Thirty-nine percent of female and 6% of male trainees reported at least one unwanted sexual victimization experience (also ranging from unwanted touching to sexual assault) before arriving at BMT. A total of 5% of trainees met cutoff scores for probable PTSD.

Suitability and acceptability of interventions and procedures

Instructor adherence to classroom content was strong. On average, instructors reported that classroom (whole group) activities were implemented without changes in 96% of Day 1 classes and 95% of Day 2 classes. Observer data were similar: their corresponding averages were 98% and 94%, respectively. The most common implementation challenge was not having enough time (reported in 20% of Day 1 and 21% of Day 2 classes). This was often because trainees arrived at the class late, trainees needed an extra restroom break before or during class, or group discussions ran long.

Individual trainee completion of tablet content was variable. Between 23 and 99% of trainees reached the end of each tablet session in the time allotted. Of the 25 program sessions (5 sessions × 5 programs), only 13 met the 75% completion threshold. However, in another eight sessions, 79% or more of the trainees who did not finish were on the last segment of the session when time elapsed, which suggests they received most of the content in that session. Completion rates were much higher for the first session than for the last session of each training day: taken together with timing data from instructors and observer reports, this suggested instructors systematically shortened time for tablet sessions to make up for delays earlier in the day.

In their logs and during feedback sessions, instructors noted the classroom activities that were the most challenging to deliver as intended and suggestions for revisions to enhance feasibility and acceptability. Time constraints at times led instructors to simplify activities (e.g., providing didactic information instead of playing a game, holding a group discussion instead of asking trainees to write down responses for instructors to review and selectively share with the class). Instructors perceived that trainees had difficulty maintaining focus during the longer tablet sessions, and they suggested shortening the tablet time, especially on Day 1, to make more time for interactive activities. For topic areas that were most sensitive (e.g., combating victim blaming), instructors reported there was not always enough time to address or correct misperceptions to control the messaging and take-home points for trainees.

Overall, trainee perceptions of likeability, comfort, and relevance met acceptable thresholds for both the classroom and the tablet-based content. Most trainees (63–71%) felt that there was an adequate balance between time on tablet content and time on classroom activities, but those who perceived an imbalance (in particular, females) typically desired more classroom and less tablet time (23–25% overall, 34–47% of females). The percentage of trainees who reported experiencing intense emotions during the tablet content was low (6–9%) but higher among participants in RVP (11–22%) than other program groups. Rates of perceived victim blaming were also low (4–9%) but appeared to vary across tablet program groups. For example, 11–15% of males in PVP and RVP perceived victim blaming in the classroom content compared to 7–9% of males in HRBI. An informal review of open-ended responses in the acceptability data also indicated the program content seemed less relevant to trainees who were married (or in a committed relationship) or who were older than the typical trainee.

Rates of trainee opt-outs and distress were similar to, or lower than, rates observed in standard SAPR training (as reported by permanent party staff instructors). Five trainees opted out of SCC, one trainee disclosed a prior sexual assault victimization experience to an instructor, and 16 and 10 trainees exhibited distress during Days 1 and 2 of training, respectively. All distressed trainees met with a victim advocate privately, outside of the classroom. Roughly 88% of distressed trainees returned to class and completed training without further distress.

Resources and ability to manage and implement the study and interventions

Instructors rated both their own performance (M = 5.0, SD = 0.8) and the performance of the other instructor (M = 5.1, SD = 0.7) as very strong. Observers also rated the instructors very highly on average (primary instructor M = 5.1, SD = 0.9; co-instructor M = 5.2, SD = 0.7), and trainees responded positively about the instructors’ presentation of information and leadership of group discussions (M = 3.2, SD = 0.9 on Day 1 and M = 3.5, SD = 0.7 on Day 2 on a scale of 0 indicating not well to 4 indicating very well). An estimated 40 h were needed for instructor staff to complete training, prepare for implementation, and implement the full 6-h program with one class of trainees across the 2 days of training (including setup, cleanup, and data processing). In the instructor logs and feedback sessions, staff recommended that two staff members be present in each classroom to deliver content, support trainee needs, and manage the materials and equipment. The two staff members could be two instructors, or one instructor could be paired with a victim advocate or someone capable of supporting technological needs in the classroom. SAPR staff felt that key instructor qualities included flexibility and adaptability, being open-minded and aware of generational differences in language, being patient and collected under pressure, having an ability to meet trainees “where they are” without shutting them down, and previous experience with SAPR or victim advocate work. It also was noted that long-term maintenance of the equipment and technology would require staff with a specific information technology skill set to support instructors.

Overall, technology-related issues were rare, and when they did arise, instruction was almost always able to proceed as planned. Only one class in the entire study was not able to participate in SCC because of a systematic technology failure. No tablets broke or required replacement. Tablet issues such as not turning on, freezing, or needing to be restarted, or trainees being in the incorrect place in the program, were noted by instructors for less than 14% of class sessions; these tended to affect only a few trainees in the class and did not disrupt implementation. The co-instructor managed technology challenges during group instruction, while the primary instructor continued instruction for the rest of the class. There were no difficulties with tablet accountability, distribution, recovery, storage, or data transfer. Other logistical challenges (e.g., running out of needed supplies), environmental factors (e.g., warm classroom temperatures), and classroom interruptions (e.g., fire drills) were rare, and if they occurred, they had minimal impact on implementation (i.e., classes were able to continue and complete SCC content).

Appropriateness of data collection procedures and measures

Screening feasibility and acceptability

The resulting program allocation after revisions to the screening algorithm aligned with what we expected based on other studies using this version of the SES [21, 38]: 6% of men and 37% of women received RVP; 5% of men and the remaining 63% of women received PVP, and the remaining 89% of men who did not experience prior victimization or exhibit risk factors for primary victimization received HRBI. Trainee acceptability ratings of the screening process were high. Almost all (98–99%) trainees reported that the questions were easy to understand, they took the questions on the screener seriously, and they answered the screener questions honestly. A total of 93% of trainees believed their answers would be kept private, and most (96%) said they felt comfortable answering the questions on the screener. Only 5% of trainees reported experiencing intense emotions during the screener, and only 9% of trainees agreed that questions on the screener brought up bad memories or upsetting feelings. Despite experiencing intense emotions during the screener, many trainees indicated that taking the screener was “worth it” or “nothing [they] couldn’t handle.” Ratings of social desirability were not significantly associated with patterns of responses to the screening measures.

Acceptability and appropriateness of planned outcome measures

As shown in Table 2, the outcome measures generally performed well (as suggested by the alphas), and very few trainees skipped survey questions (indicating refusal to respond). Pretest descriptive statistics and internal consistency on the measures are shown in Table 4. High rates of missingness on measures later in the assessments (e.g., risky dating or social behaviors) suggested that trainees did not have adequate time to complete them. Ratings of social desirability were not significantly associated with patterns of responses to the outcome measures.

Discussion

This is the first large feasibility study of a tailored sexual assault prevention program in a military training environment. This study found that the SCC program could be feasibly implemented and tested in the BMT environment, and that trainees found the program and data collection procedures to be acceptable. We identified areas in which modifications to the program content or implementation approach would benefit trainees or increase ease of implementation at BMT. The SCC program and the current study are a first step toward answering calls for development, implementation, and rigorous evaluation of comprehensive sexual assault prevention programming for the military [9, 27]. The results reinforce the importance of feasibility testing of novel interventions prior to full program implementation and evaluation [11, 29] and yield important considerations for future work.

Consistent with our hypotheses for Objective 1, adherence to program content was strong, but there were consistent challenges implementing all the tablet and classroom-based content and assessments in the time allotted for SCC classes. If program implementation remains within the same 6-h delivery window, results suggest that the amount of content should be reduced, and the assessments should be streamlined to allow all trainees to complete the tablet programs and to give instructors additional time for classroom interaction. This would reduce pressure on the instructors, improve adherence to classroom activities as designed, and ensure that trainees receive the intended take-home messages of each part of the program, all of which would improve the program’s chance of success and efficacy in a subsequent evaluation.

Trainee acceptability of SCC was generally high, but there were some variations across tablet program groups, which suggests that it is critical for feasibility studies to examine subgroup differences in acceptability rather than relying on full sample averages. Female trainees were more likely than male trainees to indicate they desired more classroom interaction, whereas male trainees were more likely than female trainees to indicate they desired more tablet time; the difference may reflect gender differences in learning and communication styles. Likeability of the tablet content was also slightly lower among RVP program participants, which may be related to their higher reported rates of experiencing intense emotions during the training. However, the low rates of trainee opt-outs and distress responses (< 1% of trainees) suggest that the content may enhance awareness of and allow trainees to confront uncomfortable feelings in a way that feels acceptable and not traumatic. Overall ratings of content acceptability met a priori thresholds, but reducing tablet content to increase time for deeper discussion of sensitive topics in the classroom may further improve acceptability.

There is often concern about whether universal prevention approaches focused on identifying individual and environmental risk factors for victimization could be perceived as blaming victims [54]. Although levels of perceived victim blaming during SCC were low overall, the proportions of trainees in some subgroups who perceived that the classroom content blamed victims for sexual assault exceeded 10%. The reason could be that some activities and discussions were cut short to stay on time, so that key corrections of language and misperceptions may have been missed. Findings suggest the need to revise some of the classroom activities to improve instructor control over messaging around victim blaming and to focus on countering victim-blaming perspectives and language.

This study determined that SCC is technologically and logistically feasible (Objective 2). However, this initial feasibility study was implemented with a large group of contracted instructors who were closely supervised by the research team and who took on technology maintenance tasks beyond the typical instructor role. The research team was also heavily involved in technology support. To be sustainable, this implementation model would require assigning or hiring additional staff to instruct classes and to maintain the technology and equipment. It will be important in future research to test implementation approaches that can rely on typical military training infrastructure—for example, one instructor per classroom and integration of content delivery with existing technology.

Under Objective 3, and consistent with Goldstein, Scaglione, and colleagues [14], this study found it was feasible and acceptable to administer a brief tablet-based screening procedure to identify individuals’ past experiences and risk factors; even trainees who had an emotional reaction to the questions understood their value. Using the screener allowed for delivery of tailored interventions, which enabled trainees to receive sensitive content privately and enabled content to reach trainees at risk for victimization and sexual assault survivors without identifying them. None of this would be possible in standard group training. Indeed, perceived relevance of the content was high. This approach may be appropriate for use in other military settings to provide the most effective programming for different risk profiles while also meeting policy requirements that all personnel receive sexual assault prevention training. However, it is also important to reflect on potential variation across participant subgroups in the accuracy, acceptability, and burden of this approach [14].

The findings also suggest the need for reductions to the data collection burden. Many trainees did not complete the survey instruments in the allotted time, yielding a loss of data. Trainee responses to the pretest questions also suggested potential revisions. For example, more than 95% of trainees responded correctly to several of the knowledge items at pretest. While this may indicate a ceiling effect or suggest this population is quite knowledgeable on the topic when they arrive at BMT, it may also suggest that knowledge items need to focus on more challenging concepts to ensure variability and room for improvement. Although internal consistency was acceptable (> 0.70) [55] for all scaled measures, we identified items within scales that had low item-total correlations and could be removed to reduce burden.

Limitations and future directions

Strengths of this study include its large scale and multiple informants and dimensions of feasibility. The study provided promising evidence of program feasibility and contributed data about levels of adherence and acceptability compared to thresholds, which are difficult to find in the literature.

Our results suggest directions for additional pilot and evaluation research on SCC, some of which is ongoing. Future studies could inform content revisions needed to enhance program acceptability and reach. Examination of factors correlated with acceptability (e.g., demographic characteristics) would shed light on the proportion of trainees who may inadvertently be receiving training that lacks personal relevance. Moreover, because of efforts to protect participant privacy, we could not identify flights in the data, so we could not examine links between instructor characteristics (such as gender or veteran status) or classroom composition (male-male or male–female paired flights) and trainee acceptability. Understanding these associations would support recommendations regarding ideal instructor qualifications or the possible need for different approaches when instructing all-male or coed classes. Future research would also benefit from linking quality and fidelity of implementation (including dosage) with trainee acceptability and behavioral outcomes to understand the potential impact of implementation improvements.

The outcome measures used in this study were adapted from the evaluations of the programs that informed SCC’s development to be relevant to SCC program content. As such, additional work to understand the psychometric characteristics of the measures, including in demographic or risk-based subgroups, is important to ensure accurate and appropriate measurement of constructs with the target population before conducting analyses of changes in program outcomes from pretest to posttest. Such analyses are in progress. Finally, rigorous evaluation of SCC, including a comparison group and measurement of longer-term, behavioral outcomes, is warranted. This may include identifying needs for adaptation and working through implementation considerations in different military settings. An ongoing evaluation of SCC at the US Air Force Academy will contribute additional information about SCC adaptation, implementation, and outcomes.

Conclusions

Future implementation and evaluation of the SCC program and other sexual assault prevention programs can benefit from the findings of this feasibility study. This study demonstrated high levels of feasibility and acceptability of SCC, suggesting that hybrid approaches to prevention program delivery may fit well in military training environments. High-quality implementation of such programs with large groups of participants may require more resources than typical training; collecting data about program effectiveness will be an important next step to inform decisions about investment of such resources.
