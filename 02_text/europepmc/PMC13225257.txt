ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInfertility significantly impacts self-image, sexuality, and interpersonal dynamics. While sexual dysfunction is a known consequence of infertility, its prevalence and impact on marital adjustment, specifically within the socio-cultural context of Bangladesh remain under-explored. The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of sexual dysfunction among infertile men and women and to examine its association with marital adjustment.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in Bangladesh and data were collected from September 2024 to June 2025. A total of 658 participants (320 men and 338 women) were recruited via systematic random sampling. Sexual function was evaluated using the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-EF), the Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT), and the 6-item Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-6). Marital adjustment was assessed using the Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS).ResultsThe overall prevalence of sexual dysfunction in this cohort was 25.4% (n = 167/658). Men were disproportionately affected compared to women. Among men (n = 320), 37.5% (n = 120) had at least one form of male sexual dysfunction, defined as erectile dysfunction (ED) and/or overt premature ejaculation (PE). Specifically, ED was identified in 33.7% (n = 108) of male participants, categorized as mild (24.1%), mild-to-moderate (8.1%), and moderate (1.6%). Overt PE (PEDT >11) was present in 13.1% (n = 42), including 9.3% (n = 30) with comorbid ED and PE, while 12 men had overt PE without ED. Probable PE (PEDT 9-10) was identified in 5.6% (n = 18) but was not included in the composite definition of male sexual dysfunction. Among women (n = 338), the prevalence of female sexual dysfunction was significantly lower at 13.9% (n = 47). Sexual dysfunction was inversely correlated with marital adjustment scores across both genders (P < .05).ConclusionA substantial “gender paradox” exists in infertility-related sexual health in Bangladesh, where men exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of sexual dysfunction than women. These findings highlight the necessity of integrating sexual medicine and psychological counseling into routine infertility management to improve both reproductive outcomes and marital quality. Strengths include the use of validated psychometric instruments and a relatively large, clinic-based sample. Limitations include cross-sectional design and reliance on self-report measures, which may limit generalizability and causal interpretation.Keywords: infertility, sexual dysfunction, erectile dysfunction, premature ejaculation, female sexual dysfunction, marital adjustment, dyadic adjustment scale, Bangladesh

BODY
Introduction

Infertility is a complex reproductive health issue that extends beyond biological dysfunction, profoundly impacting the psychological, sexual, and relational well-being of couples.1 The transition from spontaneous “love-making” to performance-oriented “baby-making” often shifts the focus of sexual intimacy toward procreation, leading to significant distress and diminished sexual spontaneity.2

Over the past two decades, research has consistently shown that infertility adversely affects multiple dimensions of marital life, including communication, intimacy, and overall dyadic adjustment.3 While some studies suggest that the shared challenge of infertility may foster emotional closeness, the prevailing evidence indicates that infertility-related stress—often exacerbated by prolonged treatment—correlates with higher rates of marital dissatisfaction.4,5 In South Asia, these challenges are amplified by pronatalist cultural norms where childlessness carries a heavy social stigma.6 In Iran and Pakistan, for instance, infertile couples frequently report moderate-to-low marital satisfaction directly linked to sociocultural pressures and infertility-related stress.7,8

In Bangladesh, the burden of infertility is particularly significant. Estimates suggest a prevalence of approximately 15% among women aged 45-49, yet it remains an under-prioritized area within the national Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights agenda.9,10 Bangladeshi women seeking fertility care report high rates of depression and anxiety, often compounded by gendered blame and extended-family pressures.11,12 Furthermore, the reliance on informal healthcare providers and the financial strain of tertiary care add layers of complexity to the couple’s relationship.13

The interplay between sexual functioning and marital adjustment is critical. Factors such as “scheduled sex,” treatment fatigue, and the psychological burden of infertility can manifest as reduced desire, arousal difficulties, and erectile dysfunction (ED).14 Despite the clinical importance of these issues, dyadic data on the prevalence of sexual dysfunction and its specific impact on marital adjustment among infertile couples in Bangladesh remain scarce.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study aims to determine the prevalence of sexual dysfunction among infertile men and women attending tertiary-care clinics in Bangladesh and to evaluate its association with marital adjustment. Understanding these dynamics is essential for developing integrated biopsychosocial interventions that support both reproductive and sexual health. In light of these complex dynamics, “How do gender-specific experiences of sexual dysfunction uniquely influence marital adjustment within the socio-cultural fabric of infertility in Bangladesh?”

Methods

Study design and setting

This hospital-based, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Reproductive Endocrinology and Infertility departments of two tertiary-level hospitals in Dhaka, Bangladesh. Data were obtained between September 2024 and June 2025. These centers serve as major referral hubs, capturing a diverse patient demographic from across the country.

Participants and sampling strategy

The sample size was determined using a single-population proportion formula. Assuming a 50% prevalence of sexual dysfunction (based on reported ranges of 43%-90% in women and 48%-58% in men),15 a 99% confidence level (𝑍 = 2.576), and a 5% margin of error, the required sample size was calculated at 664.

Participants were recruited using systematic random sampling. With an estimated clinic flow of 9600 patients during the study period, a sampling interval of 14 was established (9600/664≈14). After a random start via lottery, every 14th eligible patient was invited to participate. If the 14th was missing then the next available patient approached to be included in the study. In total 658 participants (320 men and 338 women) was recruited.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Confirmed diagnosis of infertility by an infertility specialist; and (2) currently married and cohabiting with their spouse.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Couples in which both partners were undergoing simultaneous infertility treatment at the study sites were excluded to ensure that each participant represented an independent observation (ie, no paired or couple data were included) (2) individuals with a pre-existing medical diagnosis of sexual dysfunction or those receiving treatment for the same prior to the infertility diagnosis.

Main outcome measures and tools

A semi-structured questionnaire was utilized to collect socio-demographic and clinical characteristics. Sexual and marital functions were assessed using the following validated instruments; Male sexual function was evaluated using the Bengali version of the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) and the Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT). Erectile dysfunction was defined as an IIEF-EF score of ≤25, indicating varying degrees of dysfunction.16 Premature ejaculation (PE) was identified using the PEDT, with a cut-off score of >11 indicating the presence of PE.17 Female sexual function was assessed using the 6-item Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-6), with a cut-off score of ≤19 indicating sexual dysfunction.18 Marital adjustment was assessed using the Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (R-DAS), a 14-item self-report instrument validated in Bengali that measures consensus, satisfaction, and cohesion in relationships. Scores range from 0 to 69, with ≥48 indicating non-distress and ≤47 indicating marital/relationship distress.19

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize the data. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square (χ2) tests. To identify independent factors associated with sexual dysfunction and marital satisfaction, binary logistic regression models were constructed. Variables were selected for multivariate analysis based on both univariate results (P < .05) and theoretical/clinical relevance as supported by prior literature. Statistical significance was predefined at P < .05.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the participating institution(s). All participants provided written informed consent after being briefed on the study’s objectives and the confidentiality of their data.

Results

The study included 658 participants, comprising 320 infertile men and 338 infertile women. Demographic analysis revealed significant age and occupational disparities between the groups. In the female infertility group, 66.3% of women were aged ≤30 years, while in the male infertility group, participants were more evenly distributed across age categories (Table 1). A vast majority of male participants’ spouses were housewives (83.1%), whereas spouses of female participants were more likely to be older (38.5% >35 years) and highly educated (40.2% graduates).

Regarding infertility characteristics (Table 2), women reported a significantly earlier age at marriage compared to men (82% vs. 42.8%; ≤25 years). Primary infertility was the predominant type, particularly among men (79.7%). Notably, a marked shift in sexual intercourse (SI) frequency was observed post-diagnosis; couples in the female infertility group reported a higher frequency (>3 times/month: 72.2%) compared to the male infertility group (32.2%).

Sexual dysfunction affected 25.4% of the total cohort. A “gender paradox” was evident as men were disproportionately affected compared to women. Among men, ED of any degree was identified in 33.7% (n = 108), and PE was reported by 13.1% (n = 42). In contrast, female sexual dysfunction (FSD) was significantly less prevalent, identified in only 13.9% (n = 47) of women (Figure 1).

Analysis of the R-DAS scores revealed significant differences in marital dynamics between genders (Table 3). Infertile men reported significantly higher scores in Consensus (26.39 ± 3.59), Satisfaction (16.98 ± 2.55), and Total R-DAS (52.44 ± 6.14) compared to infertile women (P < .001). Conversely, infertile women scored significantly higher in the Cohesion construct (10.99 ± 2.94) indicating a greater tendency toward shared activities and discussions despite lower overall satisfaction.

A clear association was found between sexual health and marital adjustment. Higher R-DAS scores (Consensus and Satisfaction) were positively correlated with better erectile function (IIEF) and female sexual function (FSFI-6), while showing a significant negative correlation with PE.

Marital distress was significantly more prevalent among infertile women (34%) than men (17.2%, P < .001) (Figure 2). Table 4 highlights that while the overall trend for ED and marital distress did not reach statistical significance (P = .065), specific conditions like PE were significantly associated with increased marital distress (P = .012). Furthermore, 42.6% of women with FSD reported marital distress compared to 32.6% of those without FSD, although this specific association was not statistically significant in this cohort (P = .183).

To identify the independent factors influencing marital adjustment (Total R-DAS score), both univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed (Table 5).

Note: Multivariate model includes variables that were significant in the univariate analysis and/or a priori clinical relevance. Reference (Ref) categories are provided for categorical variables. *Statistically significant at P < .05.

In the univariate model, several demographic and clinical factors were significantly associated with marital adjustment. The subject of infertility was a robust predictor (B = −2.772; P < .001), with women scoring nearly 2.8 points lower on the R-DAS than men. Additionally, higher partner’s age (B = −0.151; P < .001) and a longer duration of infertility treatment (B = −0.160; P = .034) were significantly associated with decreased marital adjustment. Conversely, higher educational attainment (B = 0.693; P < .011) and an older age at marriage (B = 0.118; P < .011) were positively correlated with relationship quality. Notably, the frequency of SI both before (P < .011) and after diagnosis (P < .011) showed a significant negative association with R-DAS scores, reflecting the potential strain of “scheduled” or performance-oriented intimacy.

After adjusting for confounding variables in the multivariate model, 4r factors remained independent predictors of marital adjustment. Being the female partner remained a strong independent predictor of lower marital adjustment (B = −2.698; β = −.169;). Participants without sexual dysfunction reported significantly higher marital adjustment scores (B = 2.367; β = .131; P = .001), highlighting the critical role of sexual health in dyadic stability. Higher level of education continued to show a positive impact on marital quality (B = 0.708; = 0.0; P = .048). Increased frequency of intercourse after the infertility diagnosis remained negatively associated with marital adjustment (B = −0.573; = −0.121; P = .010), possibly indicating that increased frequency under the pressure of conception may deteriorate overall relationship satisfaction.

Figure 2 shows that marital distress was more common among women than men, whereas men were more likely to report no marital distress.

Discussion

This study reveals a significant prevalence of sexual dysfunction among infertile couples in Bangladesh, highlighting a distinct “gender paradox.” While infertility is often culturally framed as a female-centric issue in South Asian societies, our findings demonstrate that male partners are disproportionately affected by sexual health challenges, with nearly 37.5% exhibiting either ED or PE. In contrast, FSD was notably lower at 13.9%. The high prevalence of ED (33.7%) and PE (13.1%) among male participants aligns with global trends suggesting that infertility-related stress often manifests as performance anxiety in men. The transition from spontaneous intimacy to “scheduled sex” for procreation creates a high-pressure environment, frequently leading to secondary ED. Similar reductions in intimacy and increased psychological burden among men in infertility contexts have been reported in studies from Turkey and Pakistan.7,8

The comparatively low prevalence of female sexual dysfunction (13.9%) in this study should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect underreporting rather than a true epidemiological difference. In sociocultural contexts like Bangladesh, stigma, modesty norms, and the prioritization of reproduction over sexual well-being may discourage open disclosure, leading to social desirability bias.20 A prior Bangladeshi study reported female sexual dysfunction rates as high as 56%, suggesting that sociocultural context and reporting bias may significantly influence prevalence estimates.21 Additionally, differences in measurement tools, sample characteristics, and study populations may further explain variability across studies, making these findings context-specific and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

In Bangladesh, the social meaning of childlessness is amplified by gendered blame and extended family interference. Women tend to seek treatment earlier due to societal pressure, while men often present later, reflecting gendered perceptions of reproductive responsibility.22,23 Our findings suggest that the psychological burden of these external pressures likely exacerbates sexual performance issues, particularly in men who may feel their masculinity is challenged by both infertility and sexual inadequacy. The notable rate of comorbid ED and PE further underscores the clinical complexity of addressing sexual dysfunction within infertility care in this sociocultural setting.

Our results indicate a robust inverse correlation between sexual dysfunction and marital adjustment scores across the R-DAS subscales (Consensus, Satisfaction, and Cohesion). As sexual intimacy declines, the dyadic bond appears to weaken, reinforcing a cycle of relational distress. This is consistent with regression findings from the present study, where absence of sexual dysfunction remained a strong positive predictor of marital adjustment (B = 2.36, P = .001).14 Comparable findings have been reported in sociocultural contexts such as Pakistan, Iran, and India, where infertility-related stress acts as a major contributor to marital instability and reduced relational satisfaction.6,24,25 The “baby-making” focus described in prior literature is also evident here, where the mechanical nature of intercourse during fertile windows strips away the emotional cohesion necessary for healthy marital adjustment.

A particularly noteworthy finding in this study is the paradoxical pattern observed in marital dynamics among infertile women. Although women demonstrated significantly lower overall marital adjustment scores and higher levels of marital distress compared to men, they reported significantly higher Cohesion scores (10.99 ± 2.94). This suggests that, despite experiencing greater dissatisfaction and emotional strain, women may actively engage in maintaining relational closeness through shared activities, communication, and efforts to preserve the relationship.

This apparent contradiction can be understood within the sociocultural context of Bangladesh, where women are often socialized to prioritize relationship maintenance and family stability, even in the face of personal distress.26 Higher Cohesion scores may therefore reflect compensatory relational behaviors, wherein women invest more effort in sustaining the partnership despite underlying dissatisfaction. Such behavior may be driven by societal expectations, fear of stigma associated with marital discord or infertility, and limited autonomy in decision-making within patriarchal family structures.27 Additionally, this pattern may indicate that Cohesion, as measured by shared activities and interactions, does not necessarily equate to emotional satisfaction or relational quality. Women may continue to participate in joint activities and maintain communication while simultaneously experiencing emotional distress, highlighting a divergence between behavioral engagement and subjective marital well-being.

The substantial prevalence of sexual dysfunction in this infertile cohort (approximately one in 4 individuals) necessitates a shift in clinical practice.28 Infertility management in tertiary care centers should not remain limited to hormonal or surgical interventions. Integrating sexual medicine and psychological counseling is essential. Clinicians should routinely screen for ED, PE, and FSD as part of infertility workups to break the cycle of sexual-marital distress, which may itself hinder natural conception.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the large, systematically selected sample from tertiary care settings, enhancing statistical power and contextual relevance. However, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Use of self-reported tools may introduce social desirability bias, particularly for sensitive domains such as female sexuality. The assessment of male sexual function was limited to ED and PE, potentially underestimating the overall burden. The absence of a fertile control group restricts interpretation of infertility-specific effects. Additionally, reliance on individual-level reporting for marital adjustment without partner data may not fully capture dyadic dynamics. Finally, differences in age distribution, especially among female participants, may confound estimates of female sexual dysfunction.

Conclusion

This study underscores a significant “gender paradox” within the infertility landscape of Bangladesh, where the burden of sexual dysfunction and marital distress manifests differently across genders. Our findings reveal a substantial prevalence of sexual dysfunction in this infertile cohort, with men exhibiting disproportionately higher rates of erectile and ejaculatory disorders (37.5%) compared to female sexual dysfunction (13.9%). Despite this, the psychological and relational impact remains profound for both partners, as sexual inadequacy significantly correlates with diminished marital adjustment and dyadic cohesion.

The results highlight that infertility is not merely a biological impairment but a complex relational and psychosocial condition deeply influenced by patriarchal norms and cultural expectations. Factors such as female-factor infertility, prolonged treatment duration, and advancing age further exacerbate marital instability. Conversely, higher educational attainment and the absence of sexual dysfunction serve as protective factors for relationship quality.

From a clinical perspective, these findings necessitate a paradigm shift in infertility management in South Asia. It is imperative to move beyond purely medicalized interventions and integrate biopsychosocial frameworks, including routine sexual health screening and couple-based counseling, into tertiary care protocols. Addressing the “hidden” burden of sexual dysfunction may not only improve the quality of life for infertile couples but also enhance the overall success of reproductive treatments. Future research employing longitudinal designs is warranted to further elucidate the causal pathways between infertility-related stress, sexual medicine, and long-term marital outcomes in diverse sociocultural settings.
