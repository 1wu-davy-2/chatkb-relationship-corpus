ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs intercultural marriages become increasingly common in the United States, it is crucial to examine the factors that affect their stability and satisfaction. This study explores how demographic factors, cultural orientation, and social support influence marital satisfaction in Eastern men‐Western women couples. A total of 89 individuals participated in this study, with 48.3% male (69.7% Middle Eastern and 30.3% Asian) and 51.7% female (United Statesian‐European). Cultural collectivism, social support, and level of education were significant positive predictors of marital satisfaction. Collectivism, particularly horizontal collectivism which reflects egalitarian interdependence, emerged as a significant positive predictor of marital satisfaction. While social support was the strongest predictor of marital satisfaction, collectivism was not a significant predictor when social support was included, although its association remained positive. Additionally, there were no significant gender differences in marital satisfaction. Findings suggest that clinicians working with Eastern‐Western couples should examine how partners balance collectivistic expectations, including family involvement and relational interdependence, with individualistic preferences for autonomy and independence. This balance appears to meaningfully shape marital satisfaction. Future research should examine a wider range of intercultural couples to determine whether similar patterns emerge across different racial, religious, and socioeconomic groups. Understanding these intersections can help policymakers and practitioners develop culturally responsive relationship education and support programs tailored to the needs of diverse intercultural families.

BODY
Introduction

As intercultural marriages continue to rise in North America, recent studies indicate that these unions experience a higher risk of marital dissolution compared to culturally homogamous marriages (Tura 2025; Zhang and van Hook 2009). Despite this elevated risk, little is known about the cultural processes that may either protect or strain these relationships, particularly how partners navigate differences in individualistic and collectivistic values. Understanding how these cultural orientations shape partners' expectations and relational behaviors is important for clinicians (Rajkumar 2023; Uhlich et al. 2022). Although Intercultural marriages can present unique challenges as couples navigate differing cultural norms and values, they can also offer a rich opportunity to explore the influence of these cultural orientations on relationship dynamics (Naeimi et al. 2024).

The present study specifically examines couples where the husband is from a collectivist culture (Middle Eastern or Asian) and the wife is from an individualistic (United Statesian‐European) one. We focused on these couples because research suggests that gender norms and expectations often differ significantly between collectivistic and individualistic cultures. For example, in some Eastern cultures, traditional masculinity is associated with patriarchal structures, hierarchical family roles, and strong family obligations (Beitin and Aprahamian 2014). In contrast, Western cultures generally emphasize egalitarian relationships, individual autonomy, and personal fulfillment in marriage (van de Vijver 2007). This combination of differing cultural and gender role expectations provides a unique context for studying how marital satisfaction is negotiated. This study examines how cultural tendencies, as well as social support, influence satisfaction within these Eastern husband–Western wife marriages with implications for culturally informed clinical work with intercultural couples.

Understanding how cultural orientations and social support shape partners' relational experiences is essential for research, relationship educators, and clinical practice, particularly for clinicians and scholars working with intercultural couples. This study does not offer direct intervention strategies. Instead, it highlights the complex interplay of collectivist and individualist values in shaping marital dynamics. The findings offer insight into a form of intercultural relationship that is often misunderstood or stereotyped (Iankilevitch and Chasteen 2024; Machette and Cionea 2023). This can deepen practitioners' cultural awareness and inform their work with diverse couples. Furthermore, this study addresses a gap in the literature by examining how cultural orientation, education, and social support influence marital satisfaction in Eastern men–Western women couples.

Given the culturally sensitive nature of this topic, it is important to share our own perspectives as researchers and therapists. As Iranian women and couple and family therapists, we bring a culturally informed lens to this study. While we have not personally experienced interracial marriage, our clinical work includes intercultural couples, and we frequently encounter themes related to cultural orientation, relational expectations, and the negotiation of difference. This study is rooted in both our clinical observations and a shared commitment to expanding culturally responsive research in couple dynamics.

Cultural Orientation and Marital Satisfaction: Conceptual Framework

Marital satisfaction is shaped by cultural norms, relationship expectations, and interpersonal dynamics (Bohlander 1999). This study examines marital satisfaction through the lens of individualism–collectivism, which influences gender roles, family involvement, and emotional expression (Triandis 2001). Although collectivist cultural tendencies often emphasize social harmony and family obligation, and individualist tendencies emphasize autonomy and personal fulfillment (Lucas et al. 2008), cultures can express both orientations simultaneously to varying degrees (Gorla et al. 2024).

To explore the influence of cultural orientations on marital satisfaction, it is important to understand the fundamental differences between collectivistic and individualistic cultures. In collectivist cultures, individuals prioritize the goals and desires of their in‐group, such as family or nation, over personal desires, and adhere to group norms (Triandis 2001). In contrast, individualistic cultures emphasize autonomy, personal goals, and alignment with personal attitudes (Triandis 2001). One key distinction between collectivistic and individualistic cultures is the horizontal‐vertical dimension. This dimension identifies four cultural types: Vertical Collectivism (VC), viewing the self as a part of a group that contains a normative hierarchy and inequality; Horizontal Collectivism (HC), viewing the self as a part of a group which recognizes equality among all members; Vertical Individualism (VI), viewing the self as completely independent and accepting the inequality that is present among individuals; and Horizontal Individualism (HI), viewing the individual as fully independent and considering equality among individuals as an optimal scenario (Triandis 2001).

Previous studies have explored the impact of these cultural orientations on behavior, cognition, and emotions, revealing different cultural perceptions of love and emotional expression (Gurung and Duong 1999; Kazim and Rafique 2021; Lucas et al. 2008; Tsai et al. 2006). For instance, both Lucas et al. (2008) and Kazim and Rafique (2021) emphasize that marital satisfaction is influenced by a combination of universal and culture‐specific factors. Across diverse cultural contexts, factors such as trust, emotional bonding, communication, and empathy consistently contribute to marital well‐being. These elements reflect an evolutionary and cross‐cultural core to relationship satisfaction (Kazim and Rafique 2021; Lucas et al. 2008). This dual pattern of universal and culture‐specific influences is further supported by a recent study showing that predictors of marital satisfaction, such as attachment, stress, and problem‐solving skills, operate similarly across 65 countries. At the same time, their relative impact differs across nations based on broader sociocultural contexts (Grau et al. 2025).

At the same time, each study highlights unique cultural influences on marital satisfaction. Lucas et al. (2008), in their cross‐national study of couples from the United States, Britain, Turkey, and China, emphasized the role of culturally derived norms and obligations in marriage. These cultural factors influence how spouses perceive and fulfill their marital expectations. Lucas et al. particularly underscored the role of extended family obligations in collectivist cultures, a factor that contrasts with the more autonomous relational dynamics found in individualistic cultures. In contrast, Kazim and Rafique (2021), in their mini review, focused on the interplay between family obligations in collectivist societies and the emphasis on personal autonomy in individualistic cultures. They demonstrated how cultural values mediate marital satisfaction through different social expectations. Their findings further reinforced that the expression of marital satisfaction is context‐dependent and that cultural norms influence how universal predictors (e.g., trust, communication) manifest in specific relationships.

Marital Satisfaction

Marital satisfaction refers to how well spouses perceive their partners as meeting their needs and desires (Bohlander 1999). It is linked to family cohesion and quality of life, whereas marital dissatisfaction can lead to stress, depression, anxiety, and family dissolution (Skowron 2000).

There are many factors influencing marital satisfaction, such as demographic factors (age, marriage duration, education, number of children, economic factors, employment), interpersonal factors (personality traits like extraversion and agreeableness), psychological health, communication, and interaction skills (commitment, intimacy), spiritual and religious factors, and sexual satisfaction (Zaheri et al. 2016), with recent research also highlighting the role of cultural expectations and sociocultural contexts (Grau et al. 2025; Machette and Cionea 2023); for example, based on the study by Zhang and Liang (2023), higher education levels are positively associated with marital satisfaction. Individuals with more education tend to exhibit better communication and conflict resolution skills. These skills contribute to healthier and more satisfying relationships. The findings suggest that education promotes emotional intelligence and mutual understanding, thereby enhancing marital quality.

Moreover, Rashidi Fakari et al. (2022) postulated that socioeconomic status (SES) has a significant positive relationship with marital satisfaction, with higher SES associated with increased satisfaction levels. SES indirectly influences marital satisfaction by reducing stress and depression, thereby fostering healthier relationships. The same study demonstrated that higher levels of social support directly and indirectly enhance marital satisfaction by preventing emotional withdrawal, reducing isolation, and fostering positive emotional bonds.

Other studies, such as that by Lavner et al. (2016), have explored the impact of factors like communication on marital satisfaction. This study highlighted the bidirectional relationship between communication and marital satisfaction. Cross‐sectional findings indicate that couples with higher marital satisfaction tend to exhibit more positive, less negative, and more effective communication (Lavner et al. 2016). However, Lavner et al. (2016) longitudinal analysis revealed that marital satisfaction is a stronger and more consistent predictor of communication quality than the reverse. Their findings suggest that interventions focusing solely on communication skills may not result in sustained improvements in marital satisfaction.

Marital Satisfaction in Intercultural Marriages

Marital satisfaction in intercultural marriage has been found to be influenced by multiple factors, such as age, length of the marriage, socioeconomic status, cultural differences, gender expectations, discrimination, and racism (Afful et al. 2015). While some studies suggest lower marital satisfaction among interracial couples (Bratter and King 2008; Chow 2000), others have found no significant differences compared to intraracial couples (Troy et al. 2006).

According to Rich (2023), marital satisfaction among interracial and intercultural couples is influenced by several key factors. For example, high commitment levels significantly enhance relationship satisfaction. Moreover, practices such as joint counseling, shared finances, and open communication have the potential to strengthen commitment. Additionally, Rich (2023) postulated that appreciating and understanding each other's cultural differences is critical, as perspective‐taking and broadening one's understanding of the partner's background can foster mutual respect and deeper connections.

Similarly, Machette and Cionea (2023) identified other important factors that influence marital satisfaction among interracial couples. For instance, managing relationships with in‐laws, shaped by differing cultural values, can strain the couple's privacy and pose significant challenges. Moreover, the authors highlighted that language barriers or contrasting cultural communication styles play a crucial role in the marital satisfaction of interracial couples.

In the same vein, Tili and Barker (2015) emphasized that developing intercultural communication competence is essential for marital satisfaction among intercultural couples. Key aspects include self‐awareness and open‐mindedness, which enable partners to recognize their biases and appreciate diverse cultural perspectives.

Social Support and Marital Satisfaction

Social support refers to the material and interpersonal resources provided through diverse social relationships, which benefit the individual receiving them (Thompson 1995). Among these, research highlights that social support plays a particularly critical role in influencing marital satisfaction (St. Vil 2015; Walker et al. 2013). Rostami et al. (2014) found that support from a spouse was more important than social support from external sources. Furthermore, there are gender differences in both social and spousal support, with women being more likely to provide support to their husbands than vice versa.

Interpersonal resources include emotional support, informational support, and instrumental support (Thompson 1995). Social networks offer various types of support, such as psychological, social, and economic aids (St. Vil 2015). Theoretical models on social support can be categorized as providing three perspectives: stress and coping, social constructionist, and relationship (Thompson et al. 2006).

According to the stress and coping perspective, social support functions as a protective buffer that mitigates the negative impact of stressful events (Cobb 1976). From a different angle, the social constructionist or social cognitive model posits that how individuals perceive support influences their self‐esteem, which indirectly affects their health and well‐being (Vaux 1990). Within the relationship perspective, crucial interpersonal elements like intimacy, companionship, and social skills are strongly associated with the positive benefits derived from social support (Thompson et al. 2006). The current study aligns most closely with the relationship perspective of social support because it investigates how cultural differences, spousal support, and gendered dynamics shape marital satisfaction.

Building on this understanding, St. Vil (2015) examined the impact of social networks on marital satisfaction among African American couples. The study considered emotional support from family and practical support from both family and friends. Results indicated that higher emotional support from family increased marital satisfaction, but support from friends and family had different effects based on gender. African American husbands benefited from support from both friends and family, while for wives, this support was associated with lower levels of satisfaction.

Demographic Variables and Marital Satisfaction

Marital satisfaction is related to various factors, including the number of children, socioeconomic status (SES), life cycle stage, and ethnic identity. Research shows a stronger negative correlation between a greater number of children and marital satisfaction, particularly in individualistic cultures, compared to collectivist cultures where family is highly valued (Wendorf et al. 2011). Some research suggests that limited financial resources, such as low income and material hardship, pose a significant risk to marital stability. When couples' basic needs are not adequately met, they may be less focused on maintaining intimacy and emotional connection (Jackson et al. 2017). Therefore, lower economic status may be linked to reduced self‐reported levels of marital satisfaction (Conger et al. 1990). More recent research supports this pattern, showing that economic strain continues to predict lower marital quality, increased conflict, and reduced stability in contemporary couples (Falconier and Jackson 2020; Ross et al. 2019).

While it has been suggested that children help stabilize marriages, they actually seem to lower marital satisfaction. In fact, numerous studies have shown a negative relationship between marital satisfaction and the number of children (Twenge et al. 2003; Wendorf et al. 2011). The association between marriage duration and marital satisfaction typically follows a U‐shaped pattern or is negative, with marital satisfaction often declining in the middle years and improving later in the couple's life cycle (Wendorf et al. 2011).

Ethnic identity is a strong predictor of relationship quality and satisfaction (Gurung and Duong 1999) because it encompasses shared cultural experiences such as language, traditions, and ancestry, which shape individual identity (Afful et al. 2015) and, in turn, influence relationship dynamics. Jacobs and Labov (2002) highlighted significant variations in interracial marriage patterns across different races and ethnicities, influenced by factors such as immigration and socioeconomic status, gender dynamics, and educational level.

Hypotheses

Building on this literature review, the present study proposes the following hypotheses: (1a) Participants' age, gender, race, SES, length of marriage, education level, and number of children will predict the marital satisfaction of Eastern men‐Western women; (1b) Collectivistic cultural orientation will be positively associated with greater marital satisfaction; (2) social support will moderate the relationship between cultural orientation and marital satisfaction; (3) the marital satisfaction of Eastern husbands will be higher than the marital satisfaction of Western wives in interracial marriages. Guided by the literature and the proposed hypotheses, we designed a quantitative study to examine the predictors of marital satisfaction in Eastern husband‐Western wife couples.

Method

After receiving the Institutional Review Board approval from the Alliant International University, we conducted a cross‐sectional non‐experimental survey, recruiting 141 married individuals residing in the United States through convenience sampling. Following initial screening, 52 incomplete responses were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 89 participants. Eligible participants were required to be in an intercultural marriage for at least 5 years, with the husband from a Middle Eastern or Asian (collectivist) culture and the wife from an American‐European (individualistic) background. The final sample comprised 43 men (48.3%) and 46 women (51.7%). Participants ranged in age from their late 20s to late 70s, with most identifying as Middle Eastern (men) or White United Statesian‐European (women). Most couples had been married between five and 33 years, and the majority had two to three children. Educational attainment was relatively high, with over half of participants holding a master's degree or higher. Most participants reported middle socioeconomic status.

Measures

Marital Quality

The Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale (RDAS; Spanier 1976) is a well‐validated measure of overall relationship quality, encompassing key dimensions of dyadic functioning such as satisfaction, consensus, and cohesion. While the RDAS captures the broader construct of marital adjustment, it includes marital satisfaction as a central subcomponent, making it a suitable proxy for assessing satisfaction in the context of relationship dynamics (Busby et al. 1995). In this study, we use the RDAS as a comprehensive indicator of marital quality, with a primary focus on satisfaction.

The RDAS consists of 14 items that assess various aspects of relationships, such as quarrels, mutual interests, and shared activities. Responses are rated on a five or six‐point scale with varied anchors. Sample items include questions like, “How often do you and your partner quarrel?” and “Do you and your partner engage in outside interests together?” Scores are a sum of items and range from 0 to 69, with higher scores indicating greater relationship satisfaction. In the present sample, Cronbach's alphas, calculated for the total sample, ranged from 0.72 to 0.87 across the RDAS subscales, with an overall reliability of 0.86.

Individualism and Collectivism

The Individualism and Collectivism Scale (ICS; Triandis and Gelfand 1998) includes 16 items evaluating four dimensions: Vertical Collectivism (VC), Horizontal Collectivism (HC), Vertical Individualism (VI), and Horizontal Individualism (HI). Each subscale includes four items; higher scores indicate stronger endorsement of that orientation. Sample item (HC): “I feel good when we cooperate with others.” In the present sample, Cronbach's alphas ranged from 0.64 to 0.80.

Social Support

The 2‐Way SSS (Shakespeare‐Finch and Obst 2011) has 29 items categorized into giving and receiving emotional and instrumental support, rated on a six‐point scale that ranges from 0 (not at all) to 5 (always). The scale is calculated as a sum/mean and a higher total indicates greater social support. The scale captures support from both within and outside the marital relationship, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of interpersonal support systems. Sample item (receiving emotional support): “There is someone we can talk to about the pressures in my life.” In the present sample, Cronbach's alphas ranged from 0.88 to 0.96.

Statistical Analysis

The data was checked to see if it followed a normal distribution by looking at skewness (how symmetrical the data is) and kurtosis (how heavy or light the tails of the data distribution are). We also examined box plots to spot any unusual or extreme values in the data.

We began by calculating descriptive statistics for all variables, including means, medians, frequencies, standard deviations, and ranges, to provide an overall understanding of the dataset. Next, we conducted a hierarchical regression to assess the influence of demographic variables and cultural orientation on marital satisfaction, which allowed us to test the first two hypotheses. Before conducting the analysis, we created graphs and descriptive statistics to check the assumptions of normality of distribution, linear relationship between the dependent variable (marital satisfaction) and independent variables (age, gender, ethnicity, SES, years of marriage, education level, and number of children), normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity. All assumptions were met.

We then performed independent t‐tests to compare the marital satisfaction of Eastern husbands and Western wives, addressing the third hypothesis. Finally, we used Pearson's correlation to explore the relationships between the key variables. Throughout the analyses, we controlled for demographic factors to isolate the effect of cultural orientation on marital satisfaction. The following section presents the findings from the statistical analyses conducted to test the study's hypotheses.

Hypothesis One

Hypothesis 1a proposed that participants' age, gender, ethnicity, SES, length of marriage, education level, and number of children would predict marital satisfaction. In this study, SES was collected as a three‐category self‐report variable (low, middle, upper SES), and education was coded as a dichotomous variable (bachelor's degree or lower vs. master's degree or higher). To explore potential group differences, we conducted a series of preliminary analyses. We performed independent‐samples t‐tests and found no statistically significant differences in marital satisfaction based on gender (female or male; t(80.57) = −0.94, p = 0.352) or ethnicity (White/Euro‐United Statesian or non‐White; t(80.57) = −0.94, p = 0.352). However, when we compared marital satisfaction by education level (bachelor's degree or lower versus master's degree or higher), we found a statistically significant difference, t(85.63) = −1.93, p = 0.056, indicating that participants with higher education reported greater marital satisfaction. We also ran a one‐way ANOVA to assess marital satisfaction across different SES groups (low, middle, high), but the results showed no significant differences (p = 0.352).

Correlational analyses showed that marital satisfaction was not significantly associated with age, number of children, years married, or individualism. However, we found a significant positive correlation between marital satisfaction and collectivist cultural orientation, r(87) = 0.41, p < 0.001, indicating that participants with stronger collectivist values tended to report higher marital satisfaction. Based on these findings, we included both education level and collectivism in the subsequent hierarchical regression analysis.

For Hypothesis 1b, we examined whether collectivist cultural orientation would significantly predict marital satisfaction. We conducted a two‐step hierarchical regression analysis. In Step One, education level significantly predicted marital satisfaction F(1, 87) = 3.74, p = 0.056, accounting for 4.1% of the variance, which partially supports Hypothesis 1a. Participants with higher education levels (master's and above) reported higher marital satisfaction. However, 96% of the variance in marital satisfaction remained unexplained.

In Step Two, we added collectivist cultural orientation, which significantly improved the model, explaining an additional 16% of the variance. Collectivism emerged as the strongest predictor of marital satisfaction, supporting Hypothesis 1b. Table 3 presents the full regression result.

We conducted a follow‐up Hierarchical regression analysis to determine whether horizontal or vertical collectivism better predicted marital satisfaction. After controlling for education level, adding horizontal and vertical collectivism significantly improved the model, ΔF(2, 85) = 14.16, p < 0.001. The final model at Step Two was statistically significant, F(3, 85) = 11.07, p < 0.001, and accounted for 28.1% of the variance in marital satisfaction (R 2 = 0.281, adjusted R 2 = 0.255). Horizontal collectivism, a form of collectivism that emphasizes equality and interdependence rather than hierarchical structures, was a strong predictor of marital satisfaction. This finding suggests that egalitarian interdependence, rather than hierarchical obligation, contributed more to marital satisfaction among these couples.

Additionally, based on the mean collectivism score (M = 55.76, SD = 9.4) and the collectivism cut‐off score of 36, we found that 87 out of 89 participants scored above this threshold indicating that most participants demonstrated collectivist tendencies. However, given that all White/Euro‐American participants in this study were female and all Middle Eastern and Asian participants were male, it is not possible to clearly disentangle collectivist tendencies by ethnicity independently from gender. Moreover, previous research suggests that women often score higher on relational and interdependent traits (Loscocco and Walzer 2013), which may further complicate interpretation of these tendencies as purely cultural.

Hypothesis Two

We hypothesized that social support moderates the relationship between cultural orientation and marital satisfaction. We conducted a two‐step hierarchical regression to evaluate the moderating effect of social support, defined as the emotional, instrumental, and interpersonal resources exchanged within social relationships, on the relationship between marital satisfaction and collectivism.

The hierarchical regression results revealed that social support was the strongest predictor of marital satisfaction (β = 0.66, p < 0.001). Collectivism showed a positive but nonsignificant association with marital satisfaction when social support was included in the model (β = 0.09, p = 0.345). The analysis explained 47% of the variance in marital satisfaction (R 2 = 0.47, F(2, 86) = 38.12, p < 0.001). However, the interaction between collectivism and social support did not significantly impact marital satisfaction (β = 0.04, p = 0.624). Additionally, Pearson's correlation showed a strong positive relationship between collectivist culture and social support (r = 0.51, p = 0.001). Therefore, we concluded that social support did not moderate the effect of collectivism on marital satisfaction, and hypothesis two was not supported.

Hypothesis Three

We hypothesized that the marital satisfaction of Eastern husbands would be higher than that of Western wives in interracial marriages and conducted an independent‐samples t‐test to compare the marital satisfaction between men and women. The results indicated no significant difference in the mean scores for marital satisfaction between men (M = 46.60, SD = 8.8) and women (M = 48.20, SD = 7.06), t(80.57) = −0.94, p = 0.352. Therefore, hypothesis three was not supported.

Discussion

The study aimed to examine the relationship between marital satisfaction, cultural orientation, and social support in Eastern men (Asian or Middle Eastern) married to Western women (United Statesian with European ancestry) living in the United States. It also assessed the impact of various demographic factors on marital satisfaction. Of the seven demographic factors considered, only the level of education emerged as a significant and positive predictor of marital satisfaction. Additionally, among the two components of cultural orientation, collectivism and individualism, only collectivistic orientation was found to be a significant and positive predictor of marital satisfaction in regression models examining cultural orientation. In contrast, individualistic orientation did not significantly contribute to the model. Importantly, the follow‐up analyses showed that not all forms of collectivism contributed equally to marital satisfaction. Horizontal collectivism, which reflects egalitarian interdependence, emerged as the strongest predictor, while vertical collectivism, which reflects hierarchy and authority, did not significantly predict satisfaction. The nonsignificant results for individualism also indicate that individualistic tendencies may play a less central role in long‐term intercultural marriages, or that horizontal collectivism may hold greater influence over partners' relational experiences.

Predictors of Marital Satisfaction

Level of Education

In the current study, participants with higher education levels (master's degree and beyond) reported greater marital satisfaction compared to those with lower education levels (bachelor's and below). This finding aligns with Zhang and Liang (2023), who reported a significant positive relationship between higher levels of education and marital satisfaction. They also emphasized that increased educational attainment can enhance both the material and spiritual aspects of a couple's union. Moreover, Jacobs and Labov (2002) noted that college exposure can lead to more acceptance of interracial marriages. Supporting this, 94.4% of our participants in interracial marriages had some college education, with 51.4% having advanced degrees.

Gender and Ethnicity

This study suggests an intersection of gender and ethnicity since all male participants were Easterners (Middle Eastern or Asian), and all female participants were Westerners. This study had almost an equal number of Eastern male (n = 43) and Western female (n = 46) participants. There were no significant correlations between either ethnicity or gender and marital satisfaction. This finding contradicts previous studies that suggested men are more satisfied with their marriages compared to women (Al‐Krenawi 2010; Dhaher et al. 2009; Rostami et al. 2014). Despite expectations that cultural gender roles might influence marital satisfaction differently, this study found otherwise. By challenging common stereotypes and assumptions about gender roles, this study encourages a more nuanced understanding of the unique dynamics present in Eastern husband‐Western wife marriages. This lack of gender difference in marital satisfaction may be especially relevant among highly educated couples, who comprised the majority of this sample. Clarifying the intersection of gender and cultural background in these couples can help therapists tailor interventions that better align with each partner's cultural values, communication patterns, and relational expectations.

The lack of a significant correlation between gender and marital satisfaction may be explained by the unique intersection of gender and cultural values within this sample. Although prior research suggests that women often show higher levels of relational interdependence (Loscocco and Walzer 2013), the men in this study, who were predominantly Middle Eastern or Asian, also endorsed strong horizontal collectivistic values. Horizontal collectivism emphasizes equality, mutual support, and shared decision‐making, which align more closely with egalitarian relationship practices than with traditional hierarchical masculinity norms. Given that the sample largely exhibited horizontal collectivism, it is possible that this shared egalitarian orientation minimized gender‐based differences in marital satisfaction. Importantly, this pattern also illustrates that not all collectivist men endorse hierarchical or authoritarian values; many may instead prioritize equality, mutual support, and relational interdependence, which align more closely with horizontal collectivism than with traditional gender‐role expectations. This suggests that the Eastern husbands in the sample may hold relational values that differ from stereotypical expectations of masculinity. In turn, the shared emphasis on egalitarian interdependence across genders may help explain why gender differences in marital satisfaction did not emerge. Future research should examine how masculine identities are expressed within egalitarian forms of collectivism, and how these culturally influenced gender orientations shape relational dynamics in intercultural marriages.

Moreover, the intersection of gender and culture may create a conflation in relational dynamics. This is especially evident in collectivist societies, where gender roles and cultural values are deeply intertwined (Quek 2009). As Quek (2009) highlighted, collectivist norms such as we‐consciousness and valuing family can paradoxically foster more egalitarian relationship patterns, especially in dual‐career partnerships where equality becomes a pragmatic and relational goal.

Consequently, the discussion on gender differences in marital satisfaction remains inconclusive. Given the lack of significant findings related to gender differences in marital satisfaction, this study suggests that traditional expectations about gender‐based satisfaction may not fully apply to Eastern husband‐Western wife couples. Further research is needed to explore how cultural orientations influence marital dynamics across genders in these relationships.

Similarly, the current study found no significant correlation between ethnicity and marital satisfaction, which contradicts the results of previous studies that identified ethnicity as a strong predictor (Bulanda and Brown 2007; Gurung and Duong 1999). Although all participants were in mixed relationships, ethnicity itself did not significantly predict marital satisfaction in this study.

This may be because cultural orientation, rather than ethnicity, played a more influential role in shaping marital satisfaction among participants. This suggests that although individuals came from different ethnic backgrounds, their shared collectivist tendencies, such as prioritizing family and group cohesion, created a more significant common ground in their relationships than ethnic identity alone.

Cultural Orientation

This study highlights the critical role of horizontal collectivism, a form of collectivism that emphasizes sociability, interdependence, and egalitarian relationships, as the strongest predictor of marital satisfaction. This orientation stands in contrast to vertical collectivism, which values hierarchy and authority (Triandis and Gelfand 1998). The prominence of horizontal collectivism suggests that a cultural emphasis on equality within close relationships may be more beneficial to marital satisfaction than hierarchical forms of interdependence. This is particularly noteworthy considering that participants had been living in the U.S. for an extended period and included U.S.‐born women, yet still demonstrated collectivist tendencies. While the U.S. is typically considered individualistic, the sample's orientation toward horizontal collectivism may reflect both cultural adaptation and the influence of higher education levels, which often promote egalitarian ideals. These findings also diverge from previous studies (Lalonde et al. 2004; Hofstede et al. 2010), which reported a negative relationship between collectivism and marital satisfaction, further underscoring the importance of distinguishing between different types of collectivism.

Finally, while most participants demonstrated collectivist tendencies, the demographic composition of the sample, where all Middle Eastern and Asian participants were male and all White/Euro‐American participants were female, makes it difficult to determine whether these collectivist orientations reflect ethnicity, gender, or an interaction of both. Future studies should include a more balanced representation of gender within each ethnic group. This would allow for a clearer interpretation of whether collectivist tendencies are consistent across ethnic backgrounds independently from gender. Future research should explore how these factors interact with gender roles and acculturation to provide a more nuanced understanding of marital satisfaction in intercultural relationships.

These findings suggest a need to reconceptualize how individualism and collectivism are applied in relational contexts. Rather than rigid dichotomies, this study reveals that egalitarian interdependence, an intersection of collectivist and individualist values, may be most conducive to relationship satisfaction. This adds to a growing body of literature questioning simplistic cultural binaries and invites scholars to consider how values like equality, relational self‐construal, and cultural adaptation co‐occur in diverse couples.

The Role of Social Support as a Moderator

The study found that both social support and cultural orientation significantly and positively predicted marital satisfaction. Individuals with higher levels of social support and stronger collectivistic tendencies experienced greater marital satisfaction. Although these two constructs were highly correlated, suggesting an overlap, they were measured separately using distinct validated instruments. This indicates that while social support is an important component of collectivist cultural values, it still contributes uniquely to marital satisfaction. However, contrary to a previous study (Rostami et al. 2014), social support did not moderate the effect of collectivism on marital satisfaction, perhaps because in this sample, the two constructs function almost as a unified phenomenon. Emphasizing the importance of strong, culturally sensitive support networks remains crucial in addressing the specific needs of Eastern husband–Western wife couples.

Limitations

This study faced several limitations. First, the sample size (N = 89) was smaller than the required minimum (N = 122) based on the initial power analysis. However, preliminary tests confirmed that the hierarchical regression primarily focused on three key variables—education level, collectivism, and social support—met statistical requirements. A minimum sample size of 74 participants was sufficient, though generalizability remains limited. The restrictive inclusion criteria also contributed to the small sample, as Eastern men and Western women are less likely to intermarry (Pew Research Center 2015).

Second, the online survey format may have excluded individuals with limited internet access. Third, reliance on self‐reported data raises the possibility of social desirability bias, despite assurances of anonymity. Additionally, the study did not differentiate between distressed and non‐distressed couples, limiting its ability to examine how cultural orientation influences marital satisfaction in struggling relationships. Future research should explore how these factors interact with gender roles and acculturation to provide a more nuanced understanding of marital satisfaction in intercultural relationships.

Finally, the participant pool was disproportionately composed of highly educated Middle Eastern men, with fewer Asian participants, which limits the diversity and generalizability of the findings. This skew in representation likely reflects which individuals were more inclined to participate in the study and may not fully capture the range of experiences among Eastern men in intercultural marriages.

Clinical Implications

This study highlights the importance of recognizing the complexity of cultural values when working with couples comprising Eastern men and Western women. The data suggest a moderate orientation toward horizontal collectivism, a cultural orientation in which individuals see themselves as part of a collective while emphasizing equality among members (Triandis 2001), indicating that although collectivist values play a significant role, they coexist alongside individualistic influences in these relationships.

In clinical practice, therapists can assess individualism and collectivism either through standardized measures, such as the Individualism and Collectivism Scale, or through open‐ended clinical questioning that explores autonomy, family obligations, decision‐making, and expectations for relational roles. These assessments help couples identify and name the cultural values that shape their communication, boundaries, and conflict patterns. Such discussions allow therapists to use cultural orientation as a foundation for shared understanding rather than conflict, and to tailor interventions accordingly.

For example, when partners differ in decision‐making expectations, therapists might guide them through a structured dialogue to identify whether those expectations stem from collectivistic values (e.g., prioritizing family harmony) or individualistic values (e.g., autonomy in choices). A clinician might help a couple practice balanced decision‐making by asking the Eastern partner to articulate why group consensus feels important, while encouraging the Western partner to share how individual autonomy supports their well‐being.

Similarly, when conflict arises around family involvement (such as expectations about visiting extended family), therapists can help the couple negotiate a plan that honors collectivistic obligations without overwhelming the partner who values independence. This can be done by helping each partner articulate what feels non‐negotiable versus flexible and supporting them in co‐creating a schedule or boundary that respects both cultural values.

Because these moderately collectivistic values can significantly influence how individuals approach their relationships, with implications for communication, decision‐making, and family roles, therapists might begin by exploring family and cultural narratives through open‐ended questions such as, “Can you describe how your family of origin has shaped your expectations for your marriage?” and “What cultural traditions or values do you feel are most important to your relationship?” Such inquiries help reveal how each partner's background influences their relational dynamics (Quek 2009; Knudson‐Martin and Mahoney 2009).

It is equally important for therapists to avoid rigid assumptions. Rather than presuming that all Eastern men or Western women conform to a single cultural profile or pathologizing cultural differences, clinicians should validate both the collectivistic strengths (e.g., mutual support and “we‐consciousness”) and the need for individual autonomy (Quek and Knudson‐Martin 2008).

Finally, clinicians are encouraged to reflect on their own cultural experiences and potential biases. Although our study did not include specific measures of gender or gender‐related values, therapists should remain sensitive to possible differences in gender experiences and consider these factors when interpreting couple dynamics.

Conclusion

This study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of marital satisfaction in intercultural relationships by examining Eastern men–Western women couples through the lens of cultural orientation, social support, and demographic factors. The findings show that education and collectivism were significant predictors of marital satisfaction when cultural orientation was examined alone, and it was specifically horizontal collectivism, rooted in egalitarian interdependence, that played the most significant role. However, when social support was included in the model, it emerged as the strongest predictor. In contrast, individualism and most other demographic variables did not significantly influence satisfaction. The findings also point to the need for future studies that include more diverse intercultural pairings because the current sample, which was limited to Eastern men married to Western women, cannot show whether these patterns hold across other relational constellations. Examining Eastern women married to Western men, for example, would complement the present study and help clarify how the intersection of gender, culture, and social support may function differently across pairings.
