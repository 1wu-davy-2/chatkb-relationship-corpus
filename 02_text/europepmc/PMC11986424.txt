ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhen an individual in a relationship is coping with low desire/arousal, both partners experience difficulties, including higher levels of sexual distress. Effective emotional regulation strategies, such as self-compassion, could have individual and interpersonal benefits for couples with sexual interest/arousal disorder (SIAD).AimUsing a dyadic design, we examined the role of self-compassion in the sexual distress of couples coping with SIAD, accounting for overall relationship satisfaction. The associations were analyzed cross-sectionally and prospectively. We also explored the moderating roles of having at least one member identify as sexually or gender diverse within the couple in the potential associations between self-compassion and sexual distress.MethodsIndividuals with SIAD and their partners (N = 263 couples) completed online measures at baseline and 6 months later.OutcomesIndividuals with SIAD and their partners completed the Sexual Distress Scale-Short Form and the Couples Satisfaction Index.ResultsControlling for relationship satisfaction, when individuals coping with SIAD and their partners reported greater self-compassion at baseline, they also reported lower sexual distress. For partners only, greater self-compassion was associated with the person with SIAD’s lower levels of sexual distress at baseline. No statistically significant prospective associations were found between levels of self-compassion and sexual distress 6 months later. Belonging to a sexually diverse couple was a significant moderator of the cross-sectional associations between partners’ level of self-compassion and their own level of sexual distress at baseline, such that the link between greater self-compassion and lower sexual distress was observed only in partners belonging to a sexually diverse couple. Belonging to a gender diverse couple did not moderate any of the associations.Clinical ImplicationsFindings indicate that self-compassion could be a promising tool to reduce sexual distress in couples coping with SIAD.Strengths & LimitationsStrengths of this study include the large, diverse sample of couples coping with SIAD, the use of a cross-sectional and prospective design, and the examination of the moderating role of belonging to a gender or sexually diverse couple. However, the possible lack of statistical power to measure moderation based on belonging to a gender diverse couple and the recall bias due to the use of self-report questionnaires should be considered when interpreting the results.ConclusionGreater self-compassion is associated with lower sexual distress in both partners of couples coping with SIAD. Self-compassion may be particularly beneficial for partners of individuals with SIAD belonging to a sexually diverse couple.

BODY
Introduction

Sexual interest/arousal disorder (SIAD)1 is the most common sexual dysfunction in women, and one of the most frequent reasons for seeking sex and couple therapy.1,2 In fact, an epidemiological study revealed that 39% of American women experienced low sexual desire and 26% experienced low sexual arousal,3 whereas 40% of Canadian women reported low sexual desire in the last 6 months.4 Importantly, both women with SIAD and their partners report significantly higher levels of sexual distress than couples without sexual dysfunction.5

The SIAD diagnosis is characterized by low sexual desire and/or low arousal over a period of at least 6 months and is accompanied by clinically significant sexual distress.6 Given that sexual distress is an integral component of the diagnosis of SIAD and a key motivation for seeking help,7 it has been argued that targeting sexual distress should be a key focus in the treatment of individuals and couples with SIAD.8 Previous research on SIAD and sexual desire has primarily focused on understanding how individual characteristics, such as biomedical and psychological correlates, may influence sexual desire.9-11 However, recent research and theoretical conceptualizations have highlighted the interpersonal nature of sexual dysfunction and SIAD.12-15 Studies that have included partners in the context of SIAD—although limited in number, primarily cross-sectional and conducted among mostly cisgender-heterosexual couples—have demonstrated cross-partner effects, providing support for its understanding from an interpersonal perspective.16-18 More specifically, an emerging theoretical model suggests that couples’ emotion regulation is a major interpersonal determinant of sexuality outcomes among those coping with women’s sexual dysfunction, including SIAD.15 Self-compassion, often conceptualized as an emotion regulation strategy,19 has been associated with supportive behaviors in romantic relationships, as well as lower sexual distress in clinical couples.20-22 Given the significance of sexual distress in couples with SIAD, self-compassion may help them to co-regulate their emotions (ie, addressing their own distress while also enhancing their capacity to remain receptive and supportive toward their partner). The primary objective of the present study was to assess the cross-sectional and prospective associations between self-compassion and sexual distress in couples coping with SIAD. Furthermore, given the lack of knowledge about the unique experiences of 2S/LGBTQIA+ (Two-Spirit2, Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, Queer or Questioning, Intersex, Asexual, and additional sexual orientations and gender identities) individuals in this context, we examined how the potential links between self-compassion and sexual distress in SIAD couples differed based on the presence of at least one individual identifying as sexually or gender diverse within the couple.

Emotion regulation and self-compassion

According to the Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Model of Women’s Sexual Dysfunction, both partners’ adaptive emotional regulation strategies, such as self-compassion, could lead to lower levels of sexual and psychological distress, as well as higher levels of relationship satisfaction.15 Accordingly, a recent dyadic, mixed-gender, cross-sectional study among 71 couples coping with SIAD showed that fewer difficulties regulating negative emotions and use of more adaptative emotion regulation strategies (eg, more emotion reappraisal, less emotion suppression) were linked to lower levels of depression, anxiety, sexual distress, as well as higher levels of relationship satisfaction in partners.17 While the study suggests a link between fewer emotion regulation difficulties, some specific emotion regulation strategies, and positive sexual, relationship, and psychological outcomes in couples with SIAD, further research using a prospective design and a large, diversified sample (ie, sexually and gender diverse couples) is needed to better understand how other emotion regulation strategies, such as self-compassion, are associated with lower sexual distress in both partners.

Self-compassion integrates the following three components: (1) self-kindness (ie, being kind and understanding toward oneself in instances of pain or failure rather than being harshly self-critical), (2) common humanity (ie, perceiving one’s experiences as part of the larger human experience rather than seeing them as separating and isolating), and (3) mindfulness (ie, holding painful thoughts and feelings in balanced awareness rather than over-identifying with them).22 In romantic partnerships, a dyadic study has shown that when individuals had higher levels of self-compassion, their partner described them as being significantly more caring and accepting toward them.22 Additionally, results of a dyadic cross-sectional study showed that positive partner responses to low desire in women with SIAD, such as care or support, were associated with lower levels of sexual distress in both partners.18 A dyadic cross-sectional study of self-compassion in heterosexual couples experiencing another female sexual dysfunction, genio-pelvic pain, showed that when partners of women with pain had higher levels of self-compassion, both they and the women with pain experienced lower sexual distress.20 Furthermore, a recent study among couples who were expecting a child and couples of new parents, an often vulnerable phase in couples’ sexuality, reported that self-compassion was negatively associated with sexual distress during pregnancy.23 Although self-compassion may be a relevant treatment target for couples with sexual dysfunction, previous studies have been solely cross-sectional, limiting our understanding of its role over time. Considering the similarities between genito-pelvic pain and SIAD (ie, the dyadic context, the impacts on the sexual relationship, the intimate circumstances in which they manifest), along with the fact that people suffering from genito-pelvic pain frequently struggle with low sexual desire,24 self-compassion could serve as an adaptive emotion regulation strategy for couples coping with low sexual desire/arousal, leading to decreased sexual distress.

Indeed, self-compassion, through its mindfulness component, could enhance the connection with one’s own physical and emotional sensations and thus, facilitate detachment from negative thoughts or emotions related to sexuality. A study conducted among women with low sexual desire/arousal showed that following 4, 90-minute sessions of mindfulness-based therapy, women had higher sexual desire, sexual arousal, lubrication, sexual satisfaction, and general sexual function, compared to a control group.25 Additionally, self-compassion was reported to be a significant mediator of reduced sexual distress in women with SIAD who had participated in psychoeducation and mindfulness-based therapy groups.26 Despite the documented benefits of self-compassion in romantic relationships,22 and the evidence of increased sexual distress and sexual difficulties in partners of women with SIAD,5 the benefits of self-compassion for these couples remains to be examined.

Sexual orientation and gender identity

While some studies have reported differences between cisgender/heterosexual individuals and sexually and/or gender diverse individuals regarding their sexual and romantic relationships (eg, emotional jealousy levels in relationship, emotion and sexual satisfaction in casual sex contexts),27–29 other studies have pointed out similarities (eg, perceived relationship quality, valuing of lifelong commitment, love and fidelity).28,30–32 Considering the emerging and increasing body of literature conceptualizing sexual dysfunction as intrinsically interpersonal, it seems possible that the interpersonal differences in gender and sexually diverse couples may modulate how they experience sexual desire/arousal and associated sexual distress. Moreover, heteronormativity has been argued to influence sexual desire in women.12 However, little is known concerning how couples who may be less influenced by traditional gender roles (ie, same-gender couples, gender diverse couples) may experience sexual dysfunction and distress.

A meta-analysis on the role of self-compassion among gender and sexually diverse individuals reported that higher levels of self-compassion were associated with lower levels of depression, anxiety, psychological distress, suicidal ideation, internalized homophobia/transphobia, stigma, and with higher levels of well-being, outness, and social support.33 Given the promising role of self-compassion among individuals from the sexual and gender diversity, it seems relevant to examine the potential moderating effect of belonging to a gender or sexually diverse couple in the hypothesized associations between self-compassion and sexual distress among couples coping with SIAD.

Aims

Using a dyadic, cross-sectional, and prospective design, the present study aimed to document the associations between each partner’s self-compassion at baseline and their sexual distress, both concurrently and at 6-month follow-up, among couples in which a woman or individual assigned female at birth was diagnosed with SIAD. We hypothesized that individuals with SIAD and partners who reported higher levels of self-compassion would report lower levels of their own sexual distress, and that their partners would also report lower sexual distress, both at baseline and 6 months later. The study also aimed to explore the moderating role of belonging to a gender or sexually diverse couple in the potential associations between self-compassion and sexual distress. We did not have specific hypotheses about whether different sexual or gender identities (eg, being gay, lesbian, transwomen, non-binary) showing different effects. We aimed to explore how being in a couple where at least one member identifies as sexually or gender diverse may moderate associations. Given the reported negative association between relationship satisfaction and sexual distress in individuals and couples with sexual difficulties, including low sexual desire/arousal,34–36 all actor effect analyses controlled for relationship satisfaction.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sample of 292 couples from Canada and the United States met eligibility criteria and were recruited to take part in our study from November 2020 to May 2022. Recruitment was conducted primarily through online advertisements (eg, Facebook, Instagram) to the community. Participants were eligible if one member of the couple, who identified as a woman or was assigned female at birth, presented with low sexual desire/arousal and met the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for SIAD. Both members of the couple had to meet all of the following criteria: be 18 years of age or older, fluent in English or French, have been in a committed relationship for at least one year, had at least four face-to-face contacts per week with their partner in the last month, not currently pregnant or actively planning to become pregnant within the next year, breastfeeding, or have given birth less than a year ago, and not currently in treatment for SIAD or planning to start a treatment within the next year. Among the 603 couples who completed eligibility screening, 209 (34.7%) were ineligible (n = 127) or declined (n = 82) after the screening call. After completing the clinical interview, 81 (13.4%) did not meet SIAD criteria, 7 (1.2%) were ineligible for other reasons and 14 (2.32%) declined post-interview. Of the 292 couples enrolled in the study, two couples (0.68%) failed the baseline attention test questions and there were 27 couples (9.25%) in which at least one partner declined the baseline survey, resulting in a final sample of 263 couples at baseline.

At 6-month follow-up, of the 263 couples who completed baseline, 14 (5.32%) were deemed ineligible (11 couples separated and three were pregnant). Because of the separation and the pregnancy, those couples’ data could not be estimated using the missing-at-random assumption, and were thus excluded from the prospective analyses, resulting in a final sample of 249 potential couples for analyses involving the 2 time points.

On average, participants with SIAD, either female bodied or identifying as a woman, were 34.16 years old (SD = 9.95), while their partners were 35.71 years old (SD = 10.56). The couples had been in a relationship for an average of 9.07 years (SD = 7.60) and low desire/arousal had been present in their lives for an average of 7.09 years (SD = 7.70). The majority of the individuals within our sample identified as heterosexual, accounting for 66.2% of the participants with SIAD and 78.7% of their partners. As for gender identity, 90.9% of participants with SIAD and 87.5% of partners identified as cisgender. Sociodemographic characteristics of the sample at baseline are outlined in Table 1.

Procedure

This study was part of a larger prospective bicentric study conducted at Université de Montréal and Dalhousie University. The larger study design included 3 time points of data collection (baseline, 6-month, and 12-month follow-up) along with 56 daily diary surveys completed after the baseline. Three previous publications used the data from the baseline.16,37,38 The present study included the baseline and the 6-month follow-up surveys. Interested participants were invited to contact both research teams by email and were then contacted by a research assistant for a short screening interview by phone. If the interested participant met the basic eligibility criteria and confirmed both partners’ interest in participating in the study, they were then offered to take part in a semi-structured clinical interview, conducted via phone or the Zoom platform by a member of the research team who had been trained in order to determine the concordance between the experience of low desire/arousal and the DSM-5 SIAD diagnostic criteria.6 Individuals with low desire and/or arousal meeting DSM-5 SIAD diagnostic criteria were sent a consent form via the secure online platform Qualtrics before participating in the semi-structured clinical interview, while their partners completed the consent form immediately before starting the first survey. After confirming couples’ eligibility through the clinical interview, each partner received the first survey link by email, to be completed individually on the secure online survey platform Qualtrics (T1). Reminder emails were sent to participants who did not complete the survey after 1 and 3 weeks. Six months after the baseline survey completion, participants received the 6-month follow-up survey (T2). When contacted for this follow-up, out of the 263 couples, 225 (85.55%) couples still met eligibility criteria and had at least one partner complete Time 2. Each member of the couple received a $15 CAD compensation after the completion of the baseline survey and $18 CAD for the 6-month follow-up survey completion. The present study was approved by the institutional ethical review boards of the two universities involved.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics, sexual orientation, and gender identity status

Participants were asked to report sociodemographic information such as their relationship length, duration of SIAD, education level, income, and the culture they identify with using investigator-derived questions informed by best practices. Gender identity was measured following prior recommendations at the time of study design39,40 with the following item: “Which best describes your current gender (ie, today, that is, for the purposes of this survey)? (Please select all that apply.)”. The answer options were “Man”; “Woman”; “Indigenous (eg, Two-Spirit)3 or other cultural gender identity (eg, Fa’afafine)”; “Non-binary (eg, genderfluid, genderqueer)”; and “I identify my gender as something else (with specification)”. Following best practices at the time of study design, participants’ sexual orientation, was assessed using the following question40,41: “People describe their sexual orientation in different ways. Which expression best describes your current sexual orientation? If no expression describes yourself, check ‘Not listed’ and write the answer that describes you personally”. Responses options were: “Bisexual”; “Asexual”; “Gay”; “Lesbian”; “Straight/Heterosexual”; “Pansexual”; “Queer”; “I have not figured out my sexual orientation or are in the process of figuring it out (Questioning)”; or “Not listed (with specification)”.

Self-compassion

An adaptation of the 12-item State Self-Compassion (SCS-SF)42 measure was used to assess levels of self-compassion in both partners at baseline. The SCS-SF originally comprised six positively phrased items and six negatively phrased items. A recent factor analysis study concerning the long forms of the SCS suggested that the positive and negative items represent distinct factors, namely self-compassion and self-criticism.43 Consequently, only the six positive items were chosen to measure self-compassion within our sample. Participants were asked to rate each item on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). All items referred to one of the three different facets of self-compassion (Self-Kindness, Common Humanity and Mindfulness) and participants had to rate how often they behaved according to the items (eg, “I tried to be understanding and patient towards those aspects of my personality I don’t like”). The mean of all items was computed to produce a self-compassion total score, where higher scores indicate higher levels of self-compassion. López and colleagues demonstrated very good internal consistency for the SCS, including the 12 positive items of the original scale (α = 0.86).43 Internal consistency in the present study was also satisfactory for both partners at baseline (αT1_SIADindividuals = 0.85; αT1_Partners = 0.84).

Sexual distress

Sexual distress was measured at baseline and 6-month follow-up using the 5-item Sexual Distress Scale-Short Form (SDS -SF).44 Participants indicated how frequently on a 5-point scale (0 = never, 4 = always), they experienced negative emotions about their sex lives (eg, frustration, stress). Total scores ranged from 0 to 20 where higher scores indicated higher levels of sexual distress. The measure has been shown to have both excellent reliability and validity for men (ω = 0.93) and women (ω = 0.88) in past research,44 and within the current sample at 2 time points, both for the individual with SIAD and their partner (αT1_SIADindividuals = 0.86; αT2_ SIADindividuals = 0.91; αT1_Partners = 0.87; αT2_Partners = 0.88).

Relationship satisfaction

The Couples Satisfaction Index (CSI), a 4-item scale, was used to assess levels of relationship satisfaction within the sample.45 Participants provided ratings on various aspects of their relationship, such as their overall happiness and satisfaction or the level of comfort with the partner (eg, “I have a warm and comfortable relationship with my partner”). Total scores could range from 0 to 21 and higher scores indicated higher levels of relationship satisfaction. The Couples Satisfaction Index was used as a control variable in the present study. This measure has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties (α = 0.94), 45 including in the present study (αT1_SIADindividuals = 0.93; αT1_Partners = 0.92).

Data analytic strategy

Descriptive analyses, normality (ie, skewness and kurtosis values) and correlational analyses were conducted using SPSS 28 to outline sample characteristics and examine correlations among the study variables. In order to investigate associations between self-compassion and sexual distress while taking into account levels of relationship satisfaction, an actor–partner interdependence model (APIM)46 was used in cross-sectional and prospective analyses conducted in Mplus version 8.10.47 The APIM analyses were conducted to investigate the influence of both individuals with SIAD and their partners on their own scores’ (actor effect), while controlling for partner effects, and on their partners’ scores (partner effect), controlling for actor effects.46 The hypothesized associations between self-compassion and sexual distress were first examined cross-sectionally, and then prospectively while controlling for sexual distress levels at baseline. Considering that couples in our sample always included one member with SIAD, our dyads were considered distinguishable.48 Figure 1 illustrates the APIM model tested, both cross-sectionally and prospectively. All variables were standardized for main analyses including moderations.

Actor-Partner Interdependence Model (APIM) showing hypothesized associations between selfcompassion and sexual distress, controlling for relationship satisfaction.

Two dichotomous variables were created in order to measure the potential moderating role of belonging to a gender diverse couple (0 = couple in which both partners identify as cisgender man or woman versus 1 = couple in which at least one partner identifies as gender diverse) and belonging to a sexually diverse couple (0 = couple in which both partners identify as heterosexual vs 1 = couple in which at least one partner identifies as sexually diverse) on the tested associations. More specifically, when at least one partner identified as sexually diverse within the couple, they were classified as a sexually diverse couple and when at least one member of the couple identified as gender diverse, they were classified as a gender diverse couple.

Missing values were handled using the full information maximum likelihood approach. Following Kline’s recommendations, the overall model fit was evaluated using various fit indices; the chi-square value, the comparative fit index (CFI), the root mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA), and the standardized root-mean square residual (SRMR).49 A good fit was defined as the chi-square value not reaching statistical significance, a CFI value of 0.90 or greater, and RMSEA and SRMR values below 0.08. Relationship satisfaction, measured by the Couples Satisfaction Index45 was included as a control variable given its documented association with sexual distress. Only the effects of one’s own relationship satisfaction on one’s own sexual distress (actor effect) were estimated. Age of partners, relationship length and SIAD length were not significantly associated with sexual distress beyond 0.30 and hence were not controlled for in the analyses.

At baseline, belonging to a sexually diverse couple was a significant moderator of the association between self-compassion and sexual distress, but only for partners. More specifically, the link between greater self-compassion and lower sexual distress was present only for partners from sexually diverse couples (simple slope for sexually diverse couples: β = -0.42, P < .001, simple slope for heterosexual couples: β = -0.10, P = .154). The absence of prospective associations remained unchanged despite the addition of belonging to a sexually diverse couple as a moderator. Belonging to a gender diverse couple did not moderate the cross-sectional or prospective associations between self-compassion and sexual distress, either for individuals with SIAD or their partners.

Discussion

The main goal of this dyadic study was to examine the cross-sectional and prospective associations between self-compassion and sexual distress among a diverse sample of couples coping with SIAD, a population in which sexual distress has been well documented.5,35 As expected, findings showed negative cross-sectional associations between each partner’s self-compassion and their own sexual distress. Greater partners’ self-compassion was also associated with lower sexual distress in individuals coping with SIAD. However, contrary to our hypotheses, self-compassion at baseline did not predict sexual distress 6 months later. As a second and exploratory aim, the present study investigated the potential moderating effect of belonging to a gender or sexually diverse couple on these associations. At baseline, being part of a couple where at least one member identified as sexually diverse was a significant moderator of the association between self-compassion and sexual distress among partners only, whereby the association was significant among partners of sexually diverse couples but not among partners of heterosexual couples. No significant moderation based on belonging to a sexually diverse couple was observed prospectively. Being in a couple where at least one member did not identify as cisgender did not moderate the associations, neither at baseline nor over time. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the links between self-compassion and sexual distress in a large and diverse sample of couples coping with SIAD. Results extend our understanding of the beneficial role of self-compassion among couples coping with SIAD, both at the individual and interpersonal levels, and support the Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Model of women’s sexual dysfunction.15

Self-compassion and sexual distress

The cross-sectional and negative associations between each partner’s self-compassion and their own sexual distress is consistent with findings among couples coping with genito-pelvic pain and in transition to parenthood.20,23 As sexual dysfunctions are characterized by their private and sometimes socially taboo nature, they may be less likely to be discussed between romantic partners, with loved ones or even with healthcare professionals, limiting the possibility of receiving support and thereby facilitating emotional regulation.15,50,51 Indeed, considering this possibility of reduced external validation, using self-compassion could possibly compensate by providing kindness to oneself and helping individuals to represent sexual difficulties as part of a human and shared reality, thus reducing sexual distress in both women/individuals with SIAD and their partners. For individuals with SIAD in particular, self-compassion could alleviate the recurring feelings of guilt and inadequacy as a romantic partner, which are commonly experienced by people affected by sexual desire disorders.52 Moreover, as self-compassion is associated with a greater willingness to help someone in need,53 it may help the partners gain a sense of agency, and therefore mitigate distressing feelings, such as despair about the couple’s sex life.

In line with the Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Model of women’s sexual dysfunction,15 the cross-sectional dyadic results also highlighted the interpersonal associations between an adaptive emotion regulation strategy, self-compassion, and the sexual distress of couples coping with SIAD, above and beyond the effect of relationship satisfaction. Whereas the partner’s self-compassion was associated with lower sexual distress in the individual with SIAD, no association was found between the self-compassion of the individual with SIAD and their partner’s sexual distress. These findings can be understood in line with various studies showing that self-compassion can modify attitudes and behaviors not only towards oneself but also towards others.22,53,54 For example, self-compassion was associated with less individual distress in response to someone else’s difficulty.53 In the present study, it is possible that partners with higher levels of self-compassion would feel less affected by their partner’s low sexual desire, which may make them more inclined to offer support. Accordingly, results from a cross-sectional study among a community sample of couples showed that when individuals had higher levels of self-compassion in romantic relationships, they reported more supportive and caring behaviors towards their partner.22 Considering the high and frequent levels of guilt experienced by individuals coping with SIAD,55 they may feel more secure in the context of lower levels of distress in their partner, as well as reassured by the support and care provided by their partner. This is also consistent with the results of a cross-sectional study among couples coping with SIAD, reporting that when women with SIAD perceived positive affective responses to SIAD from their partner, they had higher sexual and relationship satisfaction and lower anxiety.18

The absence of significant prospective associations between self-compassion and sexual distress was surprising considering the documented prospective associations between self-compassion and well-being both in community samples56 and clinical populations.57,58 However, our study is the first, to our knowledge, to investigate the role of self-compassion over time in a diverse population of couples coping with sexual dysfunction. Findings may suggest that self-compassion could work as a proximal emotion regulation strategy when related to sexual dysfunction. For example, it is possible that using self-compassion in response to unpleasant emotions, such as guilt linked to SIAD symptoms, can help regulate emotions in the present moment, but less so 6 months later given the multitude of other contributing factors to this future experience. Importantly, considering the crucial role of a satisfying sexual life in romantic relationships,59–61 self-compassion may not compensate for long-term distress associated with SIAD and desire discrepancies between partners.62 For instance, despite self-kindness, mindfulness of emotions, and a sense of common humanity, partners may worry about the impact of long-term sexual difficulties on their relationship.

Belonging to a sexually or gender diverse couple as moderators of the relation between self-compassion and sexual distress

Consistent with several studies highlighting the particularly positive influence of self-compassion among individuals who identify as sexually or gender diverse,33 belonging to a sexually diverse couple was found to be a significant moderator of the association between self-compassion and sexual distress, but only for partners. The association between greater self-compassion and lower sexual distress in the partners was significant only when one or both member(s) of the couple identified as sexually diverse. This result can be understood in line with the minority stress theory, which suggests that the repeated stigma and prejudice faced by 2S/LGBTQIA+ individuals based on their sexual and gender minority identity can have specific and long-term consequences on their physical and mental health.63 Notably, these specific stressors based on stigma and discrimination are related to an increase in emotion dysregulation,64 as well as shame and self-criticism.65 For instance, in couples where at least one partner identifies as sexually diverse, partners of individuals coping with SIAD may experience higher levels of emotion dysregulation, and therefore sexual distress, in response to their partner’s low desire. Perhaps in this context, self-compassion, through mindfulness of their unpleasant emotions and self-kindness, may be particularly beneficial in limiting and containing emotional overload in partners.

The lack of moderation for individuals with SIAD based on belonging to a sexually diverse couple can possibly be explained by some similarities in the individuals with SIAD and the 2S/LGBTQIA+ community. Indeed, it is possible that the traits that make self-compassion particularly beneficial for sexually and gender diverse individuals, such as higher levels of self-criticism, are also present in individuals experiencing sexual dysfunction.55,66,67 Those similarities could imply that self-compassion might reduce sexual distress of individuals with SIAD, regardless of the couples’ minority status. As for the absence of moderation based on belonging to a gender diverse couple at baseline, although surprising, it may be attributed to insufficient statistical power due to the lower proportion of couples where one of the partners identified as gender diverse.

Unsurprisingly, the introduction of belonging to a gender or sexually diverse couple as moderating variables did not affect the results of the prospective associations between our variables. These results may reinforce the argument suggesting that self-compassion might help regulate emotions experienced in the present moment but may not alleviate the enduring effects of SIAD on couples’ sexual distress over time,62 regardless of their sexual orientation and gender identity. All moderation results based on belonging to a gender or sexually diverse couple should be interpreted with caution, considering the limited sample size, which may have reduced the statistical power of our analyses.

Strengths, limitations, and futures studies

To our knowledge, this study was the first to investigate the role of self-compassion in the experience of sexual distress of couples coping with SIAD, in addition to being anchored in the Interpersonal Emotion Regulation Model of women’s sexual dysfunction.15 The use of a cross-sectional and prospective design, accounting for relationship satisfaction, helped broaden our understanding of how self-compassion can specifically benefit couples coping with SIAD, especially in a proximal timeframe. The inclusion of both partners and dyadic analyses were a strength of this study, given the calls for interpersonal conceptualizations of sexual dysfunctions and dyadic research.15,68 Our sample was inclusive in terms of sexually and gender diverse individuals, with 28% of our participants identifying as sexually diverse and 5% as gender diverse. Moreover, the moderation analyses extended the breadth of knowledge on the positive associations with self-compassion for couples from the 2S/LGBTQIA+ community coping with sexual dysfunction.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting its results. While our sample included more gender diverse participants than population-based estimates,69 the number of couples with at least one member who identifies as gender diverse may still have not been enough to detect statistically significant differences from the cisgender couples. The number of individuals in each sexual or gender identity group (eg, gay, lesbian, queer, non-binary, trans) did not allow for sufficient statistical power to measure differences between the groups, thus preventing us from understanding the nuances and differences that may exist between the groups. Moreover, as the measurement of our variables involved recalling states of self-compassion and sexual distress over the last month from memory, it may be subject to recall bias. Finally, future studies could employ a daily diary design to verify and potentially strengthen this interpretation, while also mitigating recall bias by assessing the impact of self-compassion on day-to-day levels of sexual distress.70

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, therapy including a focus on self-compassion, such as the acceptance and commitment therapy approach71 and the Compassion-Focused Therapy72 – both shown to effectively increase self-compassion in clinical populations – may be considered when treating couples coping with SIAD. Emphasizing the conscious use of self-compassion before, during, or after sexual interactions, rather than aiming to increase overall levels of self-compassion may be considered. Additionally, clinicians can encourage the use of practical exercises, such as self-compassion journals, which patients can complete at home when feeling distressed about sex.73 These exercises can then be discussed in therapy to help tailor their utility to specific individual and couple situations. Clinicians should also consider including both partners in treatment,15 as increasing self-compassion may reinforce partners’ ability to remain supportive and accepting towards the couples’ sexual difficulties, which in turn, may help the individual with SIAD cope with sexual distress.72

Conclusions

Results underscore the role of self-compassion in the sexual distress experienced by couples coping with SIAD. At the cross-sectional level, both participants coping with SIAD and their partners’ greater self-compassion were associated with their own lower sexual distress, and partners’ greater self-compassion was also linked with the individual with SIAD’s lower sexual distress. Findings reinforce the interpersonal conceptualization of sexual dysfunction and emphasize the importance of including partners in research and clinical care. Alongside the absence of prospective associations, results suggest that self-compassion could function as a proximal emotion regulation strategy in the context of sexual dysfunction and distress. Lastly, results from the moderation based on belonging to a sexually diverse couple highlight the potentially unique benefits of self-compassion among couples where at least one member is from the 2S/LGBTQIA+ community, within the context of SIAD. Findings suggest that clinicians should assess and target self-compassion in couples coping with SIAD, while also emphasizing the importance of involving both partners when treating sexual dysfunction.
