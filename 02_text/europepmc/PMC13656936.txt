ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress urinary incontinence (SUI) is the most common subtype of urinary incontinence among women, with reported prevalence ranging from approximately 15% to 35% depending on population and diagnostic criteria, and it impairs sexual function through physiological and psychological mechanisms. Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) addresses anatomical deficits but not psychosocial barriers such as intimacy avoidance and performance anxiety. Sensate focus, a psychobehavioral technique, reduces performance demand and reconnects couples with pleasurable bodily sensations. This trial evaluated whether combining sensate focus with PFMT yields superior improvement in sexual function versus PFMT alone in married women with SUI.MethodsIn this parallel-group, superiority randomized controlled trial across private gynecology clinics in Tehran and Qom, Iran, 60 married women (18–49 years) with clinically confirmed SUI were randomized 1:1 to combined sensate focus plus PFMT (n=30) or PFMT alone (n=30). Primary outcome was FSFI score change at baseline, 1, and 2 months; secondary outcome was pelvic floor muscle (PFM) strength (Modified Oxford Grading Scale). Outcome assessment was not blinded.ResultsTotal FSFI score rose from 16.97±4.21 to 30.68±0.54 with combined treatment but fell from 20.74±4.74 to 16.53±3.20 with PFMT alone (mean change from baseline +13.7 vs −4.2 points), an adjusted between-group difference in change of 17.8 points (95% CI 16.2–19.5; p<0.001), exceeding the minimum clinically important difference (8–10 points). PFM strength improved more with combined treatment, adjusted difference 1.21 grades (95% CI 0.97–1.46; p<0.001).ConclusionIn this single-center trial of 60 women followed for two months, combining sensate focus with PFMT was associated with clinically meaningful improvement in sexual function and PFM strength beyond PFMT alone. Given the limited sample size, single-center design, and short follow-up, this combined approach appears a promising adjunct to standard urogynecological care, pending confirmation in larger, multicenter trials with longer follow-up.Trial RegistrationIRCT20240821062824N1 (Registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on 2025-02-24).Keywords: stress urinary incontinence, sexual dysfunction, sensate focus technique, pelvic floor muscle training, randomized controlled trial

BODY
Introduction

Sexual health is a fundamental component of physical, emotional, and social well-being, intrinsically linked to quality of life rather than merely the absence of disease or dysfunction.1,2 Despite its clinical importance, female sexual dysfunction—encompassing persistent difficulties across one or more phases of the sexual response cycle—remains significantly underdiagnosed and undertreated.3 Epidemiological evidence indicates that up to 51% of reproductive-age women experience some form of sexual dysfunction, with prevalence rates markedly escalating in the presence of concurrent lower urinary tract conditions.4,5 Stress urinary incontinence (SUI), defined as the involuntary leakage of urine during physical exertion or increased intra-abdominal pressure, is the most prevalent form of urinary incontinence, with reported prevalence ranging from approximately 15% to 35% depending on the population studied and diagnostic criteria used.6–8 Beyond its direct physiological morbidity and economic burdens, SUI exerts a profound, bidirectional impact on intimate relationships and sexual function.9 The anatomical proximity of the bladder, urethra, and reproductive structures, combined with the psychological sequelae of anticipated involuntary leakage, frequently results in diminished sexual desire, reduced coital frequency, and active avoidance of intimacy due to severe performance anxiety.10 The pathophysiology of SUI involves complex anatomical and neuromuscular deficits, primarily inadequate urethral support driven by pelvic floor muscle weakness.11 Consequently, pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) serves as the first-line conservative intervention, demonstrating robust efficacy in restoring biomechanical integrity and reducing symptom severity.12 However, SUI-related sexual dysfunction is multifactorial; while PFMT effectively addresses muscular weakness, it fails to mitigate the accompanying psychosocial dimensions, such as intimacy avoidance and diminished somatic awareness.13 The sensate focus technique, a foundational psychobehavioral intervention, addresses these psychological barriers by systematically reducing performance demand, cultivating mindfulness, and reconnecting couples to pleasurable bodily sensations.14 While emerging evidence supports its utility in enhancing sexual satisfaction across various populations, its specific application in women with SUI—particularly as an adjunct to standard PFMT—remains underexplored; this gap is particularly evident in the Iranian clinical context, where PFMT protocols commonly used in practice focus primarily on muscular strengthening and largely overlook the psychological-relational dimension of SUI-related sexual dysfunction.15–19 Therefore, this parallel-group randomized controlled trial was designed to evaluate the hypothesis that an integrated intervention combining sensate focus training with PFMT will yield superior improvements in sexual function compared to PFMT alone in married women with SUI.

Methods

Trial Design and Setting

A two-arm, parallel-group, superiority randomized controlled trial was conducted in 2025 across private outpatient gynecology clinics in Tehran and Qom, Iran. The trial protocol was prospectively registered (IRCT20240821062824N1). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, received Tarbiat Modares University ethical committee approval (IR.MODARES.REC.1403.150), and necessitated written informed consent from all participants prior to enrollment.

Participants

Eligible participants comprised married women, aged 18 to 49 years, with a confirmed clinical diagnosis of stress urinary incontinence (SUI), based on standard clinical criteria (a history of involuntary urine leakage associated with increased intra-abdominal pressure, eg, coughing, sneezing, or exertion, together with a positive cough stress test), confirmed by a gynecologist.

Inclusion Criteria

Participants were required to be married, cohabiting with their spouse, sexually active (defined as having engaged in sexual intercourse within the preceding 8 weeks), literate in Persian, and of Iranian nationality. Eligibility was restricted to married women in an active, cohabiting sexual partnership because the sensate focus technique is an inherently couple-based intervention that requires a cohabiting partner for meaningful practice and evaluation. The 8-week window used to define recent sexual activity was chosen to ensure an active, ongoing sexual partnership while remaining compatible with the FSFI instrument’s own 4-week recall period.

Exclusion Criteria

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following operationally defined criteria: (1) urinary incontinence other than pure SUI, including mixed urinary incontinence (concurrent stress- and urgency-associated leakage) or predominant urgency urinary incontinence; (2) current pregnancy or delivery within the preceding 6 months; (3) natural or surgical menopause (≥12 consecutive months of amenorrhea not attributable to pregnancy, lactation, or other medical conditions, or previous bilateral oophorectomy); (4) clinically significant pelvic organ prolapse (POP-Q stage ≥ II); (5) symptomatic urinary tract or genital infection at enrollment, with eligibility for reassessment after treatment and symptom resolution; (6) a diagnosed neurological condition affecting bladder, pelvic floor, or sexual function (eg, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, spinal cord injury, stroke with residual deficit, or peripheral neuropathy); (7) uncontrolled or severe systemic disease likely to affect sexual function, physical performance, or intervention adherence (eg, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, severe cardiovascular disease, active malignancy, or advanced renal/hepatic disease); (8) a current major psychiatric disorder per DSM-5 criteria (eg, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, or a psychotic disorder), although mild psychological distress without a formal diagnosis was not exclusionary; (9) a current alcohol or substance use disorder; (10) a major stressful life event within the preceding 6 months (eg, bereavement, divorce/separation, serious illness of the participant or spouse, or major financial/occupational loss); (11) current use, or initiation within the preceding 3 months, of medications known to substantially affect sexual or pelvic floor function (eg, SSRIs/SNRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, antiandrogens, or hormonal therapies with a substantial sexual side-effect profile), whereas stable medications without a clinically relevant effect were not exclusionary; (12) previous surgery for SUI, pelvic organ prolapse, or other major pelvic floor reconstruction; (13) concurrent participation in another structured PFMT program, sex therapy, or couples therapy; and (14) any condition judged likely to substantially interfere with treatment adherence or reliable outcome assessment, including inability to complete the Persian-language questionnaires.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was powered to detect clinical superiority in the primary outcome, the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) total score. Assuming a minimum clinically important between-group difference of 8 to 10 points and a standard deviation of 4.5, derived from a directly comparable prior clinical trial of the sensate focus technique on female sexual function outcomes,20 a minimum of 27 participants per parallel arm was required to achieve 80% statistical power at a two-sided significance level of α=0.05. To accommodate an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the final enrollment target was established at 30 participants per group (N=60).

Randomization and Blinding

Following baseline assessment and confirmation of eligibility, participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention or control group. The randomization sequence was generated utilizing permuted block randomization with fixed block sizes of four. To ensure strict allocation concealment, the sequence was maintained within sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Owing to the nature of the psychobehavioral intervention, blinding of the participants and of the researcher delivering the intervention was not feasible. Baseline assessments were completed before randomization and therefore without knowledge of group allocation; however, the follow-up assessments at one and two months were performed by the same researcher, who was aware of group allocation, and the primary outcome (FSFI) is a self-reported questionnaire completed by unblinded participants. Outcome assessment was therefore unblinded. The statistician who performed the analyses was blinded to group allocation until the analyses were completed.

Study Procedures and Data Collection

Upon presentation to the outpatient clinic, eligible married women diagnosed with stress urinary incontinence (SUI) were comprehensively briefed on the study objectives, data confidentiality protocols, and the voluntary nature of their participation. Following the acquisition of written informed consent, baseline assessments were conducted. Participants completed a structured sociodemographic and reproductive history, alongside the baseline Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI). Concurrently, a trained researcher conducted an objective physical evaluation of pelvic floor muscle strength utilizing the Modified Oxford Grading Scale checklist. Furthermore, all participants were instructed to maintain a daily 24-hour urinary diary continuously until the end of the intervention period.

Interventions

Intervention Group (Combined Therapy)

Participants allocated to the intervention arm attended a single, two-hour, individualized training session conducted in a quiet and confidential clinical room. This comprehensive session included theoretical and practical instruction on sensate focus techniques and pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), facilitated through educational slides and multimedia video presentations. Participants were also provided with a step-by-step instructional booklet detailing both regimens. To ensure protocol adherence, the primary researcher conducted weekly telephone follow-ups with this cohort.

Control Group (Monotherapy)

Participants assigned to the control group received standard care instruction exclusively focusing on PFMT. To ensure ethical parity at the conclusion of the final two-month evaluation period, the control group was provided with the sensate focus educational booklet and offered an optional, complimentary in-person training session.

Follow-Up Protocol

Participants in both study arms were contacted at one and two months post-intervention to attend mandatory in-person evaluation sessions. During these visits, the FSFI and secondary outcome assessments were re-administered by the researcher. Participants were afforded the opportunity to ask questions, and further clinical explanations were provided as necessary. Contact information was exchanged between the research team and all participants to guarantee access for follow-up coordination and to address any emergent queries.

Outcome Measures

Primary Outcome

The primary clinical endpoint was the change in sexual function from baseline to one and two months post-intervention. This was quantified utilizing the 19-item Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), which comprehensively evaluates six domains: desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain. Total cumulative scores range from 2 to 36, with elevated scores denoting superior sexual function. The reliability and validity of the Persian version of the FSFI were rigorously established by Mohammadi et al (2008). The instrument demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.70). Furthermore, construct validity was confirmed by a statistically significant difference in the mean scores of the total scale and all respective domains between case and control groups (p < 0.001).21

Secondary Outcomes

Pelvic floor muscle strength was objectively graded utilizing the Modified Oxford Grading Scale. This assessment was performed by a single trained assessor throughout the study to minimize inter-rater variability. The Modified Oxford Grading Scale was selected over instrumented perinometry because it is non-invasive, requires no dedicated equipment, and is feasible in the outpatient clinical settings used for this trial. The Modified Oxford Grading Scale is a digital-palpation grading system (0–5) that was developed and validated, in part against perineometric pressure, within the PERFECT assessment scheme and is described in the International Continence Society terminology report on pelvic floor muscle assessment.22,23 Perineometry, in contrast, yields device-dependent pressure values that are influenced by probe size and placement and by co-contraction of the abdominal and gluteal muscles, is not standardized across devices, and requires an intravaginal probe and dedicated equipment that were not available in the participating clinics; it was therefore not used. Pelvic floor muscle strength should accordingly be regarded as an ordinal, examiner-graded secondary outcome, and this limitation is addressed in the Discussion. Daily urinary incontinence events were tracked via the self-reported 24-hour urinary diary. Sociodemographic and reproductive history—including age, educational attainment, duration of marriage, and parity—were systematically recorded at baseline. Finally, participants were continuously monitored for any intervention-related adverse events; to date, no adverse events have been reported in either group.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).Continuous baseline variables and clinical scores were expressed as means standard deviations (Mean± SD), and their normal distribution was verified using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Baseline homogeneity between the intervention and control groups was evaluated using the Independent Samples t-test for normally distributed continuous data, and the Chi-square (x2) or Fisher’s Exact test for categorical variables-which were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages (n, %). To assess the primary outcomes (longitudinal changes in the total FSFI score and its six subdomains) and the secondary outcome (pelvic floor muscle strength scores) across the three observational intervals (baseline, 1 month, and 2 months post-intervention), Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was executed to test the time-by-group interaction effects. Mauchly’s test indicated a violation of the sphericity assumption for all outcomes (p < 0.05); therefore, Greenhouse–Geisser-corrected results are reported, and all time-by-group interactions remained significant (p < 0.001). Furthermore, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was applied for post-intervention follow-ups to adjust for initial baseline variations, utilizing the respective baseline scores as covariates. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta-squared (ηp2) for the time-by-group interaction (repeated-measures ANOVA) and for the group effect (ANCOVA), with values of approximately 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively,24 and as mean change from baseline (with 95% confidence intervals) at one and two months in each group. P-values for baseline comparisons (Tables 1 and 2) are presented for descriptive purposes only and were not used for hypothesis testing, in line with CONSORT recommendations.25 Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of < 0.05. All 60 randomized participants completed the study and were included in an intention-to-treat analysis, with no losses to follow-up. To identify independent predictors of treatment response, exploratory multivariable linear regression analyses (Enter method) were additionally performed for change in total FSFI score and change in pelvic floor muscle strength, entering group assignment, age, duration of marriage, and duration of SUI as covariates (full models are reported in the Results section). Adverse events were monitored throughout the study via weekly telephone contact with the intervention group and in-person visits at one and two months in both groups, including open-ended questioning about any physical or psychological discomfort related to the intervention; no adverse events were reported in either group.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Sixty women with stress urinary incontinence (SUI) participated in this study (30 per group). Of 86 women assessed for eligibility, 26 were excluded (19 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 7 declined to participate), and the remaining 60 were randomized; no participant was lost to follow-up or excluded from the analysis (Figure 1). Both groups were comparable in demographic, socioeconomic, and obstetric factors, including age (Control: 32.03 ± 6.39 years; Intervention: 33.83 ± 6.72 years, p = 0.300), education, economic status, and parity (Table 1). However, the intervention group had weaker pelvic floor muscle strength at baseline (p = 0.016; Table 2). To account for this baseline imbalance, all subsequent between-group comparisons of pelvic floor muscle strength and sexual function at follow-up were adjusted using ANCOVA with the respective baseline score as a covariate. Baseline p-values (Tables 1 and 2) are shown for descriptive purposes only.

Primary Outcome: Sexual Function

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed highly significant time-by-group interactions for the total Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) and all its subdomains (p < 0.001). Despite presenting with lower baseline total FSFI scores (16.97 ± 4.21 vs 20.74 ± 4.74), the intervention group showed a statistically significant increase in total FSFI score, from 16.97 ± 4.21 at baseline to 30.68 ± 0.54 at two months post-intervention. In contrast, the control group experienced a gradual decline to 16.53 ± 3.20 over the same period. Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for baseline imbalances, confirmed the significant superiority of the combined intervention at both one and two months (p < 0.001). On multivariable regression adjusting for age, duration of marriage, and duration of SUI, group assignment remained the dominant independent predictor of change in total FSFI score, with an adjusted between-group mean difference of 17.8 points favoring the combined intervention (95% CI 16.2 to 19.5; β = −0.94, p < 0.001). The 13.7-point within-group improvement observed in the intervention arm exceeded the pre-specified minimum clinically important difference of 8–10 points, indicating a clinically meaningful effect in addition to statistical significance. This positive trajectory in the intervention arm was consistently mirrored across all individual functional domains-desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain-which all demonstrated highly significant improvements compared to the control group, in which scores declined significantly within the group in all domains except pain (where scores rose modestly, from 2.61 ± 0.86 to 3.13 ± 1.00) (p < 0.001 for all inter-group post- intervention comparisons) (Table 3).

Secondary Outcome: Pelvic Floor Muscle Strength

Evaluation of pelvic floor muscle (PFM) strength using repeated measures ANOVA revealed a highly significant time-by-group interaction effect (p < 0.001). Over the follow-up period, the intervention group showed a statistically significant increase, with mean PFM scores increasing from 2.20 ± 0.84 at baseline to 3.15 ± 0.68 at one month, and peaking at 4.00 ± 0.73 at two months post-intervention. In contrast, the control group showed a substantially less pronounced increase, moving from 2.73 ± 0.63 to 3.25 ± 0.53 over the same intervals.

To account for the initial baseline differences in muscle strength between the two study arms, a subsequent Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, adjusting for baseline values. The ANCOVA models successfully confirmed the initial findings, demonstrating a highly significant and superior treatment effect favoring the combined intervention group at both one and two months post-intervention (p < 0.001). (Table 4). On multivariable regression, group assignment and older age were independently associated with greater improvement in PFM strength, with an adjusted between-group mean difference of 1.21 grades favoring the combined intervention (95% CI 0.97 to 1.46; β = −0.78, p < 0.001). Clinically, the 1.8-grade improvement observed in the intervention group (from 2.20 to 4.00 on the Modified Oxford Grading Scale) represents a shift from the “weak” to the “moderate-to-good” category, a functionally meaningful, patient-perceptible change.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the integration of sensate focus therapy with pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) resulted in significantly greater improvements in female sexual function compared with PFMT alone among women with stress urinary incontinence (SUI). Participants receiving the combined intervention experienced clinically meaningful improvements across all domains of the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), accompanied by superior gains in pelvic floor muscle strength. These findings support the contemporary biopsychosocial model of sexual dysfunction, suggesting that the psychosexual consequences of SUI cannot be adequately addressed through biomechanical rehabilitation alone and require simultaneous targeting of both physiological and psychological mechanisms underlying sexual impairment.27–29 The 13.7-point improvement in total FSFI score observed in the intervention group substantially exceeds the commonly cited minimum clinically important difference of 8 to 10 points, indicating that this benefit is clinically, and not merely statistically, meaningful. This magnitude of improvement also appears to exceed that typically reported for PFMT-only interventions targeting sexual function in prior trials, which have generally documented smaller effect sizes on FSFI outcomes,18,19 consistent with the hypothesis that addressing the psychological and relational dimensions of SUI-related sexual dysfunction, rather than the biomechanical dimension alone, contributes additional benefit.

The superiority of the combined intervention appears to arise from the complementary mechanisms through which PFMT and sensate focus exert their therapeutic effects. PFMT improves urethral support, pelvic floor strength, and neuromuscular coordination, thereby reducing urine leakage and enhancing proprioceptive control of pelvic structures.30,31 Nevertheless, chronic SUI is frequently associated with coital incontinence anxiety, fear of embarrassment, and anticipatory concerns regarding urinary leakage during intimacy. These psychological stressors activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system, suppress parasympathetic sexual arousal pathways, and consequently impair lubrication, orgasmic responsiveness, and sexual satisfaction.6,32,33 Sensate focus directly addresses these psychobehavioral barriers through structured, non-demand touching exercises that intentionally shift attention away from performance-oriented sexual goals toward sensory awareness and pleasurable bodily experiences. By reducing performance anxiety and interrupting maladaptive cognitive patterns, sensate focus facilitates parasympathetic dominance and restores the neurophysiological conditions necessary for healthy sexual functioning.14,17

An important finding was the divergent trajectory of sexual outcomes between groups. Despite receiving evidence-based PFMT, women in the control group showed minimal improvement and deterioration in some sexual function domains. This phenomenon may reflect interoceptive hypervigilance and “spectatoring”, whereby excessive monitoring of pelvic floor performance and fear of incontinence reinforce catastrophic cognitions, sexual avoidance, and anxiety.34 Previous evidence suggests that the benefits of PFMT may plateau when psychological and relational determinants of sexual dysfunction remain untreated, highlighting the limitations of a purely somatic approach to SUI management.18,19

The marked improvement observed in the pain domain deserves particular attention. Although SUI does not directly cause genital tissue pathology, chronic anxiety and conditioned avoidance behaviors may induce pelvic floor hypertonicity and involuntary levator ani guarding, contributing to dyspareunia.35,36 The significant reduction in pain scores following sensate focus supports the hypothesis that anxiety reduction, graded sensory exposure, and restoration of positive sexual experiences can effectively reverse psychophysiological mechanisms underlying sexual pain. Furthermore, the greater improvement in pelvic floor muscle strength observed in the intervention group suggests a a plausible, though untested, hypothesis of a psychobehavioral and motor-training interventions. Reduced anxiety, enhanced body awareness, and improved attentional control could plausibly, though this was not directly measured, optimize corticomotor recruitment and motor learning processes, thereby increasing the effectiveness of PFMT and facilitating more efficient neuromuscular adaptation.26,37

From a clinical perspective, these findings challenge the traditional compartmentalization of urogynecological care, which predominantly focuses on continence outcomes while often neglecting psychosexual well-being. The structured, low-cost, and easily deliverable nature of sensate focus training, combined with the absence of adverse events, supports its feasibility for integration into routine urogynecological practice. Such an approach may substantially improve patient-centered outcomes and quality of life beyond symptom control alone.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively modest sample size and recruitment from private gynecology clinics may limit external validity and generalizability. The short follow-up period precludes conclusions regarding the long-term durability of treatment effects. Additionally, validated measures of anxiety, depression, body image, relationship satisfaction, and dyadic adjustment were not included, preventing direct examination of potential mediating pathways. Restriction of eligibility to married women in an active, cohabiting sexual partnership, while methodologically necessary for a couple-based intervention, further limits generalizability to unmarried, non-cohabiting, or same-sex partnered women. As with most behavioral intervention trials, participants, the researcher delivering the intervention, and the follow-up outcome assessor could not be blinded to group assignment, and the primary outcome relied on self-report while the secondary outcome relied on examiner-graded palpation; this raises the possibility that participant expectation or a response bias favoring the more novel, attention-intensive intervention contributed to some of the observed between-group difference. In addition, pelvic floor muscle strength was graded by digital palpation with the Modified Oxford Grading Scale rather than measured with instrumented perineometry or dynamometry; this scale is ordinal, examiner-dependent, and less sensitive to small changes in strength, and grading by a single non-blinded examiner may have introduced detection bias. The pelvic floor muscle findings should therefore be interpreted as secondary and hypothesis-generating. Future multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations, extended follow-up periods, and comprehensive psychosexual assessments are warranted to confirm these findings and clarify the mechanisms underlying treatment response.

Conclusion

The present study provides evidence that combining sensate focus therapy with pelvic floor muscle training was associated with significantly greater improvement in sexual function and pelvic floor muscle strength than PFMT alone among women with stress urinary incontinence, in this single-center trial of 60 women followed for two months. Combining a psychobehavioral technique with biomechanical training may represent a promising adjunct to standard urogynecological care for improving patient-reported sexual function and pelvic floor muscle strength in this population, pending confirmation in larger, multicenter trials with longer follow-up and blinded outcome assessment.
