ABSTRACT
AbstractSome countries are legalizing same‐sex marriage and assisted reproductive technologies (ART) for homosexual couples. One unique form of ART, ROPA (Reception of Oocytes from Partner), recently stirred up controversy in China, when a custody dispute between a female same‐sex couple who used ROPA brought this reproductive model into the public eye. Some Western scholars have argued for the legitimacy of ROPA from the perspective of autonomy and reproductive rights. Yet, these arguments do not easily translate into all cultural contexts, as this case will show. There is a need to articulate the ethical considerations of such technologies in light of local philosophical traditions. To that end, this paper will explore the permissibility of ROPA from a Confucian lens, exploring Confucian perspectives on naturalness, filial piety, family and social harmony. Accordingly, we hope to further the discussion of cross‐cultural bioethics.Keywords: Confucian ethics, family ethics, parental rights, reproductive ethics, ROPA

BODY
INTRODUCTION

Recently, an unprecedented child custody lawsuit between a Chinese lesbian couple was brought to the Huli District People's Court, located in Xiamen City of Fujian Province, China,1 showcasing a reproductive agreement never seen before in the country. The couple had used a relatively new form of assisted reproductive technology (ART) known as “Reception of Oocytes from Partner” (ROPA). ROPA was first introduced in 2010 and was unique in that it was specifically designed for lesbian couples in that it allowed two female partners a mode of joint participation in a pregnancy.2 One partner provides the genes and egg and the other partner the womb and labor, offering each a way to establish a “biological” connection with their future child. This format, however, raises many challenges that have yet to be fully explored.

Innovations in reproductive technology have often sparked controversy. Whether it is the invention of a pill for contraception or the ability to outsource pregnancy to a surrogate, technological changes in the kinds of reproductive choices available force societies to examine traditional assumptions and grapple with new possibilities of family structures. However, the bioethical literature on these subjects has thus far been centered in Western social contexts and is often informed by broadly liberal and individualistic philosophical perspectives. This recent legal dispute in China provides an excellent opportunity to widen the discussion and explore the unique ethical dimensions that emerge from the application of a technique like ROPA in a context that is rooted in very different philosophical traditions.

In this paper, we will discuss an often neglected perspective in bioethics, drawing on the Confucian ethical tradition. Through exploring Confucian perspectives on “filial piety,” “naturalness,” and “family,” we examine whether it is possible to reconcile ROPA with a Confucian ethical system. The outline is: we begin with introduction of the ROPA case in China, and then provide a brief discussion of where ROPA fits within the range of other available ART strategies, and from there proceed with an analysis of the ethical issues raised, finally exploring the ways that Confucian ethics and values illuminate some often neglected sides to these issues. That said, our primary goal in this paper is not to argue for the legitimacy or illegitimacy of ROPA, but rather to use this case to initiate a broader conversation into questions about reproductive ethics and highlight the importance of culture and context in any bioethical analysis. Arguments across cultures that do not account for differences in philosophical commitments will invariably fall on deaf ears.

Thus, with our Confucian analysis of the permissibility of ROPA as a form of ART, this article explores novel topics such as (1) Confucian philosophy's theoretical implication on LGBTQ+ identity, (2) the role of Confucian philosophy in addressing ethical controversies surrounding emerging reproductive technologies and (3) more importantly, it explores the moral standing of LGBTQ+ people in the context of kinship membership. The above‐mentioned contributions are supported by the solid study of key ideas in Confucian philosophy and substantial knowledge of relevant biotechnology. Existing studies on LGBTQ+ identity often either overly narrows its focus to individual desires and sexual relationships or the overgeneralized dimension of state membership as bearers of civic rights and obligations. By focusing on kinship, this article supplements existing discussions by focusing on communal relationships in the middle of the state and the personal.

THE LEGAL CASE

The Chinese legal case that serves as inspiration for this paper begins in 2018, when Ti and Li3 (both women) became a couple. In China, same‐sex marriage is not legal, and social stigma for gay or lesbian couples is still prevalent. Additionally, ART is legally confined to heterosexual married couples within China, meaning that Ti and Li could not legally pursue any form of it. Nevertheless, the couple sought assistance from an underground fertility clinic and was able to have Ti's eggs fertilized with sperm from an anonymous donor,4 before transferring the embryo into Li's womb. Subsequently, Li successfully delivered a baby girl in December 2019. When the baby was born only Li was listed as the biological mother on the birth certificate, while the father's name was left blank. There was no legal or official way to record Ti's biological ties to the child.

As fortune would have it, months after the baby was born, Ti and Li ended their romantic relationship. Following this break up, Li absconded with the baby without informing her former partner. She also then prohibited Ti from visiting the child or maintaining any family connection. Ti then sued Li in court over the rights to child custody, framing their prior relationship as a broken surrogacy agreement, since there was no other legal means to articulate the grievance in a Chinese court. During the first trial in July 2020, Ti lost the case to Li on the grounds that Li was the gestational mother who had given birth to the baby. The court also did not accept Ti's claims that there was a surrogacy arrangement, as Li provided sufficient evidence su their previous romantic involvement. Dissatisfied with the initial verdict, Ti appealed for a retrial. However, the original verdict of the district court was upheld by the Xiamen Intermediate People's Court in November 2021, thus officially closing this legal dispute case in accordance with China's two‐tiered judicial system (Civil Judgement, 2021).5

Not surprisingly, a flurry of discussions and debates were ignited nationwide as soon as news revolving around this legal dispute was reported. It is the first time that the parenthood dispute under ROPA procreation in China was resolved by methods other than inter‐partner negotiation, which put such a legislative gap in front of the judiciary, and verified the value position and viewpoint of the judiciary in the form of individual cases. Similar lawsuits have already been reported in other countries, such as a ROPA case involving a genetic mother of twins who sought to obtain parental rights through the Court of Appeal from Portsmouth County in 2014 when the legal mother (the woman had given birth) took the children with her during the couple's separation.6 Yet, the case of Li and Ti was the first of its kind within China.

The case raises questions about reproductive ethics, how parenthood is conceived and understood, which kinds of kinship ties are seen as legitimate, how parental rights are formed, and the importance or unimportance of genetic ties. While there is a rich corpus of philosophical work on many of these topics, the unique intersection with Chinese cultural values that this case presents has yet to be explored. Before we explore the Confucian lens, however, it is necessary to cover some aspects of the existing discussion around ROPA.

A UNIQUE REPRODUCTIVE MODEL

ROPA is distinct from other forms of ART in several important ways: (1) the individuals involved typically do not have medical indications for undergoing fertility treatment, (2) both partners seek to establish a biological connection with the future child, (3) both partners also intend to act as mothers of the child, and (4) the pregnancy and birth are kept within the partnership (in contrast to surrogacy, but similar to heterosexual couples).

Comparing ROPA with IVF, DI, GS, and MRT

The core technique of the ROPA model is built upon an existing practice of in‐vitro fertilization with embryo transfer (IVF‐ET). In heterosexual couples, IVF‐ET involves taking samples of sperm and egg from each partner, combining them to form embryos in the lab, and then implanting the embryos into the woman's womb. Typically, access to IVF‐ET is provided for couples struggling with infertility. In contrast, ROPA is designed to be used by lesbian couples, regardless of either partner's health or fertility status. Thus, the primary motivation for ROPA is the prospect of shared “biological” motherhood.

ROPA stands apart from other forms of ART like artificial insemination by donor insemination (DI). DI offers lesbian couples a chance to start a family, but without joint participation, in that the same woman who contributes the egg also carries the child. ROPA is also distinct from gestational surrogacy (GS), which allows both members of a couple (heterosexual or homosexual) to have a child without either partner undergoing pregnancy or labor. Markedly, the surrogate is in a transactional role (being paid for her services) and does not intend to form any kinship ties through the act of labor.7

Superficially, ROPA resembles another novel medical innovation, mitochondrial DNA transfer (or mitochondrial replacement technique (MRT)). MRT is primarily used to enable a couple to conceive without imparting a mitochondrial disease to their future child, but it also in theory allows for a biological and genetic connection to be established between three parties. While this treatment has its origin and justification in the avoidance of certain genetic diseases, applications of the technique for gay and lesbian families remain controversial8.

The medical status of ROPA

Several key issues need to be settled with regard to the medical status of ROPA before we can explore its intersection with Confucian values.1. Whether ROPA can be considered a valid treatment for infertility?

The most common argument against the use of ROPA is founded on the fact that most people seeking it are not typically infertile, meaning there is no medical indication for IVF‐ET. If there is no medical problem that ROPA fixes, what justifies the procedure? In the book The Goals of Medicine: The Forgotten Issues in Health Care Reform, the authors articulate four basic goals of medicine, (1) prevention of disease, (2) relief of suffering, (3) care of the ill, and (4) avoidance of premature death.9 At first glance, ROPA does not necessarily fulfill any of these (except perhaps relief of suffering if the longing for a child can be understood to cause pain). Yet our conception of medicine is not set in stone. Indeed, as Dondorp et al.10 have argued, our view of what counts as properly medical “is not only informed by biological facts, but [is just] as much a matter of social conventions and justifications” (813). If the goal of medicine in present times is no longer limited to merely curing diseases and relieving physical pain, but has rather transcended into improving people's overall well‐being and quality of life, then given that procreation is important for a person's vision of the good life, supporting this goal is plausibly within the medical purview.11

Nevertheless, detractors would say that ROPA is not a legitimate therapy since it does not fulfill any valid medical function; it does not prevent disease, it treats no illness, and has no effect on longevity. Some scholars go further than this in their arguments against ROPA, arguing that it is not only not medical, but that it should be regarded as a “bizarre reproductive scheme”12 (Dondrop et al., 2010). This argument, however, rests on the assumption that more conventional models of IVF (those already available to heterosexual couples) fit a definition of a medical treatment or therapy that ROPA would fail. When we look closely, such a definition does not seem forthcoming. First of all, there is no anatomical or physiological function that is restored by any form of ART to an infertile couple. Rather, the infertility of either one or both of the individuals is bypassed. Even after successfully conceiving a child through fertility treatments, the couple in question is still as infertile as before. Thus, it does not seem that ART's legitimacy as a medical treatment can be said to be rooted in any ability to rectify an underlying pathology or restore function.13

What truly differentiates ROPA from other ART is not that it is any more or less medical, but that the source of the infertility it treats is contextual rather than biological. A lesbian couple is not infertile because of any factor internal to either person, but from the situation of same‐sex partnership itself. Whether this should matter with regard to the question of whether ROPA can be justifiably offered as a form of ART is thus better categorized as a social rather than medical question (we will pick up on this in Section 4). Perhaps someone might question whether it is permissible to use a biological intervention for a non‐biological problem. We think the answer is still plausibly yes. For instance, it is widely permissible to prescribe Ozempic for weight loss, when it is possible for many people to manage their weight with lifestyle changes. The fact that there is a non‐pharmaceutical option doesn't make it impermissible to offer the pharmaceutical option here. Similarly, in this case, the lesbian couples could adopt, as a non‐medical means of achieving a family. Yet, for reasons we discuss later in the paper, the possibility of establishing biological kinship is attractive for certain cultural reasons.

Whether it incurs unjustifiable risks compared to other forms of ART

Even so, this is not the only factor necessary for a procedure to be medically justified. It must also generally be considered safe and effective, or at least no more risky than existing treatments. Indeed, ROPA is not risk free. For lesbian couples who choose ROPA, the giving partner risks facing ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) or other possible complications during and/or after retrieval of the oocytes; meanwhile the receiving partner faces the same range of risks associated with any gestation and/or labor. All of these risks are similar to those involved in any IVF‐ET treatment. Thus, the risk carried by ROPA does not significantly differ from other widely accepted forms of ART.

This is further supported by the results of a retrospective study of lesbian couples (n = 121) receiving ROPA treatments in the United Kingdom, where it was found that this reproductive model is safe and effective, with reassuring obstetrical and perinatal outcomes.14 Therefore, it seems ethically unreasonable to deny lesbian couples access to this treatment option on the grounds of medical risk. However, some scholars, such as Guido Pennings (2016), believe that although shared maternity might be “acceptable,” it does not mean, “that one should accept this as normal practice” (Pennings, 2016, p. 255). Likewise, T. F. Murphy15 has argued that, “the overall total risks for a lesbian couple wanting a child could, however, be reduced by relying on intrauterine insemination for one woman, and forgoing ovum donation by the other, although that approach precludes shared maternity” (1032).

It is also worth considering the issue of fair treatment. As Zeiler and Malmquist16 have pointed out, in some countries (like Sweden) there seems to be an inconsistency in how ART is regulated. In Sweden, IVF with ROPA is illegal, yet egg donor IVF for heterosexual couples is allowed and practiced, as is sperm donation for lesbian couples. This inconsistency seems to discriminate against same‐sex couples wishing to create shared biological ties, a desire which is easily fulfilled (without criticism or invalidation) by heterosexual couples. Additionally, an article by a clinical doctor in the Netherlands documented a case where a lesbian couple was denied the ROPA model at different fertility clinics (Dundorp et al., 2010). The reason given was that since the couple could have had children through DI, there was no medical justification for them to expose themselves to the risks associated with IVF treatment, despite their expressed preference.

Whether the desire for genetic kinship can be said to rise to the level of a “need”

This brings us to our final consideration for the medical justification of ROPA. If ROPA is safe, or at least not significantly riskier than other fertility treatments, and is also desired by lesbian couples, is that enough to permit the practice? Or, put differently, if a lesbian couple could already legally access IVF with DI, then what justifies the extra steps of egg donation and embryo transfer? Unless the egg donation is done to avoid some genetic disease, then the only justification for these extra steps lies in the prospect of shared biological kinship. Is this preference for biological ties justified?

Some scholars argue that biological connections have inherent significance, since they are related to the role of family history in personal identity. For instance, Velleman17 has argued that family history is important for one's identity and self‐understanding. Some scholars have even defended the importance of biological connection and advocated for the right of lesbian couples to access the even more elaborate procedure of MRT for the sake of forging shared genetic ties, rooting their argument in social justice and reproductive freedom.18

Meanwhile, other scholars criticize this view, stating that biological connections themselves have no meaning or should at least not matter to people who intend to become parents.19 Likewise, Franscoise Baylis (2018) has argued stridently that we must not confuse wants and needs, and that while the desire for a biologically related child may be deeply felt, it does not rise to the level of a need and thus there is no need for the medical establishment to “pander to an acquired desire for genetic filiation” (Baylis, 2018, p. 865).

But even though Di Nucci (2016) argues, “there is no inherent value in the biological relationship between parents and children” he also stipulates that any country that permits the practice for one type of couple but not another is “guilty of both incoherence and discrimination” (786). Where IVF‐ET is legally available to heterosexual couples, it seems fair to offer it to any married couple—regardless of sexual orientation. Of course, this is not the case in China, where access to any form of ART is restricted to married heterosexual couples alone. So, while the question of fair access is not immediately relevant to the Chinese context (unless or until same‐sex marriage becomes legal), the question of whether biological ties rise to a level that can be called a “need” rather than a “want” is one that is worth exploring. To settle this question, we will have to leave the realm of the medical and enter the realm of the social.

The limitation of the discourse of personal autonomy on reproduction in China

As we have seen, in much of the existing literature on this issue, the permissibility of techniques like ROPA is framed as a matter of personal autonomy. Additionally, in contexts where same‐sex marriage is legal, ROPA can also be justified in the interests of justice (e.g., treating like as like).20 Yet, the rights of same‐sex couples remain controversial in many parts of the world. For instance, in Russia same‐sex marriage is illegal, and in much of the Islamic world, like Saudi Arabia and Iran, gay sex is punishable by death.21 And of course, same‐sex marriage (as well as any access to fertility care for same‐sex partners) is legally prohibited in China.

Of course, the fact that such romantic relationships are stigmatized (socially or legally) does not mean that there are no homosexual people living in those societies, nor does it mean that these couples will not long to start families, or try to create them—even if doing so requires that they break the law (as was the case with Li and Ti).

Discourse that frames the issue as one of autonomy, though valuable for many reasons, is subject to many stumbling blocks when arguing across cultural bounds, where the emphasis on personal liberty can seem alien to how many people in other parts of the world (like China) see themselves. Beginning from this standpoint, and with an interest in advocating for the human dignity and needs of sexual minorities, we recognize that there is a need for conceptual language that can articulate their struggles without imposing this individualistic lens.

Thus, what we aim to do here offer an assessment of the practice from the perspective of Confucian ethics. We have selected this lens for analysis because of Confucianism's strong influence on the history and culture of China, though we also acknowledge that many modern Chinese people may not think of themselves as explicitly or intentionally following Confucianism. Nevertheless, the Confucian perspective is one identifiable strand of local philosophical tradition. Through engaging with this tradition, we hope to strengthen the cross‐cultural dialogue and mutual understanding between Confucian ethical principles and other bioethical discourse on reproduction and new reproductive methods.

CONFUCIAN ETHICS AND ROPA

Although there is no systematic theoretical work on this subject in the ancient sources, glimpses of Confucian views on reproduction can be found in the writings of Confucius, Mencius, and other representative figures of pre‐Qin Confucianism. In what follows, we explore the Confucian understanding of “naturalness” (as aligning with the “Way of Heaven”), the concept of the “three bonds (gang)” that govern the Confucian social and ethical system, as well as the concept of filial piety and its relationship with blood ties to attempt to illuminate the unique ways that ROPA interacts with these concepts.

The Way of Heaven, naturalness, and ROPA

Central to all of Confucian thought is the belief that life, and especially human life, is good. The Confucian ethical system, thus, is deeply informed by the concept of naturalness and the ideal of living in harmony with “The Way of Heaven.” However, Confucianism's vision of the natural order places some important constraints on human behavior that will be relevant to the present discussion, namely, constraints on marriage and family structure. But before we discuss Confucian family ethics, it is worth observing the philosophy's attitude towards medicine and technology more generally.

Because of the inherent value Confucianism places on human life, it has always held medicine in high esteem. Since the Song Dynasty, there has been a popular saying that states, “If one cannot become a good statesman, one should become a good physician.” Additionally, Confucianism believes that everything, including technology, should serve human ends.22 Thus, Confucianism is not inherently techno‐phobic and has no in‐principle objection to medical and technological interventions that further human thriving. Confucianism also does not construe homosexuality as inherently sinful or unnatural. All of these factors, combined with the high‐value Confucianism places on procreation and the continuing of family lineages, would initially seem to indicate a welcoming attitude towards a technique like ROPA.

However, this does not necessarily mean that Confucianism holds a positive attitude towards ART, nor does it mean that Confucianism would permit the provision of such technologies to the LGBTQ+ community. In the ordinary (unassisted) course of things, two female partners cannot produce offspring. It is only through novel fertility technologies like ROPA that the possibility can arise. From the Confucian perspective, this can be seen as posing serious challenges to the “naturalness” of life and family. Nature, in the Confucian view, is construed as inherent and unchanging. An ethical human life seeks to align itself with the natural order and the “Way of Heaven,” as opposed to trying to bend nature to human desires. Thus, while Confucianism is not against technological advances per se, it would be deeply suspicious of any technology that so drastically alters the ordinary course of things. The “naturalness” of families, in particular, is an essential part of the Confucian ethical and social system. If this natural order is altered, the existing ethical framework is undermined. As we shall demonstrate, while Confucianism can accept the existence of same‐sex attraction as within the realm of the natural, the same cannot be said of same‐sex marriage or procreation. To see why, we will need to explore some of the history of how same‐sex relations have been understood in China's history and Confucian texts.

Confucianism and same‐sex marriage in China

There is no direct criticism of homosexuality in Confucian texts, nor are there records of punishment for homosexual behavior. Compared to many other societies, China's history can seem remarkably peaceful and tolerant in its attitude towards homosexuality, especially in eras where the country was prosperous. It is recorded that during the Ming and Qing dynasties, in Fujian Province, which was known as the “Coastal Zou Lu” due to its association with Zhu Xi (a Neo‐Confucian scholar), male homosexuality was surprisingly prevalent.23 During these eras, the characteristic attitude towards homosexuality in China was relatively neutral and stable.

Some scholars continue to express this tolerant attitude, such as the contemporary Confucian philosopher Zhang Xianglong.24 Addressing the issue of same‐sex marriage from a Confucian perspective, he writes that “distinguished from the Christian harsh repulsion and the Greek slight encouragement, Confucianism has been guardedly tolerant of homosexuality and judged homosexuals according to their personal moral behaviour.” In Zhang's understanding, Confucian cosmology has no problem accepting homosexuality as a kind of natural deviation from the mean, considering it to be a morally neutral and non‐pathological deviation from the norm.25 This stands in contrast to the characterization of homosexuality as a sin, found in Christianity.

Despite this promising start, Confucianism's emphasis on the importance of marriage and procreation still left little room for homosexual partnerships to achieve legitimate or legal status. In the “Introduction to the Hexagrams” (Xu Gua) within the The Classic of Changes (I Ching) it states,

There is heaven and earth, and then there are the ten thousand things. There are the ten thousand things, and then there is male and female. There is male and female, and then there is husband and wife. There is husband and wife, and then there is father and son. There is father and son, and then there is ruler and subject. (Lynn 1994, p. 106)26

In this expression of the natural order, the inevitable flow from heaven to family and to social harmony is traced. Thus, Confucianism regards the progression of male and female to husband and wife, and eventually parent and child to be the normative ideal. This family structure is seen as the embodiment of the fundamental structure that constitutes the world, not as something accidental or socially constructed, and certainly not amenable to change.

The marital relationship within Confucian rites and rituals serves as the foundation of the social system and the essence of a harmonious Confucian society. Various social constructs such as kinship, gendered role differentiation and social division of labor are based on the husband and wife relationship. As Zhang Xianglong explains,

Confucianism cannot advocate the legalization of homosexual marriage, because a union between homosexuals does not belong to a true yin‐yang means of combination and production. To treat it legitimately as being the same as a marriage between heterosexuals could lead to consequences that would be unfavorable to the longevity of human communities. (62)

Hierarchy and social harmony

While homosexuality itself may not be violently condemned within Confucianism, the prospect of same‐sex marriage is still often seen as a threat to social harmony. This is because the Confucian social system is strongly gendered and hierarchical. In fact, Confucianism frames human beings as fundamentally relational, rather than as individual selves.27 The Confucian normative system is characterized by an emphasis on roles, especially the “three bonds (gang),” namely, the hierarchical relationships of (1) ruler over subject, (2) father over son, and (3) husband over wife. The virtuous Confucian life is one defined by “living one's family roles to maximum effect.”28 On the one hand, the “three bonds (gang)” is a holistic rule of the individual‐family‐society/state that governs all relationships within and outside the family; On the other hand, it is also a centralized manifestation of “patriarchal” ideology and social structure. The difficulty of integrating lesbian families into the Confucian ethic is mainly manifested in the difficulty of compatibility with the social structure outside the family and with the idea of “patriarchal.”

The Confucian text, The Book of Rites (Liji) further emphasizes the subordinate position of the mother, stating,

The service due to a father is employed in serving a mother, and the love is the same for both. (But) in the sky there are not two suns, nor in a land two kings, nor in a state two rulers, nor in a family two equally honorable ‐ one (principle) regulates (all) these conditions. (Liji, San Fu Si Zhi, 7).

While acknowledging the mother's importance in the family, the passage also emphasizes that the mother, as a woman, is subordinate to the father. Confucian family ethics also prescribe a strict division of labor between the sexes: the father is the main economic provider for the family, while the mother is primarily responsible for managing the household and nurturing the children, serving as their first moral teacher. Since the Confucian vision of the natural order divides these roles along gendered lines, strictly speaking, same‐sex couples are not able to fulfill these familial duties. Even if one partner exhibits or takes on the role of the opposite sex, Confucianism would still frame this as a violation of the natural order and threat to social harmony.

Thus, while violence against homosexuals has been rare in Chinese history, same‐sex partnerships have nevertheless been denied legitimacy. This, combined with the strong cultural emphasis on marriage and family, has led many Chinese homosexuals to choose opposite‐sex marriages and face unhappiness in their marital lives to cope with the societal and familial pressures.29 That said, while Confucianism cannot accept same‐sex marriage, it does not categorically reject children born outside of marriage. In fact, Confucianism advocates for the love, care, and education of such children. In the next section, we will explore how a Confucian ethical system might regard the children conceived through ROPA or similar techniques within same‐sex partnerships.

Filial piety and same‐sex procreation

Even if same‐sex marriage is not considered ideal or acceptable under Confucianism, of course, some modern Chinese homosexual couples may still choose to enter into such partnerships. After all, just because a person does not adhere to Confucian standards in one aspect of life, that does not mean that they wholly reject the tradition in other domains. As they strive to reconcile their personal desires with the traditions that surround them, many Chinese homosexual couples feel a strong need to produce genetic offspring of their own as part of expressing their filial obligations to parents. So, it is also worth asking whether procreation enabled by novel forms of ART like ROPA could be reconciled with a modernized Confucian tradition, even without legalization of same‐sex marriage.

Can Confucianism support a technology like ROPA that allows a lesbian daughter to achieve the goal of continuing her family lineage outside the bounds of a heterosexual married relationship? The answer depends on which value is taken to be more important, upholding filial piety or fitting into the traditional (and patriarchal) family structure, assuming it is not possible to do both.

First, we must establish what filial piety is and its importance to Chinese culture. The intense value placed upon family lineage has deep roots within Confucianism and Chinese culture more broadly. The praise “Thousand descendants, hundred blessings, and billions of offspring” first appeared in the poem, “Jia Le,” in the The Classic of Poetry (Shijing),30 a text that dates to sometime between the 11th and 7th century BCE. Additionally, in the “Miscellaneous Records” part 2 (Zajixia) in Book of Rites (Liji), Confucius himself expresses his disapproval when there was an excess of land but a shortage of people, stating, “if he be charged with the care of a large territory, and the people be not correspondingly numerous, a superior man feels ashamed of it” (122). Indeed, Confucius considered population, wealth, and education to be the three main elements for building a country. Likewise, Mencius stated, “Wide territory and a numerous people are desired by the superior man” (Legge 1861, Vol. II, Chapter 13).31 Vigorously developing the population signifies national strength and thus has been a subject of the political consciousness of rulers throughout Chinese history. Confucius said, “There is no greater act than the birth of a child to parents” (Legge 2010, in “The Government of the Sages”).32 Mencius also stated, “There are three unfilial acts, and having no offspring is the greatest” (Legge 1861, Chapter 7).

While the same influence cannot be attributed to every classical Confucian belief, clearly, filial piety is still deeply woven into the fabric of Chinese culture. Writing about the ways in which Confucian concepts like filial piety inform modern family roles in ethnically Chinese households, as Muyskens explains,33

…the exact boundaries of [filial piety] are blurry. On the positive side, this has allowed the obligations to evolve with the times. Duties to help one's parents run the family farm or business have evolved to accommodate modern lifestyles. Thus, filial piety may cover a variety of activities and expressions today, from generally treating one's parents with politeness and kindness to providing them with financial assistance. However, this flexibility is always tempered with reverence for tradition – meaning Confucian values still sometimes find themselves at odds with competing liberal values from the West, particularly regarding gender roles, sexuality, and individuality. (Muyskens 2020, p. 5)

Consequently, for many Chinese people, carrying on the family line has risen to the level of a moral obligation, not just a matter of personal preference or choice. Passing on the family line is considered so important that the notion of having no children is considered disrespectful to one's parents and ancestors. To forgo having children would mean bearing the stigma of being “greatly unfilial.” In fact, historically it is the very inability to procreate that has been one of the reasons why the LGBTQ+ community has not been accepted by mainstream society in China. With innovations like ROPA, same‐sex couples can use technological means to procreate and express their respect for the family, ancestors, and the desire to carry on the family legacy. Thus, for many Chinese homosexual people, the ability to have children of their own is doubly important—it fulfills the filial duty and can be a way of trying to resist the stigmatization of homosexuality itself. Some people may say that filial piety is not of the highest importance in lesbian partnerships, as women do not bear the main responsibility for the transmission of the family line. This argument holds some true but overlooks the specific context. Considering that most homosexual individuals seeking ROPA services are the only children in their families—due to China's one‐child policy implemented since 1978 and ends 2016—the responsibility of continuing the family lineage naturally falls on them. Unlike their last generation, who might have had many siblings to rely on for carrying on the family line, they do not have such an option.

However, despite this novel procreation strategy and its implications for filial piety, defending same‐sex procreation within Confucianism still faces challenges. Beyond simply passing on genes, Confucian family ethics emphasizes the need for parents to engage in the moral and cultural education of their children. Some might argue that without both a male and female role model within the family, this would negatively impact a child's development. Even so, this viewpoint has been refuted by an increasing number of empirical research findings. For example, a study that included 2,971 children with same‐sex parents and over a million children with different‐sex parents followed from birth indicate that children raised by same‐sex parents from birth perform better than children raised by different‐sex parents in both primary and secondary education.34 Another study concluded that “there is a clear consensus in the social science literature indicating that American children living within same‐sex parent households fare just as well as those children residing within different‐sex parent households over a wide array of well‐being measures: academic performance, cognitive development, social development, psychological health, early sexual activity, and substance abuse.”35 To the extent that a Confucian system might be responsive to such data (itself a by no means a settled topic), there may be hope for more flexible interpretations of what makes an ideal family structure.

ROPA and biological ties

Confucianism places great emphasis on family and kinship ties. The continuation of human beings through the union of men and women is considered an important social good, and an essential part of continuing “the legacy of the sages,” making the marital union symbolic of the great relationship of humanity.36 Thus, within this cultural context, the desire to have one's own biological children is still deeply held by many Chinese people as an unquestioned primary value. Biological kinship, in contrast with social kinship (e.g., the parent–child relationship formed through adoption, fostering, or other non‐biological means), is strongly culturally preferred within China as blood ties are considered stronger and more legitimate.37

As Liao et al.38 articulate, the blood connection is of utmost importance in the fulfillment of filial duties. They go on to note that this traditional view is especially strong among rural communities, and that this cultural preference has expressed itself in the practice of donor insemination (even legally performed for medical reasons within straight couples struggling with infertility) continuing to be controversial. As they describe, Chinese bioethicists have attempted to reinterpret Confucian values to focus on the bonds of affection rather than merely the genetic connection, nevertheless, it is clear that for many Chinese families, nothing short of blood ties will satisfy their understanding of fulfilling this filial duty (Liao et al 2010).39 This cultural pressure makes choosing the ROPA model all the more attractive to lesbian partners within China, since it is one of the only means for both partners to establish biological kinship with the child, thus forming what would be seen as a legitimate parental bond within a broadly Confucian framework.

Negotiating trade‐offs and navigating the hierarchy of values

To summarize our discussion up to this point, we have demonstrated that Confucian views on naturalness can allow for homosexual identities, even if they are stigmatized for their inability to procreate. We have also seen that the extreme importance placed on procreation as one of the most important filial duties can lead many to feel that access to fertility treatments (especially those that allow for the formation of biological ties) rise to the level of a “need” and not merely a “want.” From a subjective standpoint, though this Confucian lens, the ROPA model may ironically seem more medically justifiable for female same‐sex couples than in other more liberal social contexts. It is precisely the devaluation for social kinship in the surrounding environment that justifies this otherwise more radical form of ART. This unique reproductive arrangement allows both partners to actively participate in the process, establish a biological connection with the child, and share the role of motherhood. In doing so, both members can in some sense fulfill their filial duty and enact an important social role. Of course, even if this can be understood to allow a lesbian couple fulfill an important filial duty, it does so outside of the prescribed Confucian family structure.

Our attempt to reconcile Confucian values with a novel reproductive technique like ROPA, thus comes down to one question: What should we take as the more important and central value in the Confucian ethical framework? Is it more important that a person fit into the hierarchical and heteronormative social structure? Or more important that they pass on their bloodlines? The answer seems to hinge on where we think filial piety sits within the pantheon of Confucian ethical commitments, and where within filial piety the obligation to procreate and pass on one's genes fits among the other filial duties. As we have already discussed, according to the Confucian sages (Confucius and Mencius) the duty to procreate is one of the highest if not the highest filial obligation.

As for where filial piety sits among other Confucian duties, we can find some indication of the answer from two stories from the classical Confucian texts (in the Analects 13.18 and in Mencius 7A25),40 where it seems that filial piety ranks above many other moral concerns, including the obligation to follow the law (Li, 2008). In each tale, the sage (whether Confucius or Mencius) seems to indicate that filial loyalty would permit and even require covering up the crimes of one's parents, even to the point that it meant helping one's father get away with murder (Li, 2008)!

Thus, for homosexuals living in China, ROPA can seem to be an attractive solution to the question of how to navigate the dual pressures of filial duties and the wish to form partnerships that are personally satisfying. Yet, both same‐sex marriage and ROPA remain illegal in China at present. And without legal protection, any couples who obtain access to this form of ART remain vulnerable to the kind of situation that befell Li and Ti. As Fu and Zhao41 discuss in their recent Bioethics article on the same legal case, the lack of access to legal and legitimate reproductive assistance does not prevent lesbian couples from desiring or seeking assistance through ROPA, it merely drives such couples underground.

By now we have discussed a number of Confucian views, which seem to indicate a mostly negative, though ultimately mixed and ambiguous attitude toward an innovation like ROPA. From the Confucian perspective, which emphasizes procreation and the importance of blood ties, ROPA's ability to promote the formation of biologically tied families certainly has its initial selling points. On one hand, ROPA would allow some couples to fulfill their filial duties in a way that was not previously open to them—and in a manner that also allows them to form partnerships more to their liking. Yet, on the other, ROPA's very power to do so threatens to undo the “natural” order of Confucianism's social system. Given that Confucian society is so starkly hierarchical and gendered, requiring procreation to take place within the context of heterosexual marriage, and under a patriarchal authority, ROPA seems destined to remain illegal as long as Confucian values hold sway.

However, cultural norms are never set in stone, and there are signs that some of these attitudes may be changing. On April 20, 2001, the third edition of the “Chinese Classification and Diagnostic Criteria of Mental Disorders” (CCMD‐3) was issued, removing “homosexuality” from its categorization, marking the “depathologization” of homosexuality in China.42 More recently, an empirical study conducted over a 15 year period has shown that “although there are still prejudices in certain aspects of public attitudes towards homosexuality, overall, the public is relatively tolerant, and over time, there is a trend of increasing tolerance towards homosexuality.”43

CONCLUSION

The desire to procreate and establish familial bonds is one that extends across cultures. Yet which family structures are seen as legitimate is highly variable across cultures. An assisted reproductive technique like ROPA makes possible a novel family structure, one that consists of two mothers and a child. In countries where same‐sex marriage is legal, ROPA offers lesbian couples a way to add an additional layer of biological ties to their existing legal ones. Where there is preexisting social support for same‐sex marriage and reproductive freedoms, including the rights to procreate outside of marriage (as in the case of single women), ROPA seems like a logical (if arguably superfluous) addition to the range of available ART offerings.

In contrast, in a context like China, where same‐sex marriage is still illegal, ROPA may raise more questions than answers for same‐sex couples. For lesbians within such a society, ROPA offers a way to reconcile their own wishes to form romantic partnerships that align with their own innate preferences, while also fulfilling what they perceive as their obligations to parents and ancestors. Yet, without legal recognition, this family structure stands on shaky ground. Without legal protection, and without an identifiable patriarch, ROPA violates the requirements of the Confucian ethical understanding of marriage and procreation. Same‐sex procreation, along with any procreation outside marriage, is heavily stigmatized in China. This stigma is rooted not just in Confucian values, but also upheld and perpetuated in the family planning management system, and the regulation of ART.

It is evident that as long as LGBTQ+ rights, as well as the reproductive rights of other individuals (such as single women44) are considered ‘luxury rights’ in China, ROPA is not likely to be legalized. Yet with our discussion in this paper, we hope to have shown that there are plausible local theoretical grounds for this to change. Confucianism is not inherently against homosexuality, and this means there is potential for attitudes to soften in the future. Interestingly, the very existence of technologies like ROPA, that allow for same‐sex couples to conceive children may eventually help to align such relationships with a Confucian family system. Not every individual lesbian or couple imbues biological or genetic ties with meaning or value, but for those that do, ROPA offers a method to instantiate those values. Where doing so would not incur harm, we would argue that such an option would be morally justified. We hope that our discussion in this paper illuminates some of the ways that traditional values intersect with novel reproductive technologies, and that this paper can help to initiate further cross‐cultural conversations in bioethics.45
