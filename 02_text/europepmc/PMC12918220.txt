ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveMarriage and fertility rates in China have declined steadily in recent years, raising concerns about their social and structural determinants. College students, as young adults at a critical life stage, play an important role in shaping future demographic trends. Guided by the socio-ecological framework, this study examined Chinese college students’ attitudes toward marriage and childbearing and explored how individual, social, and structural factors jointly shape these attitudes.Participants and settingA total of 238 college students participated in the quantitative phase, and 12 college students were interviewed in the qualitative phase.MethodsA mixed-methods design was employed, combining standardized questionnaires with semi-structured interviews. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multivariate logistic regression, while qualitative data were examined through thematic analysis. Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated to clarify mechanisms underlying observed statistical patterns.ResultsQuantitative results indicated substantial variation in attitudes toward marriage and childbearing. Male students, senior students, and those in romantic relationships reported more positive attitudes toward marriage, while male students and junior students expressed more positive attitudes toward childbearing. Qualitative findings showed that women’s cautious attitudes, as shaped by gendered social experiences, were influenced by pre-childbearing anxiety, reproductive health concerns, and perceived workplace penalties, which helped explain the observed gender differences. In addition, economic pressure, restrictive policies, and high-pressure social environments emerged as key structural factors contributing to the low prevalence of highly positive marriage and childbearing attitudes across the sample.ConclusionsCollege students’ attitudes toward marriage and childbearing are shaped by multi-level influences within the socio-ecological framework, including embodied risk perceptions, economic pressure, cultural norms, and institutional constraints. By integrating quantitative patterns with qualitative mechanisms, this study demonstrates the value of a mixed-methods design grounded in the socio-ecological framework for understanding contemporary marriage and fertility attitudes and for informing education and policy interventions aimed at reducing structural barriers to family formation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26326-3.Keywords: College students, Marriage, Childbearing, Influencing factors, Mixed research

BODY
Introduction

Marriage and childbearing are important life choices. They are also key parts of human social development [1]. These choices reflect the combined influence of social culture, economic conditions, and personal values [2]. In recent decades, social modernization has accelerated around the world, cultural exchange has increased and economic structures have also changed. As a result, people’s views on marriage and childbearing have changed in many societies. In East Asia, delayed marriage and low fertility have become clear demographic trends [3]. In China, marriage rates and the number of newborns continue to decline each year. This situation has raised strong concerns among researchers and policymakers [4]. College students are at a key stage of early adulthood. They will also form an important part of future society. Their attitudes toward marriage and parenthood shape their own life paths. These attitudes also influence family stability and long-term social development [5]. For this reason, studying college students’ views on marriage and parenthood is important. Such research helps explain how individual attitudes develop within family settings, social environments, and institutional contexts during social change.

Many studies have examined the factors that shape young people’s views on marriage and childbearing. These studies show that no single factor explains these views. Instead, attitudes form through the combined influence of multiple social influences. In traditional societies, marriage and childbearing are closely tied to family duty, gender roles, and obligations between generations. For example, some research shows that Confucian values and religious beliefs strengthen marriage and childbearing as social norms [6, 7]. In modern societies, the situation is different. Young people place more emphasis on personal growth, emotional satisfaction, and quality of life when making decisions about marriage and parenthood [8]. Arnett’s theory of emerging adulthood explains these changes from a developmental perspective [9]. According to this theory, college students spend more time exploring their identities in a rapidly changing social environment. Their views on marriage and parenthood are shaped by their stage of education, career plans, and uncertainty about the future. Many studies show that economic pressure and education costs limit young people’s willingness to marry and have children [10]. These pressures do not affect everyone in the same way. Differences related to gendered social roles, family resources, and biological conditions can strengthen or weaken their impact [11–13].

Cross-cultural research also shows clear differences in attitudes toward marriage and parenthood. Social context plays a major role in these differences. In Japan, low fertility is often linked to high economic pressure, unequal childcare responsibilities, and delayed or non-marriage [14]. In China, attitudes are strongly influenced by urban–rural differences, gender preferences, and traditional family values [15–17]. These findings suggest a shared conclusion. Individual attitudes alone do not determine marriage and parenthood decisions. Family relations, social structures, and institutional conditions also play important roles.

Despite these contributions, existing research still has several limitations. First, many studies rely on either quantitative or qualitative methods alone. This approach makes it difficult to capture both overall patterns and personal experiences at the same time. Second, many studies list individual, family, and social factors side by side. Few studies examine how these factors are jointly situated within different social levels in a systematic way.

In this study, we adopt the socio-ecological framework as the main analytical framework. The socio-ecological framework explains human behavior as shaped by multiple levels of the social environment [18]. These levels usually include the micro level, such as individual traits, psychological states, and health perceptions. They also include the meso level, such as family relationships, peer interactions, and intimate ties. The macro level includes economic structures, cultural norms, and public policies. The socio-ecological framework emphasizes that attitudes and behaviors do not result from a single level of influence. Instead, they are shaped by the combined influence of factors operating across multiple levels. In this study, college students’ views on marriage and childbearing are understood as outcomes shaped across multiple levels of the socio-ecological framework. Individual emotions and risk perceptions shape personal expectations. Family expectations, gender roles, and social norms shape decision-making contexts. Economic conditions, employment opportunities, and fertility policies influence judgments about feasibility and timing [4, 19]. The socio-ecological framework offers a clear and integrated structure for linking these influences.

In summary, college students’ views on marriage and childbearing emerge through the joint influence of individual psychology, family interactions, and broader social and policy environments. The socio-ecological framework provides a useful basis for understanding this process. Based on this framework, this study uses a mixed-methods design. The quantitative analysis describes overall patterns and key correlates of attitudes toward marriage and parenthood among Chinese college students. The qualitative interviews explore how family, social, and policy contexts shape these attitudes in everyday life. The study then integrates both types of evidence within the socio-ecological framework. Through this approach, the study aims to clarify the multi-level mechanisms that shape college students’ views on marriage and childbearing in China. The findings are expected to support marriage and parenthood education in universities. They may also inform more targeted and systematic family support policies. Finally, the study offers empirical insight into the relationship between individualization, family change, and social structure during modernization in the Chinese context.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. Quantitative data were collected and analyzed first, followed by qualitative data collection to complement and interpret the quantitative findings.

Quantitative study

This study was conducted from September to October 2024, and the snowball sampling method was used to select students enrolled in colleges and universities in Eastern China as study subjects. Inclusion criteria: ① full-time undergraduate and master’s degree students willing to participate in this study; ② age ≥ 18 years old. Exclusion criteria: students who dropped out or took a leave of absence in the middle of the study. According to Kendall’s [20] principle for sample size calculation, the sample size is generally 5–10 times the number of variables. In this study, there are 11 variables, so the required sample size is 55–110; the actual sample size included was 238. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. All variables were categorical, count data were described using frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Multivariable ordinal logistic regression models were applied to examine factors associated with attitudes toward marriage and childbearing. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated and are reported in the results. The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the test of parallel lines and was not violated in either model. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Quantitative study instruments

General information questionnaire

This part of the questionnaire was designed to collect basic sociodemographic information, including sex (self-reported biological sex: male/female), grade, only-child status, prior relationship experience, and current relationship status.

Marriage and childbearing opinions questionnaire

This part of the questionnaire was developed based on the study objectives, a review of relevant literature, and the socio-ecological framework, which conceptualizes attitudes toward marriage and childbearing as individual-level outcomes shaped by broader social contexts. It consisted of six single-item questions, including current attitudes on marriage, attitudes on childbearing, ideal age at marriage, intention to marry in the future, factors influencing the decision to marry, and factors influencing the decision to have children (Table S1). Among these items, three questions—attitudes toward marriage, attitudes toward childbearing, and intention to marry in the future—were measured using ordered categorical response options and reflected a common underlying construct related to marriage and childbearing attitudes and intentions. These three items were therefore treated as an attitudinal subscale for psychometric evaluation. Internal consistency reliability of this subscale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding an acceptable coefficient (α = 0.779). Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.677, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² = 211.595, df = 3, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. These results support the basic reliability and structural validity of the attitudinal subscale used in the quantitative analysis. The remaining items addressed ideal timing of marriage and specific factors influencing marriage and childbearing decisions and were analyzed descriptively, as they were not designed to function as scale-based measures.

In this study, sex refers to participants’ self-reported biological classification (male/female), whereas gender is used to describe socially constructed roles and expectations (e.g., women and men) in the qualitative analysis. Sexual orientation was not explicitly assessed in the questionnaire. The study therefore reflects participants’ attitudes toward marriage and childbearing as they are commonly framed within the prevailing heterosexual marriage and family context in China.

Qualitative exploration

Based on quantitative results, semi-structured interviews were conducted with students who were willing to participate and were purposively selected to represent male and female students and students who were in relationships or single. The interview guide, developed from the study objectives and the literature and informed by the socio-ecological framework, was designed to explore family-, social-, and policy-related contexts surrounding individual marriage and childbearing attitudes and covered the following topics: (i) perceived significance of marriage; (ii) perceived significance of childbearing; (iii) perceived obstacles to marriage and childbearing; (iv) suggested policies or measures to promote marriage and childbearing; (v) opinions on young women’s reluctance towards marriage and childbearing; (vi) personal marriage plans and ideal age; (vii) ideal number of children and gender preference; (viii) personal childbearing plans, ideal number, and timing. Before the interviews, the purpose was explained and participants were encouraged to share their genuine views. Interviews lasted 15 to 20 min. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached (no new themes emerged during analysis), resulting in 12 participants. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim within 24 h, and combined with field notes. NVIVO 14.0 facilitated data analysis using thematic analysis to identify and refine themes related to students’ views, as shown in Table S2.

Results

Background characteristics of the respondents

Table 1 presents the background characteristics of the respondents. A total of 238 individuals were included in the quantitative study, including 79 males and 159 females. Approximately 66.4% of respondents reported having ever been in a romantic relationship, and 31.5% reported being in a relationship at the time of the survey. Regarding attitudes toward marriage, 15.1% of respondents reported “special expectations,” whereas 60.1% reported neutral or indifferent attitudes and 24.8% reported negative attitudes. For childbearing, only 9.7% reported “special expectations,” while 45.0% reported indifferent attitudes and 45.3% reported neutral or negative attitudes.

Marriage and childbearing as important pathways for social stability and family construction

When respondents talked about the significance of marriage, they believed that it could enhance the ability of individuals to take responsibility, such as Interviewee L1: “I think it is first of all to be able to make a family for two people who really love each other and take responsibility for each other.” At the family level, Interviewees believed that marriage could connect two families together and produce a new small family, such as respondent L5: “I think the significance of marriage is to connect two families together and produce a new small family.” Interviewees also believed that having children is a kind of pivot to connect couples and is also a crystallization of love, such as D2: “For a family then, procreation, because having a baby coming may become a pivot for a family, and then it may also make the family happier and more fulfilled.” Fertility also enables family continuity and traditional culture to be passed on from generation to generation, as Interviewee L1: “For a family, and for a family with a relatively large population, fertility plays a significance of passing on the family name to the next generation.” and Interviewee L3: “A lot of Chinese culture needs to be passed on from generation to generation.” At the broader social and national level, Interviewees believed that marriage and childbearing could maintain social stability and promote national development. Fertility was considered to reduce population aging and build the country, such as Interviewee L4: “The significance of childbearing is that it can increase the population of China first of all, now the population is aging so seriously and there are fewer newborn babies, so childbearing can increase the population, and the society will be very good if the population is large, and it will bring some new development.”

Structural facilitating and constraining effects of policy, economic, and social environments on marriage and childbearing intentions

Respondents felt that simplifying divorce procedures could lower barriers to marriage. They feel that the divorce procedure is cumbersome and the setting of the divorce cooling-off period may be a hindrance to marriage, e.g., L3: “The divorce cooling-off period deters some women students from marriage; having witnessed or experienced unhappy marriages, they fear this policy will prevent a smooth divorce.” Interviewees also spoke of how promoting gender equality in the workplace can encourage childbearing. The current employment environment is inequitable toward unmarried women without children; for example, D3 stated, “Some companies prefer hiring men over similarly qualified women to avoid costs associated with marriage and maternity leave.” Women who are married, have children, or are pregnant also experience unfair treatment; for example, interviewee D2 stated, “From a woman’s perspective, pregnant working women first face the risk of unemployment, followed by childcare and other pressures, including those related to family. In addition, returning to work after childbirth may be met with rejection, potentially leading to unemployment.” Respondents believe that financial subsidies are key to increasing young people’s willingness to marry and have children, and that the primary impediment to marriage and childbearing among young people at present is the economy; for example, L5 said, “The overall economic situation is rather tough, so people don’t have such a material basis for childbearing or marriage.” L1: “Higher child-rearing costs deter young people from having multiple children because they fear they cannot provide adequate financial support.” Therefore, respondents believe that if they can provide effective economic subsidies for childbearing, it can significantly increase the fertility rate, e.g., L4: “Supporting childbearing: if you have two or three children and the state exempts children’s tuition fees or provides welfare protection, I think it can promote childbearing.” Respondents also suggested that improving the social environment and media climate can positively influence views on marriage and childbirth, and that serious social involution and high pressure on young people will affect the motivation to marry and have children, as illustrated by L5: “Nowadays, the degree of social involution is more serious, which may also affect people’s initiative to marry and have children.” In the age of self-media, many software platforms release false information or video clips to attract traffic, which may cause anxiety among young people, as in L1: “In this era of rapid Internet development, exposure to excessive negative online information may trigger unnecessary anxiety, thereby hindering people from getting married and having children.” Therefore, respondents believe that strengthening positive media coverage and creating a harmonious, warm family atmosphere can increase young people’s motivation to get married and have children; for example, participant L1 said, “To promote marriage, I think that we can increase publicity in some public media to encourage young people and talk to them about the benefits of getting married.”

Men’s involvement in childcare promotes fertility intentions through shared family responsibilities

When discussing parental leave policies, some interviewees suggested that increasing men’s paid paternity leave would not only promote fairness but also allow men to participate in the postnatal childcare, reduce pressure on women, and improve fertility rates (e.g., L3: “Measures to promote childbirth include providing adequate maternity leave for women and longer paternity leave for men, recognizing that this is a shared responsibility of the family.”) This enables fathers to actively participate in childcare and avoids placing the entire responsibility on mothers. L4: “Policy interventions should include extending maternity leave for women and introducing (or increasing) paternity leave for men to facilitate shared childcare responsibilities.”

Dual influences of pre-childbearing body-related anxiety and post-childbearing psychological support on fertility intentions

When it comes to fertility intentions, interviewees emphasized that women’s psychological state before and after childbearing plays a crucial role in shaping their willingness to have children. At the pre-childbearing stage, concerns about bodily changes and irreversible physical damage were found to substantially weaken women’s fertility intentions; for example, D1 stated: “I am very resistant to giving birth, because I understand that during childbirth and the so-called postpartum recovery period, women will experience changes in their physical body structure. And I am a woman who cares a lot about my appearance, so I do not want my body to suffer some irreversible damage just because I gave birth to a child. That would make me feel very distressed.” At the post-childbearing stage, the lack of professional psychological support was perceived to exacerbate postpartum stress and further constrain fertility intentions. D2 emphasized the necessity of specialized mental health services for postpartum women, stating: “I recommend that communities provide specialized psychological counselling, ideally delivered by qualified mental health professionals, for postpartum women. Without such support to mitigate significant pressure, it is challenging to promote fertility and increase birth rates.”

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated to clarify how observed statistical patterns reflect underlying mechanisms situated within the socio-ecological framework (Table 6).

Integrated interpretation of quantitative and qualitative findings on attitudes toward marriage and childbearing

Quantitatively, male students reported more positive attitudes toward marriage and childbearing than female students. Qualitative interviews indicated that women’s more cautious attitudes were closely related to pre-childbearing body-related anxiety and perceived workplace penalties associated with pregnancy and childbearing. These experiences help explain the observed sex differences by highlighting gendered experiences that contribute to women’s higher perceived risks and costs.

In addition, the quantitative finding that only a small proportion of students expressed highly positive attitudes toward marriage and childbearing was further illuminated by qualitative evidence. Interviewees frequently cited economic pressure, policy constraints, and competitive social environments as major deterrents. These structural factors contribute to widespread neutral or negative attitudes by framing marriage and childbearing as high-cost and uncertain life choices.

Together, the integrated findings suggest that gendered risk perceptions and broader structural pressures jointly shape college students’ attitudes toward marriage and childbearing.

Discussion

Women’s heightened caution toward marriage and childbearing driven by reproductive and structural risks

We found that male students reported more positive expectations toward marriage (OR = 0.402) and childbearing (OR = 0.181). This pattern is consistent with traditional views in Chinese family culture. Under the influence of Confucian ideas of filial duty [21] and long-standing patriarchal norms [22], marriage and family formation are often seen as key markers of men’s entry into adulthood. One male participant described his ideal family as “two or three children… one son and two daughters, or one son and one daughter” (D5). This idealized image suggests that men are more likely to imagine larger families without directly facing the practical risks and pressures that women participants often mentioned. In contrast, women’s more cautious stance reflects their comprehensive assessment of risks across multiple levels of the socio-ecological framework. At the individual and interpersonal levels, women face disproportionate biological burdens and caregiving expectations when considering marriage and future childbearing. At the organizational and institutional levels, they must also navigate long-term uncertainties stemming from structural workplace inequalities. As revealed by both existing research and the findings of this study’s interviews, these risks manifest specifically as potential career interruptions [23], physical exhaustion from childbirth [24], and heightened psychological stress stemming from gendered childcare divisions [25]. The accumulation of these multi-layered risks contributes women to adopt more cautious strategies when forming attitudes toward marriage and childbearing.

Further analysis shows that women’s fear of childbirth does not come from a single cause. Instead, it develops through a process that involves several levels of the socio-ecological framework. This process includes biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors working together [26, 27]. At the individual physical level, severe pain during childbirth is a direct source of fear [28]. This pain is caused by uterine contractions and pressure on surrounding tissues. Although the level of pain differs across individuals, it often reaches very high levels. Psychologically, research indicates that women’s fear of childbirth correlates closely with their level of knowledge. Lack of information makes individuals more susceptible to the influence of others’ negative experiences, thereby amplifying feelings of fear [29, 30]. At the sociocultural level, depictions of childbirth as a dangerous and painful process [31], societal expectations around reproduction, and idealized definitions of motherhood [32] all deepen women’s fears. Structural inequalities in the workplace further exacerbate women’s caution [33]. Participants described discriminatory hiring practices, pregnancy-related penalties, and the risk of unemployment during or after maternity leave. These institutional factors steadily increase women’s expected costs related to marriage and childbearing. They show how limits within the system build up across different levels of the socio-ecological framework and, over time, shape women’s attitudes toward marriage and having children.

Women’s cautious attitudes toward marriage and childbearing do not arise from personal preference alone. They result from the combined effects of physical risks, psychological stress, cultural expectations, and institutional limits at different levels of the socio-ecological framework. A deep understanding of these multi-layered mechanisms provides crucial theoretical groundwork for developing targeted interventions to reduce women’s perceived risks of childbearing and improve their expectations regarding marriage and childbearing.

Structural economic pressures and social environments jointly shape low levels of positive attitudes toward marriage and childbearing

Within the socio-ecological framework, economic instability is an important structural factor that affects intentions toward marriage and childbearing. At the macro and institutional levels, it shapes how individuals judge the risks of marriage and having children. This study shows that economic pressure is widespread and plays an important role in delaying or avoiding marriage and childbearing. Previous studies also point to a clear link between economic barriers and delayed family formation. In particular, higher housing prices and childcare costs lower young people’s willingness to have children [34].

At the organizational and institutional level, respondents repeatedly highlighted the substantial financial investment required for marriage. One respondent stated bluntly: “Hosting a respectable wedding demands financial backing, while post-marriage housing, daily transportation, and future livelihood security all rely on a stable economic foundation. Current economic pressures represent the primary practical obstacle” (D5). This perception of marriage costs transforms it in young people’s eyes from a purely emotional choice into an institutional arrangement demanding long-term financial commitment. The high cost of child-rearing is widely regarded as a major factor suppressing fertility intentions [35]. “The steep expenses of raising children deter young people, who worry they cannot shoulder the sufficient financial burden” (L1). As educational investments, living costs, and parenting expectations continue to rise, childbearing is increasingly perceived as a high-risk, high-cost long-term investment. Social exchange theory views marriage and childbearing as a process of weighing costs against benefits. When economic burdens exceed an individual’s capacity, the willingness to marry and have children naturally diminishes [36]. Existing research also shows that higher child-rearing costs, driven by stronger social competition and greater spending on education, further reduce young people’s desire to have children [37]. In a fiercely competitive social environment, “the increasingly severe phenomenon of social entropization may affect people’s willingness to marry and have children (L5),” with such pressures also dampening young people’s enthusiasm for marriage and childbearing. In a context marked by uncertainty, many young people focus more on career development and financial stability [38]. Social comparison theory [39] suggests that when individuals feel they are behind their peers in income or career progress, they are more likely to postpone marriage and childbearing. Within the socio-ecological framework, this kind of comparison pressure comes from both economic conditions and social norms. At the individual level, it appears as a stronger sense of risk. This helps explain survey results showing that only a small share of people hold very positive views on marriage and childbearing, while neutral or negative views are more common.

Multi-level intervention strategy

From the socio-ecological framework, attitudes toward marriage and childbearing come from the joint effects of many factors. These factors include national institutions, organizational settings, community support, and individual psychological traits. To reduce young people’s cautious views on marriage and childbearing, actions are needed at several levels. These actions should focus on lowering the risks that young people perceive and face in daily life.

At the individual and educational context levels, universities should play an active role in shaping perceptions of marriage and childbearing. Marriage, intimate relationships, and fertility issues should be integrated into mental health education systems, accompanied by professional counseling and supportive activities. Enhancing students’ understanding of and coping abilities regarding the risks associated with marriage and childbearing will strengthen their psychological preparedness for future family roles. At the community and service system level, enhancing medical and psychological support can effectively alleviate women’s fear of childbirth. Systematic childbirth education programs—including labor knowledge dissemination, pain management guidance, and postpartum recovery education—help reduce anxiety stemming from information gaps [40]. Concurrently, providing routine psychological counseling and postpartum support services at the community level [41] assists women in managing emotional stress during pregnancy and the postpartum period, thereby enhancing their psychological resilience.

At the organizational and workplace levels, reducing institutionalized gender discrimination is particularly crucial. Legislation can explicitly establish paternity leave systems [42] and safeguard women’s wage continuity and career progression [43] to promote the redistribution of family responsibilities. Evidence from other countries suggests that well-designed maternity and parental leave policies can play an important role in alleviating women’s childbearing-related burdens and mitigating negative career impacts. Research on maternity leave indicates that access to paid leave is associated with better maternal mental health, whereas unpaid or insufficient leave is linked to higher risks of postpartum depressive symptoms, underscoring the protective role of adequate maternity leave during the childbearing period [44]. At the same time, longitudinal evidence from Sweden shows that fathers’ use of parental leave is associated with more balanced caregiving responsibilities and improved employment outcomes for mothers, suggesting that shared parental leave arrangements can help reduce women’s long-term career penalties following childbirth [45]. Related research further indicates that paternal leave use may generate positive spillover effects within families, contributing to greater relationship stability and shared childcare responsibilities [46]. Together, these findings imply that combining sufficient maternity leave with institutional support for fathers’ parental leave may help reduce women’s perceived risks related to marriage and childbearing. Concurrently, strengthening enforcement against gender discrimination and encouraging flexible work arrangements can help mitigate the career costs women bear due to childbearing. At the macro-institutional level, economic and policy support plays an important role in lowering the costs of marriage and childbearing. Governments can set up marriage and childbirth subsidies [47] and offer marriage and childcare allowances [37]. These measures can work together with tax reductions and special additional deductions [48] to ease the long-term financial pressure that families face during marriage and childbearing. At the same time, improving employment structures and increasing stable job opportunities can reduce young people’s worries about future uncertainty.

Overall, progress is needed in policy, institutions, services, and education at the same time and at different levels of the socio-ecological framework. These combined efforts can reduce the structural risks that young people face when making decisions about marriage and childbearing. In this way, more positive and stable attitudes toward marriage and parenthood can be supported.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths: The integration of quantitative and qualitative methodologies enabled this study to capture both broad trends and nuanced perspectives on college students’ attitudes toward marriage and childbearing. In particular, the mixed-methods design revealed critical gender-based divergences in attitudes that may have been overlooked by a single-method approach. Moreover, the interview-derived policy recommendations (e.g., paid paternity leave, workplace gender equality, and psychological support for women) provide practical pathways for addressing the challenges identified in this study. Limitations: Several limitations should be noted. First, snowball sampling was used rather than probability-based sampling, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. Second, the study was conducted in a single province (Anhui), which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Third, although basic reliability and construct validity were assessed for the attitudinal subscale of the Marriage and Childbearing Opinions Questionnaire, the instrument primarily relied on single-item and descriptive measures, and full psychometric validation as a multi-item scale was not performed. Future studies should develop and validate more comprehensive measurement instruments. Fourth, this study did not aim to construct or test a causal model. Given the cross-sectional design and the complex, multi-level nature of marriage and childbearing attitudes, causal relationships could not be established. Future research may build on the present findings by adopting longitudinal designs or causal modeling approaches to further examine the directional pathways among key factors. In addition, sexual orientation was not measured as part of the study design. As a result, the findings primarily reflect attitudes toward marriage and childbearing within a heteronormative social and institutional framework. Students with other sexual orientations may have been less likely to participate or may have provided responses that do not fully reflect their situations, potentially introducing bias. Finally, some potentially relevant factors, such as student debt and detailed career trajectories, were not included and warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study examined college students’ views on marriage and childbearing in Anhui Province, China. The findings show that these views are heterogeneous and shaped by factors operating at multiple levels within the socio-ecological framework, including individual characteristics, relational contexts, and broader social and institutional conditions. Quantitative results indicated that sex, academic stage, and relationship experience were associated with attitudes toward marriage and childbearing, while qualitative findings helped explain these patterns by highlighting gendered reproductive concerns, economic pressure, social competition, and perceived institutional constraints. Rather than reflecting isolated personal preferences, students’ cautious attitudes appear embedded in a wider social context characterized by uncertainty and structural risk. Although based on a regional sample, this study provides empirical insight into how young adults’ marriage and fertility attitudes are shaped by layered social conditions, and it offers evidence to inform education and policy discussions in societies facing delayed marriage and declining fertility.
