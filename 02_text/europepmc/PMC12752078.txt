ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesThis study examined the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage in young adulthood. Within this scope, a model incorporating the mediating role of cognitive flexibility was tested.MethodsThe study included 436 young adults (240 women, 196 men) living in Türkiye who had experience in romantic relationships. Data were collected using the Personal Information Form, Relationship Satisfaction Scale, Inonu Marriage Attitudes Scale, and Cognitive Flexibility Scale. The established model was analyzed using structural equation modeling via the AMOS 24 program, and the significance of the mediating effect was assessed using the bootstrapping method.ResultsRomantic relationship satisfaction positively predicts both marital attitude and cognitive flexibility; cognitive flexibility also significantly predicts marital attitude. Mediational analysis revealed that cognitive flexibility played a significant partial mediating role in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude.ConclusionThe findings of the study indicate that increased romantic relationship satisfaction has direct and indirect positive effects on young adults’ attitudes toward marriage. Based on the results obtained, various recommendations have been made to practitioners and researchers.Keywords: Marital attitude, Romantic relationship satisfaction, Cognitive flexibility, Young adulthood

BODY
Introduction

The importance of relationship satisfaction and the identification of factors that influence it have long been a topic of interest for researchers in the fields of psychology and social sciences. Studies in this field have revealed important findings about the psychological, social, and environmental factors that affect individuals’ happiness in romantic relationships and the sustainability of the relationship [1–3]. There are many factors that affect romantic relationship satisfaction. Attitudes, thoughts, and beliefs about relationships and marriage, social factors, emotional intelligence, attachment styles, problem-solving styles, length of marriage, type of marriage, and romantic jealousy are some of these factors [4].

Individuals’ attitudes toward marriage are also closely related to relationship satisfaction. Positive or negative beliefs about marriage, thoughts about romantic relationships, and behavioral patterns exhibited in relationships directly shape individuals’ decisions about marriage [5]. Therefore, examining the meaning attributed to marriage is an important area of research for understanding individuals’ attitudes toward marriage [6]. Changes in marriage and family structure since the 1990s, along with declining marriage rates, have necessitated a more detailed examination of attitudes toward marriage and family. These changes have transformed marriage into a worrying phenomenon for some individuals [7].

Young adulthood (ages 18–29) is a critical stage in which individuals face fundamental developmental tasks such as identity development, forming close relationships, and gaining independence [8]. College years are a particularly intense period in this process; individuals shape both their personal and romantic relational identities. According to Erikson’s [9] psychosocial development theory, romantic relationships are a critical developmental task for individuals aged 21–30 in terms of resolving the crisis of “intimacy versus isolation” [9]. Romantic relationships not only increase individuals’ emotional satisfaction but also play a decisive role in identity development and psychological well-being [10]. In this developmental context, satisfaction derived from current romantic relationship experiences plays a central role as a factor that directly influences attitudes toward future long-term commitment and the institution of marriage.

In the literature, it has been shown that the level of satisfaction experienced in romantic relationships is inversely proportional to individuals’ irrational beliefs. For example, Topkaya and colleagues [11] found a negative relationship between the irrational beliefs of individuals with different thinking styles and their relationship satisfaction [12]. The negative impact of irrational beliefs on relationship satisfaction highlights the importance of flexibility in how individuals perceive events and situations when faced with difficulties. It has been reported that as satisfaction in romantic relationships decreases, irrational beliefs increase and, consequently, cognitive flexibility decreases [13]. This situation shows that Cognitive Flexibility is a critical component for a healthy relationship trajectory.

Existing studies in the literature reveal that different variables are related to relationship satisfaction and marital attitude, but they also indicate a need to examine these variables together. This study aims to investigate the psychological mechanism of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude. Cognitive flexibility is a concept that centers on an individual’s awareness of behavioral alternatives and their capacity to adapt to situations [14]. The study’s justification of this mediating role is based primarily on cognitive-behavioral and social-cognitive theories, which focus on individuals’ capacity to adapt their perceptions and behaviors in response to relational difficulties. Our theoretical framework is based on the idea that high RRS enhances cognitive flexibility by using the individual’s emotional satisfaction and rewards as a resource. This enhanced flexibility, in turn, positively influences the individual’s attitude toward marriage by increasing their ability to adapt to uncertainties and evaluate new ideas related to marriage. This mechanism demonstrates that the effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on marital attitude is partially mediated through cognitive flexibilityn capacity.

The current study contributes to the literature with original insights at both theoretical and empirical levels. In this regard, the study develops a new theoretical perspective by positioning cognitive flexibility not only as an individual adaptation indicator but also as a process variable explaining the psychological mechanisms underlying relationship satisfaction and marital attitudes. The fact that no study has been found in the literature where these three variables have been tested together using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) reinforces the original value of the research. Furthermore, by addressing cognitive processes in romantic relationships and attitudes toward marriage from a developmental perspective, the study offers important insights into the psychosocial dynamics specific to young adulthood. Based on this theoretical framework and empirical findings, the following key hypotheses were formulated in the study:

Hypothesis 1: It is expected that romantic relationship satisfaction in young adulthood will be positively related to marital attitudes.

Hypothesis 2: It is expected that romantic relationship satisfaction will be positively related to cognitive flexibility.

Hypothesis 3: It is expected that cognitive flexibility will be positively related to marital attitudes.

Hypothesis 4: It is predicted that cognitive flexibility will play a meaningful and explanatory mediating role in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitudes.

Romantic relationship satisfaction

Interpersonal relationships, including family, friendships, and romantic relationships, are an important factor influencing individuals’ life choices, satisfaction levels, and future plans ( [1, 2] [3]). From adolescence onwards, individuals tend to seek romantic relationships and form close bonds with the opposite sex to develop their identity and meet their emotional needs Research shows that approximately 70% of 17-18-year-olds have experienced romantic relationships, and it is emphasized that these relationships play a more decisive role in the lives of young adults [15, 16]. According to Erikson’s [9] psychosocial development theory, romantic relationships are a critical developmental task for individuals aged 21–30 in terms of resolving the crisis of “intimacy versus isolation.”

Romantic relationships play a decisive role not only in increasing individuals’ emotional fulfillment but also in identity development, attachment styles, and psychological well-being [10]. Relationship satisfaction, an important variable in these relationships, is one of the fundamental factors determining the quality and sustainability of the relationship [17]. Sabatelli [18] states that relationship satisfaction is formed as a result of the interaction between the expectations of the individual and their partner, and that these expectations may change over time. Sternberg [19] emphasizes that the difference between an individual’s perception of an ideal relationship and their current relationship is an important determinant affecting relationship satisfaction.

Relationship satisfaction is closely related to the investments individuals make in the relationship, their level of commitment, and the closeness they establish with their partner [10]. High relationship satisfaction allows individuals to perceive the positive aspects of their relationships more and notice the negative aspects less [1, 2]. Additionally, factors affecting relationship satisfaction include individuals’ attitudes and beliefs about marriage and romantic relationships, social factors, emotional intelligence, attachment styles, length of marriage, and romantic jealousy [4, 5]. These findings are important for understanding the satisfaction young adults experience in romantic relationships and the effects of this satisfaction on their attitudes toward marriage.

Marriage attitudes

Marriage is a multidimensional relationship that provides mutual support, trust, and belonging between individuals, fulfills basic needs, and shapes people’s outlook on the future [20]. Recognized as one of the most important and enduring interpersonal relationships throughout human history [21], marriage is a critical turning point in an individual’s life. The transition from singlehood to marriage can lead to various changes in an individual’s life, profoundly affecting psychological and social adjustment [22].

Understanding attitudes toward marriage is important in terms of revealing the meaning of the institution of marriage for individuals and the resulting positive or negative approaches [6]. The decline in marriage rates, particularly since the 1990s, has necessitated a detailed examination of individuals’ perspectives on marriage and family structure (7). Attitudes toward marriage encompass not only the decision-making process of getting married but also mate selection, approval of marriage, and motivations for marriage [23].

Current research indicates that despite young people’s tendency to form long-term relationships, they believe they lack the necessary qualifications for marriage [7]. This finding highlights the importance of understanding attitudes toward marriage in young adulthood and identifying sources of individual motivation [24].

Mediating variable in the relationship: cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility is a concept centered on an individual’s awareness of behavioral alternatives and their capacity to adapt to situations [14]. This concept encompasses an individual’s awareness of communication options, willingness to adapt, and self-efficacy regarding flexibility. Cognitive flexibility has been shown to be positively related to engagement, awareness, and interpersonal communication competence, and negatively related to rigidity and reluctance to communicate [25, 26].

Research shows that cognitively flexible individuals can evaluate various communication and behavioral options in social situations and are more successful in finding the right response [14]. Although studies examining cognitive flexibility in the context of romantic relationships are limited, existing research indicates that flexible individuals have higher effective communication skills and may increase relationship satisfaction [26, 27].

Cognitive flexibility has also been found to be related to the resilience individuals develop in response to stress and difficulties. Resilience is defined as the capacity to show positive adaptation despite adverse conditions and is supported in a relational context by factors such as empathy, compassion, flexibility, and interpersonal sensitivity [26, 28, 29]. For example, it has been observed that couples with moderately stressful life experiences are better prepared for new challenges and exhibit increased support-seeking behaviors [26, 30].

The positive effect of cognitive flexibility in romantic relationships is reinforced by its negative correlation with irrational beliefs and its support for problem-solving skills [31–33]. Therefore, individuals’ capacity to recognize different options and develop solutions in their relationships emerges as a critical factor in ensuring that the relationship continues in a healthy and satisfying manner.

The relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage

Romantic relationships are complex processes that affect many different areas of an individual’s life and are also influenced by many factors. One of the most decisive factors for individuals in this process is relationship satisfaction. Relationship satisfaction reflects an individual’s feelings, attitudes, and thoughts about their relationship with their partner. These feelings and thoughts directly affect both the quality and sustainability of the relationship [4].

Factors affecting relationship satisfaction include attitudes toward relationships and marriage, social influences, emotional intelligence, attachment styles, ways of resolving marital problems, length of marriage, type of marriage, and romantic jealousy. In addition, the factors that influence individuals’ decision-making processes regarding marriage and their attitudes toward marriage are also quite diverse. The positive or negative beliefs individuals develop about marriage, their thoughts about romantic relationships, their relationship satisfaction, and their behavioral patterns in relationships shape their attitudes toward marriage and the decisions they make [5].

Previous romantic relationship experiences play a decisive role in individuals’ subsequent relationships and marriage decisions [34, 35]. In this context, high or low levels of satisfaction in romantic relationships can positively or negatively influence attitudes toward marriage [36]. The current literature consistently shows that young adults with high relationship satisfaction develop more positive expectations, beliefs, and intentions toward the institution of marriage. This is supported by both cognitive and social psychology theories, emphasizing that positive relationship experiences shape long-term commitment and perceptions of marriage [37].

Attitudes toward romantic relationships and marriage are shaped more by the values and norms of the cultural context in which one lives than by individual preferences. In societies with strong collectivist tendencies, such as Türkiye, relationships are not merely a bond between individuals but also a social phenomenon that involves the approval of families and the social structure [38]. Accordingly, individuals’ romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage are related not only to their personal satisfaction but also to their level of conformity with social expectations. Cognitive flexibility can facilitate individuals in developing more harmonious attitudes in their relationships by balancing cultural norms with personal desires. Therefore, examining romantic relationship satisfaction, cognitive flexibility, and attitudes toward marriage together offers a unique contribution to understanding the interaction between individual and social values in Türkiye’s sociocultural structure.

Romantic relationship satisfaction not only reflects the emotional fulfillment and relational rewards individuals experience with their partners; it also serves as an important psychological resource that supports the development of constructive and positive attitudes toward marriage by enhancing cognitive flexibility and adaptability [39]. Therefore, it is predicted that romantic relationship satisfaction is a key determinant influencing marital attitudes and that this effect can be explained through cognitive flexibility.

Within this framework, the hypothesis of the present study is that there is a meaningful and positive relationship between the level of relationship satisfaction experienced by young adults in their romantic relationships and their attitudes toward marriage. High relationship satisfaction may positively influence individuals’ attitudes toward marriage, and this relationship can be explained through cognitive flexibility.

The mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage

Cognitive flexibility is considered not only a concept related to psychological problems, but also a fundamental component of an individual’s responses to events and situations and their overall thought structure [40]. In this context, an individual’s responses to new situations encountered in romantic relationships are directly related to their level of cognitive flexibility. Ellis [41] emphasized that irrational beliefs can lead to “emotional turmoil” in romantic relationships and that couples need to have high levels of cognitive awareness and flexibility to prevent such situations. Cognitive flexibility enables individuals to recognize various options in communication processes, allowing people with healthy communication skills to cope more effectively with difficulties in relationships [42].

Studies conducted on young adults in the literature indicate that numerous variables influence romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage. A study conducted by Topkaya et al. [11] found a negative relationship between irrational thoughts and relationship satisfaction among individuals with different thinking styles in romantic relationships. This finding is consistent with the existing literature suggesting that irrational beliefs may reduce relationship satisfaction in married or romantically involved individuals [2, 43–45].

Examining the attitudes and beliefs that influence young adults’ romantic relationships not only contributes to understanding individuals’ emotional lives but also provides a foundation for social improvement efforts aimed at strengthening the institution of the family [37, 46]. The literature shows that attitudes and beliefs towards romantic relationships are mostly evaluated through married individuals. However, considering that young people’s life experiences shape their attitudes towards marriage, studies conducted on single young people offer more comprehensive and diverse perspectives [47].

The existing literature indicates that individuals with high romantic relationship satisfaction have more positive attitudes toward marriage. However, it should be noted that romantic relationship satisfaction is not the only factor determining marital attitudes, and other variables that may accompany this effect should also be considered. In this context, cognitive flexibility emerges as an important mediating variable that can influence attitudes toward marriage. Martin and Anderson [14] defined cognitive flexibility through three core components: the individual’s awareness of alternative options, openness to change and flexibility, and confidence in their ability to be flexible. It is predicted that the presence of these components in individuals may positively influence their attitudes toward marriage. Furthermore, increased romantic relationship satisfaction contributes to the strengthening of these components.

Romantic relationship satisfaction reflects the level of fulfillment individuals experience in their relationships and the emotional rewards they derive from them. High relationship satisfaction allows individuals to use their emotional and cognitive resources more flexibly; in this context, cognitive flexibility plays an important role as the individual’s ability to adapt to different thoughts and perspectives. Therefore, increased romantic relationship satisfaction strengthens individuals’ cognitive flexibility, enabling them to be more effective in evaluating new ideas, resolving conflicts, and solving problems. Increased cognitive flexibility can directly influence marital attitudes in a positive way; individuals with flexible thinking can tolerate uncertainty, adapt to processes of spousal harmony, and develop positive expectations for the future. This mechanism demonstrates that the effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on marital attitudes is partially mediated through cognitive flexibility.

The theoretical basis for addressing cognitive flexibility in this study is based on individuals’ capacity to adapt their perceptions and behaviors in a consistent manner when faced with relational difficulties. Cognitive-behavioral and social-cognitive theories suggest that individuals with high cognitive flexibility can make more adaptive assessments, develop flexible strategies for problem solving, and thus exhibit higher relationship satisfaction and more positive attitudes. In this regard, examining cognitive flexibility alongside relationship satisfaction and marital attitudes allows for a theoretical understanding of cognitive adaptation processes that extend to both emotional and attitudinal dimensions.

In line with the literature, the main hypothesis of this study is that cognitive flexibility will play a meaningful and explanatory mediating role in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude in young adulthood. The present study aims to fill a gap in the literature by examining cognitive flexibility as a mediating variable to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitudes. The variables of romantic relationship satisfaction, marital attitudes, and cognitive flexibility were examined together in the present study, and the relationships between these variables were addressed from a holistic perspective. The study reveals the factors affecting satisfaction in romantic relationships and attitudes toward marriage, while also clarifying the mediating role of cognitive flexibility. The fact that no study in the literature has tested these three variables together using structural equation modeling (SEM) demonstrates that the research offers a unique contribution to the field.

Method

Participants

The data for this study was collected in January and February of the 2024–2025 academic year. To reach a wider participant base, the data collection process was conducted online, and the scales were presented to participants via a standardized Google Forms tool. The online link prepared for participation in the study was shared via email and digital messaging platforms. Participants were clearly informed that participation in the study was entirely voluntary, that the data obtained would be used solely for scientific purposes, and that it would not be shared with third parties. Participants were also informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time, and their voluntary participation consent was obtained.

The sample of the study consists of young adults residing in Türkiye who have experienced romantic relationships (240 women, 55%; 196 men, 45%). Convenience sampling, a non-random sampling method, was used to reach participants. This method was chosen to reach a broad sample of young adults from different cities across Türkiye via online platforms. Due to the nature of this sampling method, the generalizability of the findings is limited to individuals who participated in the study on a voluntary basis. This limitation is detailed in the Discussion/Limitations section of the study.

This method is based on the inclusion of individuals who are willing and accessible to participate in the study, and it enabled reaching participants from different cities through online platforms [48]. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 29, with a mean age of 24.0 (SD = 2.93).

The participants in this study are young adults aged 18–29. This age range corresponds to the critical young adulthood stage, during which individuals face fundamental developmental tasks such as identity development, forming close relationships, and gaining independence [8]. As Erikson [9] specifically noted, the period between the ages of 21 and 30 is a process in which the crisis of intimacy versus isolation is resolved and romantic relationships take on a decisive role. Therefore, in line with the objectives of the study, the focus is on this developmental period, during which relational issues such as relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage are most intense.

In this study, to ensure that the findings were not influenced by demographic differences, participants’ genders, ages, educational levels, and romantic relationship durations were controlled as covariate variables. This approach enabled the relationships between the key variables to be examined in a more reliable manner with higher internal validity.

Measures

The scales used in this study were not developed for this study. The scales were developed by other researchers and published elsewhere. References are provided under the relevant heading.

Relationship satisfaction

In this study, the Relationship Satisfaction Scale (RSS) was used to measure relationship satisfaction. The scale was developed by Hendrick [49] to measure romantic relationship satisfaction and was adapted into Turkish by Curun [50]. This scale has a single-factor structure, consists of 7 items, and uses a 7-point Likert-type rating scale. Items 4 and 7 on the scale are reverse-scored. High scores on the scale indicate a high level of satisfaction in individuals’ romantic relationships. In the present study, the Cronbach Alpha internal consistency coefficient for the scale was calculated as 0.95.

Attitude towards marriage

In this study, the Inonu Marriage Attitude Scale (IMAS) was used to measure attitudes toward marriage. Developed by Bayoğlu and Atli [51], the Inonu Marriage Attitude Scale (IMAS) is a single-factor instrument consisting of 21 items designed to measure individuals’ attitudes toward marriage. Applied with a 5-point Likert-type scale, this scale evaluates participants’ attitudes toward marriage by rating them from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” It is used specifically to determine attitudes toward the institution of marriage among young adults. The Inonu Marriage Attitude Scale (IMAS) is a tool developed to assess individuals’ attitudes toward marriage. The scale has a 5-point Likert-type rating system and measures participants’ thoughts about marriage. Scoring ranges from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree. The highest possible score on this scale is 105, and the lowest is 21. High scores reflect a positive attitude toward marriage, while low scores reflect a negative attitude. In the current study, the Cronbach Alpha internal consistency coefficient for the scale was calculated as 0.96.

Cognitive flexibility

In this study, the Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS) was used to measure cognitive flexibility. The Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS), developed by Dennis and Wal [52] and adapted into Turkish by Gülüm and Dağ [53] was used in the study to determine the levels of cognitive flexibility among young adults. This scale is a widely used assessment tool that has established validity and reliability in measuring cognitive flexibility.

The Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS) is a measure consisting of two subscales and a total of 20 items. These subscales consist of the “Alternatives” subscale with 13 items and the “Control” subscale with 7 items. The Alternatives subscale measures individuals’ ability to evaluate various behavioral options and generate solutions, while the Control subscale measures their tendency to perceive challenging situations as controllable. The items included in the Alternatives subscale are: 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18, 19, 20; while the items included in the Control subscale are: 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, 15, 17. The CFS is a 5-point Likert-type scale, and items 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, and 17 are reverse-scored. High scores on the inventory indicate that individuals have high levels of cognitive flexibility. This structure is a very useful and widely used tool for assessing individuals’ cognitive flexibility. The reliability coefficient of the CFS for this study was calculated as 0.97 Table 1.

Data processing and analysis

In the analysis of the data obtained in this study, descriptive statistics were used to examine the basic characteristics of the data, correlation analyses were performed to determine the relationships between variables, and appropriate analyses were conducted in terms of data normality, outliers, and missing values. The analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) software. The model created to test the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude was analyzed using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). The bootstrapping technique was applied to determine the significance of the mediating effect, and the lower and upper limits within the 95% confidence interval were calculated with 5000 resampling options. SEM analyses were performed using AMOS 24.0 (Analysis of Moment Structures) software. Structural equation modeling is a covariance-based analysis method that requires a large sample size to be used effectively [54]. Kline [55] has pointed out that a minimum of 200 participants is required for SEM analysis. In this context, the analyses conducted with 436 participants in the current study were considered statistically valid and sufficient. Additionally, participants’ gender, age, education level, and duration of romantic relationships were included in the model as covariate variables to control for potential demographic effects in the analyses. This was done to ensure that the results could be interpreted independently of demographic variables and to enhance the model’s internal validity.

Testing measurement models

In this study, measurement models were examined separately for each measurement tool used. As a result of the first-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the Romantic Relationship Satisfaction Scale, the initial model fit values were found to be inadequate. Therefore, the modifications suggested by the software were evaluated and applied incrementally until the fit values improved. The analysis yielded a chi-square value of 566.19 (df = 185, p < .001) and a χ²/df ratio of 3.06. Since this ratio was below 5, the model was determined to show acceptable fit. Other fit indices obtained were GFI = 0.98, CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.038, and SRMR = 0.02. These values indicate that the model meets the fit criteria proposed in the literature [56–59]. In conclusion, it can be stated that the single-factor structure of the Romantic Relationship Satisfaction Scale has been confirmed in the current data set.

The results of the first-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the Marriage Attitude Scale initially indicated that the model’s fit indices were not at an acceptable level. Accordingly, the modifications suggested by the software were systematically evaluated and implemented in stages until the target fit level was achieved. The analysis yielded a chi-square value of 528 (df = 183, p < .001) and a χ²/df ratio of 2.88. Since this ratio was below 5, the model was considered to fit well. Other fit indices were calculated as GFI = 0.89, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.06, and SRMR = 0.03. The findings indicate that the model is consistent with the goodness-of-fit criteria specified in the literature [56–59] and that the single-factor structure of the scale is confirmed by the current data.

First- and second-order confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) of the Cognitive Flexibility Scale yielded consistent results. The research model focused on the overall level of cognitive flexibility rather than its subscales. Therefore, second-order CFA was applied, and it was initially observed that the model’s fit indices were not at an acceptable level. To improve the fit values, the modifications suggested by the program were applied in sequence, and the necessary corrections were made. Ultimately, the chi-square value was found to be 470.89 (df = 166, p < .001) and the χ²/df ratio was 2.83; since this ratio was below 5, the model was determined to show acceptable fit. Other fit indices were calculated as GFI = 0.90, CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.06, and SRMR = 0.03. These findings indicate that the model meets the fit criteria proposed in the literature [56–59] and that the two-dimensional structure of the scale is validated by the current data.

Findings regarding testing research hypotheses

At this stage, a structural equation model containing latent variables was utilized to test the research hypotheses. First, a simple effects model was tested to determine the total effect of the romantic relationship satisfaction independent variable on marital attitude. Then, by including the cognitive flexibility mediator variable in the model, the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and cognitive flexibility, the relationship between cognitive flexibility and marital attitude, and the indirect effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on marital attitude through cognitive flexibility were investigated. This analysis process aims to test the validity of the model and evaluate the accuracy of the hypotheses by revealing the direct and indirect interactions between the variables.

According to the research findings, it was determined that the total effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on marital attitude (path c) was positive and significant (β = 0.87, 95% CI [0.69, 0.92], t = 2.636, p < .01). Furthermore, romantic relationship satisfaction explains approximately 76% (R² =0.758) of the variance in marital attitude. The results indicate that romantic relationship satisfaction positively and significantly predicts marital attitude among young adults.

According to the results of the mediational structural equation modeling analysis, it was found that the direct effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on the mediating variable of cognitive flexibility (path a) was positive and significant (β = 0.80, 95% CI [0.74, 0.85], t = 2.500, p < .05). Furthermore, romantic relationship satisfaction explains approximately 64% of the variance in cognitive flexibility (R² = 0.643). Based on the findings, romantic relationship satisfaction in young adulthood significantly predicts cognitive flexibility in a positive direction Fig. 2.

Similarly, it was found that the direct effect of cognitive flexibility, which is the mediating variable, on marital attitude (path b) was also positive and significant (β = 0.46, 95% CI [0.32, 0.60], t = 1.979, p < .05). Accordingly, cognitive flexibility in young adulthood significantly predicts marital attitude in a positive direction. After analyzing the total and direct effects, when the model was rerun with cognitive flexibility included as a mediating variable, the previously significant predictive relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude (β = 0.87, p < .001) retained its significance (β = 0.51, 95% CI [0.36, 0.62], t = 7.285, p < .001). Furthermore, cognitive flexibility explains approximately 26% of the variance in marital attitude (R² = 0.260) along with romantic relationship satisfaction.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude within the framework of a structural equation model incorporating a mediating effect. The analysis results showed a positive and statistically significant relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude during young adulthood. This finding supports Hypothesis 1: “Romantic relationship satisfaction positively and significantly affects attitudes toward marriage.”

This finding is consistent with the results in the existing literature. Karabacak and Çiftçi [37] noted that individuals in healthy romantic relationships have significantly more positive attitudes toward marriage. Similarly, Saraç et al. [2] found that decreased relationship satisfaction negatively affected attitudes toward marriage. The positive effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on attitudes toward marriage indicates that experiences gained in romantic relationships play an important role in shaping individuals’ perceptions and attitudes toward marriage. Indeed, Park and Rosen [5] state that romantic relationships have a decisive influence on marriage decisions and attitudes.

This strong relationship must be evaluated in light of the sociocultural structure, such as that of Türkiye where the research was conducted, where communitarian tendencies are dominant. In collectivist societies, relationships are not only a source of personal satisfaction between two individuals, but also a social institution that requires the approval of families and the social structure. In this context, the fact that romantic relationship satisfaction explains approximately 76% of the change in attitudes toward marriage (R2 = 0.758) reflects the strong interaction between these social expectations and individual satisfaction.

After examining the relationship between dependent and independent variables, cognitive flexibility was added to the model as a mediating variable. The findings showed a positive and statistically significant relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and cognitive flexibility (β = 0.80, p < .05). This result supports the second hypothesis: “Romantic relationship satisfaction predicts cognitive flexibility in a meaningful and positive direction.” This finding is consistent with the results of studies by Topkaya et al. [11], Asıcı and İkiz [60], Kurt and Gündüz [61]. High relationship satisfaction enhances the individual’s positive affect and motivation, leading to a more flexible thought structure. Cognitive flexibility plays a decisive role in strengthening social bonds and increasing relationship satisfaction [14].

The study examined the relationship between cognitive flexibility and marital attitude and found a positive and significant relationship (β = 0.46, p < .05). This finding supports the third hypothesis: “Cognitive flexibility positively and significantly affects marital attitude.” Individuals with high cognitive flexibility cope more effectively with relational and communicative difficulties; this flexibility allows individuals to stretch their emotional and cognitive responses by increasing their capacity to adapt to difficulties [62] and supports their attitudes toward marriage in a positive direction.

Finally, the study examined the mediating role of cognitive flexibility in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude. Bootstrapping analysis revealed that romantic relationship satisfaction had a significant indirect effect on marital attitude through cognitive flexibility (β = 0.36, p < .01). After including cognitive flexibility as a mediating variable in the model, the previously significant strong relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude (β = 0.87, p < .01) was found to remain significant (β = 0.51, p < .01) but at a lower level. This indicates that cognitive flexibility plays a partial mediation role. These findings confirm the final hypothesis, Hypothesis 4: “Cognitive flexibility mediates the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude.”

Interpreting the partial mediation result requires explaining why the direct effect remains strong and why cognitive flexibility cannot fully mediate.

The Persistence of Direct Effects and the Sample Effect: Research findings reveal that romantic relationship satisfaction has a stronger effect than cognitive flexibility in predicting marital attitudes. The fact that the entire participant group consisted of single young adults (aged 18–29) explains this situation. Young adulthood is a critical stage in which the crisis of “intimacy versus isolation” is resolved [9]. For these individuals, romantic relationship experience (RRS) is closer and more experiential than marriage (ATM), which has not yet become an institutionalized concrete experience. Therefore, high satisfaction from the current relationship acts as the primary factor that directly and powerfully nourishes positive attitudes toward marriage in the future, independent of cognitive processes. The preservation of the direct effect’s significance indicates that RID’s impact on Marriage Attitude is supported not only by cognitive adaptation (CF) but also by other elements such as emotional satisfaction and perceived social conformity.

Potential Mediating Variables That Could Explain the Remaining Variance: Although cognitive flexibility explains part of the total effect, there is still a significant direct effect. This situation indicates the existence of other psychological mechanisms that could explain the remaining variance between romantic relationship satisfaction and marital attitude. Future research should test these mechanisms using multiple mediation models. In this context, important potential mediating variables include: Irrational Relationship Beliefs. The literature shows that irrational beliefs negatively affect relationship satisfaction and reduce cognitive flexibility [11, 13]. It can be predicted that these beliefs will play a negative mediating role in the positive conversion of satisfaction into marital attitudes. Furthermore, attachment styles may also be a strong mediator; as previous romantic relationship experiences influence attachment styles [10] and, consequently, long-term commitment attitudes toward marriage [35].

These findings offer a new and original contribution to the literature on the role of cognitive flexibility in romantic relationships and attitudes toward marriage. This model emphasizes the importance of increasing cognitive flexibility to strengthen young adults’ positive attitudes toward marriage and prevent them from developing irrational beliefs. Increased cognitive flexibility strengthens positive attitudes toward marriage, and these positive attitudes support a healthy perception of marriage. In this context, it can be said that cognitive flexibility plays a role in increasing the effect of romantic relationship satisfaction on attitudes toward marriage and may be a protective factor against negative attitudes toward marriage in young people.

Conclusion

As a result of the analyses conducted, all hypotheses formulated in line with the study’s objective were confirmed. In this context, it was found that romantic relationship satisfaction (H1) in young adulthood significantly predicted marital attitude in a positive direction, that romantic relationship satisfaction (H2) predicted cognitive flexibility in a positive direction, and that cognitive flexibility (H3) also positively influenced marital attitude. The main hypothesis, H4, proved that cognitive flexibility plays a significant mediating role in the relationship between romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage. These findings reveal that increased romantic relationship satisfaction has direct and indirect positive effects on young adults’ attitudes toward marriage.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. The sample in this study was obtained using a non-random convenience sampling technique and is limited to individuals who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study via Google Forms. Due to this methodological choice, the generalizability of the findings to the entire population of young adults in Türkiye is limited. Future research may aim to increase the generalizability of these findings by employing random sampling methods and targeting broader populations. The fact that all data in the study were collected through participants’ self-reports, in a single session, and only in an online environment (Google Forms) poses a methodological limitation in the form of common method variance risk. This situation may cause the relationships between the measured constructs to be artificially inflated due to the effect of the single measurement method used. This risk is a natural limitation of the cross-sectional design of the study. To mitigate the impact of this risk, it is strongly recommended that design-based solutions be used in future research. In this context, data collection at different times (time-lagged designs) is recommended to test the causality of the relationship between variables more robustly and reduce the OMV effect, and it is recommended that data be collected at different time intervals (e.g., 2–3 months apart) for romantic relationship satisfaction, cognitive flexibility, and marital attitude. For the statistical control of OMV, using marker variables unrelated to the research variables in the model (e.g., perceived workload or social desirability) and isolating statistical variance through these variables will enhance the methodological robustness of the study. Future studies may use mixed methods approaches that combine different data sources, such as semi-structured interviews or observations, to overcome this limitation.

The generalizability of the findings is limited to young adults aged 18–29, and may be limited for individuals outside this age group. Future research could test the developed model on participants from different age groups (e.g., middle adulthood or older age) to examine its validity and age-related dynamics. This research was conducted with a sample of young adults in Türkiye. Since the relationships between romantic relationship satisfaction, attitudes toward marriage, and cognitive flexibility may be sensitive to cultural differences, it is recommended that the findings be tested in different cultural contexts.

Research findings indicate that the level of satisfaction experienced by young adults in their romantic relationships predicts a positive increase in their attitudes toward marriage. Accordingly, it is important to conduct studies aimed at increasing individuals’ satisfaction in romantic relationships. Research in the field reveals that as young individuals’ level of knowledge about partner selection in premarital romantic relationships increases, their romantic relationship satisfaction rises and their attitudes toward marriage become more positive. Therefore, universities or relevant institutions can collaborate to organize informative training sessions, seminars, or conferences led by experts on partner selection in romantic relationships and factors affecting the marriage process, targeting young people. This can increase young individuals’ awareness of marriage, supporting them in making more conscious and healthy decisions.

To support young adults’ cognitive flexibility and positively develop their attitudes toward marriage, individual or group psychological counseling programs can be implemented where individuals can share the satisfaction they derive from their romantic relationship experiences, the positive and negative emotional processes they experience, and receive professional support. Such psychological counseling services can enable young individuals to evaluate their relational experiences in a healthier way, contribute to the development of their cognitive flexibility, and thus positively shape their attitudes toward marriage.

Enhancing young adults’ attitudes toward marriage and fostering their cognitive flexibility necessitates awareness and access to premarital relationship counseling services. Research indicates that romantic experiences prior to marriage shape young adults’ future expectations and marital perceptions, which are linked to their cognitive flexibility. Accessing counseling services may help individuals better understand themselves and their partners, make informed marital decisions, and develop coping strategies for negative emotions. Integrating such services within universities and educational institutions can promote healthy marital attitudes and support the development of cognitive flexibility.

Research indicates that unhappy family environments and parental separation are linked to negative marital attitudes among young people, whereas higher family happiness enhances cognitive flexibility. Mitigating the impact of adverse family experiences is crucial for supporting psychological development. Interventions that improve family relationships, including family and individual counseling, can positively influence cognitive flexibility and marital attitudes. Expanding the accessibility of online and in-person counseling services may further support young adults in strengthening their cognitive flexibility.

Recommendations for researchers

This study investigated the relationships between romantic relationship satisfaction, attitudes toward marriage, and cognitive flexibility using a quantitative approach. For future research, it is recommended that qualitative methods also be used to develop a deeper understanding of these variables. Furthermore, since the study was conducted with young adults in Türkiye, testing the model on different cultural samples would increase the generalizability of the findings.

In this study, cognitive flexibility and romantic relationship satisfaction were examined as predictors of attitudes toward marriage. Future studies are recommended to include additional psychological, social, and cultural variables in the model and to examine interactions between variables in larger samples. On the other hand, the fact that the study was limited to the 18–29 age group highlights the need to test the model in different age groups.

However, it is important that future research not be limited to offering general recommendations, but rather develop concrete and structured intervention programs aimed at increasing cognitive flexibility. For example, an 8-week Cognitive Flexibility Development Program (CFDP) could be designed for young adults. This program could include cognitive reappraisal training, cognitive exercises that develop executive functions, mindfulness-based attention and emotion regulation exercises, and relationship-based problem-solving and perspective-taking workshops.

Future studies could test the extent to which such intervention programs increase cognitive flexibility using pre-test–post-test experimental designs and whether they create meaningful changes in romantic relationship satisfaction and attitudes toward marriage. Furthermore, comparing different types of interventions, such as digital cognitive training applications, mindfulness-based interventions, or relationship education programs, will reveal which methods are more effective in developing cognitive flexibility.

Such specific intervention-based research will both provide the opportunity to test causal relationships between variables more robustly and offer practical program recommendations for mental health professionals and relationship counselors.
