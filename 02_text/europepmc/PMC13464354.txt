ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLow levels of physical activity among university students have become a persistent public health concern. Although regular exercise is well known to benefit both physical and mental health, substantial individual differences remain in exercise affective experiences and participation behaviors. Prior research has not sufficiently clarified how university students differ in their exercise intensity preference and tolerance, nor how such differences relate to exercise affective experiences and physical activity. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of exercise intensity preference and tolerance among university students and to examine differences across profiles in physical activity levels and exercise affective experiences.MethodsA total of 1,274 university students (Mage = 19.5 years, SD = 1.21) from a university in China were recruited. Participants completed online self-report measures assessing exercise intensity preference and tolerance, physical activity levels, and exercise affective experiences.ResultsFive latent profiles were identified based on the Preference for and Tolerance of Exercise Intensity Questionnaire: Low-Intensity Rejection Type (LIRT, 4.5%), High-Intensity Inclined Type (HIIT, 57.8%), Extreme Low-Intensity Avoidance Type (ELAT, 4%), High-Intensity Dominant Type (HIDT, 5.7%), and Moderate Balanced Type (MBT, 27.9%). Compared with other profiles, the High-Intensity Dominant Type showed the highest levels of physical activity, competence, vitality, pleasure, and attraction, and the lowest level of tension. The Extreme Low-Intensity Avoidance Type scored lowest on most positive affective indicators. The Low-Intensity Rejection Type had the lowest physical activity level, with no significant difference from the Extreme Low-Intensity Avoidance Type.ConclusionFocusing on two psychological characteristics—exercise intensity preference and tolerance—this study examined their associations with physical activity behavior and exercise affective experiences. The findings offer a new perspective for understanding insufficient physical activity among university students and provide empirical support for more targeted exercise interventions, differentiated instruction, and personalized exercise guidance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04953-0.

BODY
Introduction

Low levels of physical activity among university students have gradually become a global health concern. Global evidence indicates that approximately three-quarters to four-fifths of young people (including college students) do not achieve the World Health Organization’s recommendations for moderate-to-vigorous physical activity [21, 22]. Notably, Physical activity levels among university students have been consistently reported to decline during the transition from adolescence to early adulthood, making this developmental period a critical window for understanding changes in health behaviors [1]. This transition is often accompanied by increased academic pressure, prolonged sedentary behavior, and reduced participation in structured physical activity, all of which may further shape how university students perceive and respond to different exercise intensities [7]. Common factors contributing to insufficient physical activity in this population include time constraints, academic pressure, limited exercise awareness, and inadequate access to facilities [37, 46, 50, 55]. In the Chinese context, these challenges may be further amplified by the highly competitive academic environment and culturally embedded emphasis on academic achievement. University students often experience sustained academic pressure and structured schedules, which may not only limit available time for exercise but also shape their preferences toward lower-intensity or more convenient forms of physical activity. Importantly, insufficient physical activity not only affects students’ physical health but is also negatively associated with mental health and quality of life [12, 26, 51].

Although numerous studies have documented generally low physical activity levels among university students, focusing solely on behavioral quantity does not adequately explain why some students actively engage in physical activity while others remain persistently inactive [30, 56]. Increasing attention has therefore been given to individuals’ subjective affective experiences and cognitive appraisals during exercise, as these psychological factors largely determine whether a person is willing to participate again [17, 43]. Particularly in moderate-to-high intensity exercise contexts, individuals show marked differences in affective responses to the same intensity stimulus: some report feelings of vitality and pleasure, whereas others experience discomfort or even aversion [24, 41]. Such experiential differences are often closely related to prior exercise habits, self-efficacy, and expectations toward exercise [2, 47, 54]. Understanding these subjective differences across exercise intensities is therefore a key step in explaining variability in physical activity participation.

However, when examining why individuals exhibit markedly different affective responses to the same exercise intensity, researchers have increasingly recognized that the key lies not in the objective intensity itself, but in how that intensity is subjectively experienced. In this context, Ekkekakis et al. [15, 16] introduced two central constructs—exercise intensity preference and tolerance—and developed the PRETIE-Q to assess individuals’ subjective selection tendencies toward different intensity levels as well as their capacity to persist when discomfort arises during exercise [16]. Within this framework, exercise intensity preference primarily reflects the intensity level an individual is inclined to choose, representing a psychological tendency grounded in anticipated pleasure. In contrast, exercise intensity tolerance emphasizes the ability to continue exercising after physiological or affective discomfort has emerged. Subsequent research has shown that although these two dimensions are related, they are not equivalent: individuals who prefer high intensity may not necessarily tolerate the discomfort it brings, while those with high tolerance do not always actively seek high-intensity exercise [13]. Moreover, when actual exercise intensity aligns with an individual’s preference and tolerance characteristics, positive affective experiences are more likely to occur, thereby promoting adherence to exercise; conversely, mismatched intensity settings are more likely to elicit negative affective responses and increase the risk of dropout [40].

Physical activity is not only a physiological behavioral process but also accompanied by complex and multidimensional affective experiences, which largely influence whether individuals are willing to engage in exercise again and sustain participation over time [25]. Early studies often assessed exercise affect using a single pleasure–displeasure dimension, but with advances in measurement tools, researchers have increasingly conceptualized exercise affective experiences as a multidimensional structure that includes pleasure, vitality, perceived competence, and relaxation [18]. Evidence suggests that positive affective responses and enjoyable experiences during exercise are significantly associated with subsequent physical activity adherence, whereas negative experiences such as tension, discomfort, and fatigue substantially increase the likelihood of dropout [39, 45, 59]. Longitudinal studies further indicate that positive affective experiences during exercise can significantly predict future participation in physical activity, whereas negative experiences raise the probability of discontinuation [27]. Among university students, positive affective experiences during exercise not only predict subsequent exercise frequency and intention to persist but are also associated with higher life satisfaction and psychological well-being [10, 43]. In addition, affective experiences have been regarded as an important psychological pathway linking physical activity to mental health benefits, with mechanisms that extend beyond mere physiological adaptation [42].

Importantly, the multidimensional structure of exercise affective experiences provides a nuanced framework for capturing these variations in affective responses across different exercise contexts. Within this framework, dimensions such as pleasure, vitality, and tension can be understood as specific manifestations of the broader affective patterns described by theoretical models such as the Dual-Mode Theory. According to the Dual-Mode Theory, at higher exercise intensities, physiological cues and cognitive appraisal jointly shape affective responses, and individuals’ psychological characteristics regulate how these affective experiences unfold [14]. When actual exercise intensity aligns with an individual’s preference and tolerance levels, negative affective responses are effectively buffered, while pleasure, vitality, and a sense of control are notably enhanced [5]. In contrast, when the intensity exceeds what an individual finds acceptable, even if it objectively falls within “health-recommended” levels, feelings of fatigue, tension, and discomfort are more likely to arise [35]. In addition, evidence suggests that when core pleasant exercise affective experiences are consistent with strong exercise attraction, exercise behavior over the following 14 days can be predicted [6]. Taken together, it is necessary to consider individuals’ preference for exercise intensity and the associated tolerance of exercise intensity, as this perspective helps explain differences in exercise affective experiences as well as behavioral differences in exercise tendency and adherence.

Although prior research has revealed associations between exercise intensity preference and tolerance, affective experiences, and physical activity, most studies have relied on traditional variable-centered approaches that emphasize linear relationships between variables and behavioral outcomes while overlooking the combined psychological characteristics that differentiate individuals. Particularly among university students, there is still a lack of systematic investigation into the possible combination patterns of exercise intensity preference and tolerance and how these patterns relate to differences in affective experiences and behavior. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), as a person-centered approach, can identify subgroups with similar psychological characteristics based on multiple indicators, thereby uncovering heterogeneity within the population [34]. Compared with variable-centered approaches, LPA allows for the examination of how multiple dimensions jointly operate within individuals rather than isolating independent effects, making it particularly suitable for capturing the combined patterns of preference for and tolerance of exercise intensity. From the perspective of the Dual-Mode Theory, as exercise intensity increases, affective responses become less dominated by cognitive appraisal and more influenced by physiological signals. However, this shift does not occur uniformly across individuals; rather, it is regulated by their distinct psychological characteristics, resulting in different affective experiences.

Based on the above considerations, this study aims to apply LPA to identify latent profiles of university students based on exercise intensity preference and tolerance and to compare differences among these profiles in physical activity levels and multiple dimensions of exercise affective experiences. This approach provides empirical support for developing more individualized exercise interventions. Based on this rationale, we propose the following hypotheses: (1) university students exhibit distinct latent profiles in exercise intensity preference and tolerance; (2) significant differences exist among profiles in exercise intensity preference and tolerance, physical activity levels, and exercise affective experiences; and (3) individuals with higher exercise intensity preference and tolerance demonstrate higher levels of physical activity, more exercise positive affective experiences, and fewer exercise negative affective experiences.

Methods

Participants

This study recruited 1,295 university students from a comprehensive public university in central China, with participants recruited across the whole campus to ensure diversity in academic backgrounds. Participants were currently enrolled university students able to complete the questionnaire independently, with no additional restrictions on exercise experience, health status, or age; and, the sample represents the general student population. After excluding 21 cases due to incomplete demographic information, data from 1,274 participants were retained for analysis. The final sample included 668 males (52.4%) and 606 females (47.6%), with a mean age of 19.5 years (SD = 1.21). The effective response rate was approximately 98.4%.

Procedure

Data were collected through an online self-report survey. Prior to participation, all students provided informed consent and were informed about the confidentiality and purpose of the study. To minimize potential social desirability bias, participants were explicitly informed that there were no right or wrong answers and were encouraged to respond honestly based on their actual experiences. In addition, no identifying information was collected, and the survey was completed independently, reducing potential evaluation concerns. Participants were also informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the institutional ethics committee.

Measures

Exercise Intensity Preference and Tolerance: Preference for and tolerance of exercise intensity were assessed using the Preference for and Tolerance of Exercise Intensity Questionnaire (PRETIE-Q). The questionnaire consists of 16 items across four dimensions: preference for low exercise intensity (4 items), preference for high exercise intensity (4 items), low tolerance of exercise intensity (4 items), and high tolerance of exercise intensity (4 items). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating stronger endorsement. The preference for low exercise intensity and low tolerance of exercise intensity dimensions were reverse-scored prior to analysis. The scale has been validated in Chinese university student populations [58]. In the present study, the overall reliability coefficient was 0.843, and the reliability coefficients for the four subscales were 0.787, 0.763, 0.749, and 0.720, respectively.

Exercise Affective Experiences: The Chinese version of the Affective Exercise Experiences Questionnaire (AFFEXX-C) was originally developed by Ekkekakis et al. [18] and later adapted into Chinese by Wang et al. [52]. The questionnaire consists of 30 items across eight dimensions and assesses three overarching components: antecedent appraisals, core affective exercise experiences, and exercise motivation [52]. The eight dimensions specifically include identification, competence, showing off, attitudes toward group exercise (liking vs disliking), energy (vitality), pleasure, tension, and exercise attraction. Items are presented as pairs of contrasting statements describing attitudes and perceptions about exercise experiences (e.g., “Exercise is something I dread” vs. “Exercise is something I look forward to”). Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“the statement on the left fully describes what you want to say”) to 7 (“the statement on the right fully describes what you want to say”). Higher scores on each factor indicate more positive antecedent appraisals, more positive core affective exercise experiences, or greater attraction to exercise (as opposed to aversion). In the present study, the reliability coefficient was 0.807.

Physical Activity: Physical activity was assessed using the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire (GLTEQ; [20]). The GLTEQ includes three items measuring the frequency of mild, moderate, and strenuous exercise performed for more than 15 min during a typical week. Total physical activity was calculated by multiplying the weekly frequencies of mild, moderate, and strenuous activities by 3, 5, and 9 metabolic equivalents, respectively, and summing the results, following the standard scoring procedure of the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire, where these metabolic equivalents values represent typical energy expenditures for different intensity levels. Higher scores indicate higher levels of physical activity. Although the GLTEQ is widely used and demonstrates acceptable reliability, it relies on self-reported data and may be subject to reporting biases.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses for exercise intensity preference, exercise intensity tolerance, physical activity, and affective exercise experiences were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was then performed in Mplus 8.0 to identify subgroups based on exercise intensity preference and tolerance, with model selection guided by multiple fit indices. Specifically, lower values of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC) indicate better model fit. Significant results for the Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) suggest that a model with k classes fits the data significantly better than a model with k–1 classes. Entropy, ranging from 0 to 1, was used as an index of classification accuracy, with higher values indicating clearer classification; values above 0.80 suggest classification accuracy exceeding 90%. Finally, multivariate analyses of variance were conducted to examine differences across the identified profiles in all study variables and their respective dimensions.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis of variables

To assess common method bias from self-report measures, Harman’s single-factor test was performed. The first unrotated factor explained 28.31% of the total variance, below the 40% threshold, indicating that common method bias was not a serious concern.

The correlation analysis indicated generally stable relationships among exercise intensity preference and tolerance, physical activity, and exercise affective experiences.

First, examining the internal relationships among the PRETIE-Q subscales, a significant positive correlation was observed between high preference and high tolerance (r = 0.68, p < 0.01), and a similarly strong positive correlation was found between low preference and low tolerance (r = 0.63, p < 0.01). In contrast, low preference combined with high tolerance showed a significant negative correlation (r = − 0.30, p < 0.01), suggesting that preference and tolerance form partially aligned but distinct dimensions rather than a single continuum.

Second, with respect to physical activity, both high preference (r = 0.24, p < 0.01) and high tolerance (r = 0.28, p < 0.01) were significantly positively associated with physical activity levels, whereas low preference exhibited a weak negative correlation with physical activity (r = − 0.07, p < 0.05). These findings indicate that individuals who are more inclined toward and capable of sustaining higher intensity exercise tend to engage in more frequent physical activity.

Third, regarding exercise affective experience dimensions, high preference and high tolerance were systematically positively correlated with multiple indicators of positive affect, particularly in core dimensions such as Energy, Pleasure, and Attraction (r range: approximately 0.25–0.41). In contrast, low preference was negatively associated with these positive affect dimensions (e.g., Pleasure: r = − 0.21; Attraction: r = − 0.09), suggesting a less favorable affective experience during exercise.

Finally, physical activity was significantly positively correlated with Pleasure (r = 0.32), Attraction (r = 0.41), Perceived Competence (r = 0.30), and Energy (r = 0.29), while showing negative correlations with Group Aversion (r = − 0.31) and Tension (r = − 0.24). Together, these patterns support the interrelated roles of intensity related characteristics, behavioral engagement, and affective experiences in physical activity. Latent profile analysis of exercise intensity preference and tolerance

A latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted on college students’ exercise intensity preference and tolerance, comparing model fit across 1- to 6-class solutions. As the number of classes increased, the Log(L) continued to rise, while AIC, BIC, and aBIC gradually decreased, indicating progressively improved model fit. However, the selection of the latent class model should not rely solely on fit indices; statistical significance, classification quality, and class stability must also be considered.

Results from the LMR and BLRT tests showed that the 2-class models to 6-class models each represented a significant improvement over the preceding model, suggesting that increasing the number of classes was statistically meaningful. Regarding classification accuracy, the 5-class models and 6-class models demonstrated high entropy values of 0.823 and 0.845, respectively, indicating good class separation. However, further examination of the class distributions revealed that the additional class in the 6-class model accounted for only 1.8% of the sample, falling below the commonly recommended minimum class size of approximately 5% in latent profile analysis. This suggests that the additional class may result from over-splitting of an existing class, offering limited theoretical distinction. Specifically, the additional class largely reflected a fragmentation of an existing profile rather than a substantively meaningful subgroup. Based on these considerations—statistical indices, classification quality, and interpretability—the 5-class model was ultimately selected as the optimal latent profile structure for college students’ exercise intensity preference and tolerance. Profile 1 scored relatively low on high preference and high tolerance while showing comparatively higher scores on low preference and low tolerance, suggesting that this group tends to avoid high-intensity exercise and has limited capacity to tolerate exercise intensity. This profile was therefore labeled Low-Intensity Rejection Type (LIRT), accounting for 4.5% of the sample.

Profile 2 demonstrated relatively balanced scores across all four dimensions, with overall levels slightly above the mid-range, indicating a moderate tendency toward and adaptability for high-intensity exercise. This profile was labeled High-Intensity Inclined Type (HIIT), comprising 57.8% of the sample.

Profile 3 scored highest on low preference and low tolerance and lowest on high preference and high tolerance, reflecting a strongly low-intensity orientation and strong aversion to high-intensity exercise. This profile represents a typical high-intensity avoidance group and was labeled Extreme Low-Intensity Avoidance Type (ELAT), accounting for 4% of the sample.

Profile 4 scored significantly higher on high preference and high tolerance than other profiles, while scoring lower on low preference and low tolerance. This indicates that members of this group not only prefer high-intensity exercise but also possess a high capacity to tolerate exercise intensity. This profile was labeled High-Intensity Dominant Type (HIDT), representing 5.7% of the sample.

Profile 5 showed overall scores slightly above the mid-level, with high preference slightly lower than low preference, and high tolerance slightly higher than low tolerance. This pattern does not indicate a strong intensity preference, suggesting a relatively stable and neutral adaptability to different exercise intensities. This profile was labeled Moderate Balanced Type (MBT), accounting for 27.9% of the sample.

Profile differences in exercise intensity preference and tolerance, physical activity, and exercise affective experiences

The results of ANOVA indicated that the latent profiles of preference for and tolerance of exercise intensity differed significantly across all dimensions, including preference for and tolerance of exercise intensity, physical activity, and exercise affective experiences (Fs = 4.65–1033.56, ps < 0.001, η2 = 0.01–0.77).

Regarding the preference for and tolerance of exercise intensity dimensions, all five profiles showed significant differences, further supporting the validity of the five-profile model. On the low preference dimension, ELAT scored the highest (M = 17.71), significantly exceeding the other four profiles, whereas LIRT scored the lowest (M = 7.78), with the difference between the two being significant (p < 0.05). On the high preference dimension, HIDT scored the highest (M = 15.64), significantly higher than the other profiles, while ELAT scored the lowest (M = 5.27). Similar patterns were observed on the low-tolerance and high tolerance dimensions, with ELAT scoring highest on low tolerance (M = 17.42) and HIDT scoring highest on high tolerance (M = 16.02), both significantly exceeding the remaining profiles.

Regarding physical activity, HIDT exhibited the highest activity levels (M = 48.11), whereas LIRT showed the lowest (M = 19.43). High-Intensity Inclined Type and MBT were at moderate levels, with no significant differences between them.

In terms of exercise affective experiences, the profiles differed significantly across all affective dimensions. HIDT scored significantly higher than the other profiles on positive affect dimensions, including Perceived Competence (M = 21.84), Energy (M = 14.94), Pleasure (M = 16.44), and Attraction (M = 25.72), while showing the lowest score on Tension (M = 7.66). In contrast, ELAT scored relatively low on most positive affect dimensions, such as Energy (M = 9.75) and Pleasure (M = 10.69), significantly lower than HIDT.

In addition to statistical significance, the magnitude of between profile differences varied substantially across variables. The η2 values ranged from small (e.g., η2 = 0.01 for liking vs. disliking group exercise) to very large (e.g., η2 = 0.77 for high preference for exercise intensity), indicating that not all significant differences were equally meaningful. Specifically, variables directly reflecting exercise intensity preference and tolerance showed large effect sizes, suggesting that the identified profiles were clearly differentiated along these core dimensions. In contrast, some affective outcomes, such as liking versus disliking group exercise, exhibited relatively small effect sizes, indicating that although differences between profiles reached statistical significance, their practical magnitude was limited. Discussion

This study focused on university students and used latent profile analysis to examine heterogeneity in exercise intensity preference and tolerance, as well as how these profiles relate to physical activity levels and affective exercise experiences. Correlation analyses first showed that exercise intensity preference and tolerance were significantly associated overall. Higher preference and higher tolerance were both positively related to physical activity and several positive affective dimensions, whereas lower preference was negatively associated with physical activity and some positive affective dimensions. Physical activity, in turn, was positively associated with multiple positive affective dimensions and negatively associated with some negative affective dimensions. Building on these findings, latent profile analysis revealed that students did not differ along a simple continuum of high versus low scores. Instead, five distinct profiles emerged, each characterized by a unique configuration of preference and tolerance: Low-Intensity Rejection Type, High-Intensity Inclined Type, Extreme Low-Intensity Avoidance Type, High-Intensity Dominant Type, and Moderate Balanced Type. These profiles showed clear differences in how preference and tolerance were combined within individuals. Further analyses of variance indicated significant differences among the profiles in exercise intensity preference, exercise intensity tolerance, physical activity, and all dimensions of affective exercise experiences. Specifically, ELAT scored highest on low preference and low tolerance, whereas HIDT scored highest on high preference, high tolerance, physical activity, and most positive affective dimensions, while scoring lowest on certain negative affective dimensions. While the analyses of variance results revealed statistically significant differences across profiles for all variables, the magnitude of these differences varied substantially. In particular, some affective outcomes (e.g., liking versus disliking group exercise) showed relatively small effect sizes, suggesting that these differences may have limited practical significance. This pattern may indicate that certain affective responses are less strongly shaped by intensity preference and tolerance, and may instead be influenced by contextual or social factors beyond individual intensity-related characteristics. Overall, these findings highlight substantial heterogeneity in how university students perceive and respond to exercise intensity and demonstrate that different configurations of preference and tolerance are meaningfully associated with both behavioral engagement and affective exercise experiences, providing a foundation for theoretical interpretation in the following sections.

Consistent with previous research, this study also found a systematic association between exercise intensity preference and tolerance. This finding echoes the view proposed by Ekkekakis et al. [15, 16]: preference for exercise intensity primarily reflects an individual’s subjective tendency to choose certain exercise intensities, whereas tolerance of exercise intensity represents the capacity to persist once discomfort arises. Although related, the two constructs are not functionally identical at the psychological level [16]. This pattern of association may stem from individuals gradually forming stable psychological evaluations of intensity stimuli through repeated exercise experiences, such that “willingness to choose” and “ability to endure” become aligned to some extent while still retaining distinct functions. In addition, exercise intensity preference and tolerance are important individual-difference variables in predicting physical activity participation. Both a greater tendency to choose higher intensity exercise and a stronger capacity to tolerate exercise induced discomfort were significantly associated with higher frequency and overall levels of physical activity, a pattern that appears particularly stable among university students [52].

Regarding affective exercise experiences, higher exercise intensity preference, higher exercise intensity tolerance, and physical activity were all significantly positively correlated with Competence, Showing of, Energy, Pleasure, and Attraction, and significantly negatively correlated with disidentification, disliking group exercise, and tension. Similar to earlier findings, the subjective emotional responses individuals experience during exercise serve as key psychological mechanisms influencing subsequent participation and adherence [29]. Likewise, Rodrigues et al. [44] reported that individuals with higher preference and tolerance experienced less competence frustration during exercise, and that higher preference was positively associated with competence satisfaction, which facilitates the formation of stable exercise habits [44]. Other studies have also shown that feelings of energy and competence during exercise are critical affective signals predicting future adherence, whereas tension and fatigue are often linked to dropout behaviors [19, 32]. It is worth noting that low tolerance of exercise intensity showed relatively weak or non-significant associations with physical activity and most affective dimensions. Tolerance of exercise intensity is not a psychological trait that operates uniformly across all stages of exercise participation; rather, its function becomes evident primarily in situations where exercise intensity exceeds an individual’s preference or comfort zone and produces noticeable discomfort [8]. Prior research indicates that when exercise intensity matches one’s preference or remains below the tolerance threshold, the need for tolerance is rarely activated, thereby weakening its association with physical activity and affective experiences [48]. Given that most daily physical activity among university students occurs at intensities that do not trigger substantial physiological discomfort, situations requiring “tolerance” may not be fully activated [9]. Overall, these correlation patterns illustrate from multiple angles how psychological characteristics related to exercise intensity are linked to physical activity behavior and affective exercise experiences, providing an empirical basis for the subsequent person-centered analysis of exercise intensity preference and tolerance.

In addition, this study found that university students did not differ along a simple linear continuum in exercise intensity preference and tolerance. Instead, they formed several distinct psychological patterns with clear combinational characteristics, which could be classified into five latent profiles. Meanwhile, the results of the ANOVA were highly consistent with the structure shown in the profile plot, further indicating that these five profiles differed significantly along the pathway of exercise intensity preference – exercise intensity tolerance – exercise affective experiences – physical activity behavior. Across the four dimensions of exercise intensity preference and tolerance, ELAT and HIDT represented two very clear poles: ELAT scored highest on low exercise intensity preference and low exercise intensity tolerance, whereas HIDT scored highest on high exercise intensity preference and high exercise intensity tolerance. This pattern indirectly supports the appropriateness of adopting a five-profile solution. Previous studies have shown that individuals do not passively accept intensity stimuli during exercise; rather, they actively select and regulate exercise intensity based on subjective acceptability and discomfort thresholds. This psychological matching of exercise intensity, to some extent, shapes subsequent exercise affective experiences and the sustainability of physical activity behavior [36, 38]. From this perspective, the significant differences in exercise intensity preference and tolerance across profiles provide person-centered methodological support for prior findings.

More specifically, LIRT scored relatively low on high exercise intensity preference and high exercise intensity tolerance, and also had the lowest score in physical activity. The co-occurrence of low preference and low tolerance suggests a generalized disengagement from exercise intensity. Rather than merely disliking high intensity, these individuals show low anticipated reward and low perceived coping capacity for exercise discomfort. In other words, they neither prefer high-intensity exercise nor can they comfortably endure the discomfort that may arise during exercise. At the affective level, when individuals in this group face an intensity that exceeds their psychological matching level, they are more likely to experience negative affective responses earlier in the exercise process, such as fatigue, tension, or displeasure, thereby reducing the likelihood of repeating future exercise behavior [35]. Thus, the LIRT profile represents a subgroup with both avoidance tendencies and reduced psychological readiness for demanding activities. This combination increases vulnerability to early dropout, as even moderate intensity increases may exceed their motivational and tolerance thresholds.

In contrast, High-Intensity Inclined Type although less pronounced than HIDT in high exercise intensity preference and tolerance, still scored above the moderate level overall, showing a relatively mild inclination toward high intensity and a good capacity for tolerance. Similarly, High-Intensity Inclined Type ranked just below HIDT on most affective and behavioral indicators, which is consistent with its relatively balanced and moderately high scores across the four exercise intensity dimensions. These findings suggest that this group has a certain level of acceptance and adaptability to higher exercise intensity and typically does not exhibit extremely positive or negative affective responses. Their exercise participation may depend more on situational and motivational factors—such as group atmosphere and goal setting—rather than on intensity itself [3, 49]. This further indicates that exercise intensity preference and tolerance do not influence physical activity behavior in a simple linear manner, but may be mediated by exercise affective experiences or other external factors.

It is particularly noteworthy that the ELAT scored highest on low exercise intensity preference and low exercise intensity tolerance, while scoring lowest on high exercise intensity preference and high exercise intensity tolerance, forming a pronounced low-intensity orientation. Correspondingly, ELAT also showed relatively low levels of physical activity, with no significant difference from the LIRT. Individuals in this group are unwilling to engage in high-intensity activities and are almost unable to tolerate any discomfort during exercise. Even slightly elevated intensity stimuli can easily trigger strong negative experiences, such as fatigue and anxiety, which further undermines their persistence in exercise. This finding suggests that the key barrier for this group may not be physical capacity, but a low threshold for negative affective responses, which rapidly discourages continued engagement. In contrast, the HIDT demonstrated the highest scores in high exercise intensity preference, high exercise intensity tolerance, and physical activity, while scoring low on low exercise intensity preference and tolerance, reflecting a high-intensity matching pattern. These individuals are not only willing to choose high-intensity exercise but are also able to persist despite discomfort, making them more likely to experience positive affective responses in high-intensity contexts, such as a sense of accomplishment and vigor, and thus to maintain or even pursue higher-intensity exercise. This suggests that the alignment between preference and tolerance may function as a reinforcing mechanism, whereby positive affective feedback further consolidates high-intensity exercise habits. This is consistent with previous findings showing that individuals who can accept higher exercise intensity while maintaining relatively comfortable experiences are more likely to develop stable exercise participation patterns, whereas those who avoid high intensity or frequently experience discomfort during exercise are more likely to interrupt or reduce participation [4, 54].

Although the physical activity level of the ELAT group was not the lowest overall, it was clearly lower than that of HIDT, suggesting that avoiding high exercise intensity is not equivalent to being inactive, but it does constrain exercise frequency and sustainability. Importantly, this distinction indicates that interventions for this group may benefit more from affect regulation strategies and gradual exposure to tolerable intensity levels, rather than simply promoting increased activity volume. At the level of exercise affective experiences, HIDT scored highest on perceived competence, vigor, pleasure, and attraction, while reporting the lowest tension. In contrast, ELAT scored lowest on most positive affective indicators. This pattern is highly consistent with the predictions of the Dual-Mode Theory, which proposes that when exercise intensity is within an individual’s tolerable range, affective responses are primarily shaped by cognitive appraisal and tend to be positive; however, when intensity approaches or exceeds the tolerable threshold, affective responses are increasingly driven by physiological strain, leading to discomfort and tension [13, 33, 53]. From this perspective, the balance between cognitive appraisal and physiological cues may differ substantially across latent profiles. Individuals in the HIDT, due to their higher exercise intensity preference and tolerance, may continue to rely more on positive cognitive appraisal processes—such as perceived competence, challenge, and self-control—even under relatively demanding exercise conditions, thereby buffering the negative influence of physiological discomfort. In contrast, individuals in the ELAT may become more sensitive to physiological cues such as fatigue and tension at relatively lower intensity thresholds, allowing physiological discomfort to override positive cognitive appraisal more rapidly and resulting in fewer positive affective experiences during exercise [23, 31, 57]. More broadly, this pattern supports the notion that individual differences in intensity-related characteristics shape not only immediate affective responses but also longer-term behavioral trajectories in physical activity.

Finally, positioned between these extremes is MBT, which scored at moderate levels across all four dimensions and did not display a pronounced bias toward or advantage in any particular intensity orientation. This suggests that this group holds a relatively neutral and stable subjective attitude toward exercise intensity: they do not prefer very high intensity, yet they possess a certain capacity to tolerate it. This profile may correspond to students who participate in physical education classes or daily exercise at a moderate level, neither overly enthusiastic nor avoidant. Compared to more extreme profiles, the MBT pattern reflects a neutral, flexible orientation toward exercise intensity. Lacking clear preferences or tolerance thresholds, individuals show no strong affective or behavioral anchors. This suggests MBT may represent a context sensitive group, more susceptible to situational influences (e.g., social environment, task framing, motivational cues). Without a strong intensity preference, participation depends more on external conditions than internal regulation. It remains unclear whether this profile is stable or transitional. Future longitudinal research could clarify if individuals shift toward defined profiles or maintain this balance.

Overall, the differences observed in physical activity behavior and exercise affective experiences across the profiles align closely with their underlying psychological structural characteristics. This further supports the necessity of using a latent profile approach to capture individual differences in exercise intensity matching. The existence of these latent categories not only reflects the heterogeneity of exercise-related psychological characteristics within the university student population, but also suggests that, in exercise interventions and curriculum design, considering individual psychological matching may be more effective than prescribing exercise solely based on intensity levels. For example, individuals classified as LIRT, who tend to avoid high-intensity exercise and exhibit low tolerance for discomfort, traditional high-intensity training approaches may be ineffective or even counterproductive. For this group, physical education programs could begin with low-intensity and socially engaging exercise modules, such as cooperative games, recreational sports, walking-based group tasks, or rhythm-based activities (e.g., dance or group fitness), before gradually progressing toward more demanding exercise intensities. Additionally, allowing students to self-select exercise intensity or gradually increase intensity based on their comfort levels may help foster more positive affective experiences and reduce exercise-related anxiety. Particularly in higher education settings, profile-based exercise recommendations may help physical education teachers better match exercise demands with students’ psychological characteristics, thereby optimizing affective experiences and improving long-term participation and adherence.

It is worth mentioning that previous studies have identified subgroups based on affective responses or behavioral patterns, often distinguishing between more positive versus negative exercise experiences [11, 28]. To some extent, the contrast observed in the present study between profiles such as HIDT and ELAT aligns with this general distinction, as individuals with higher tolerance and preference for intensity tend to report more positive affective experiences and higher activity levels. However, the profiles identified here are not directly equivalent to those reported in prior studies. Unlike earlier work that primarily classifies individuals based on affective or behavioral outcomes, the current profiles are derived from intensity-related psychological characteristics—exercise intensity preference and tolerance—which reflect how individuals regulate and respond to exercise intensity. Therefore, the present findings extend existing research by providing a more proximal, mechanism-oriented classification that helps explain why similar exercise conditions may lead to different affective and behavioral outcomes across individuals.

This study adopted a person-centered analytical approach to conduct a latent profile analysis of university students’ exercise intensity preference and tolerance, and further examined differences across profiles in exercise affective experiences and physical activity behavior. Several notable strengths can be highlighted. First, rather than remaining at the level of traditional variable-centered linear relationship analysis, this study employed latent profile analysis to identify distinct combinations of exercise intensity preference and tolerance within the student population. This person-centered perspective better reflects how individual differences manifest in real-life exercise contexts and provides a more ecologically valid understanding of heterogeneity among students. Second, this study examined how different profiles of exercise intensity preference and tolerance differed in both exercise affective experiences and physical activity behavior. Compared with studies that focus solely on affective experiences or only on physical activity, this research refines the group characteristics related to exercise intensity preference and tolerance, thereby offering a more nuanced explanation of why students may exhibit markedly different affective responses and participation behaviors under the same exercise conditions. Third, at a theoretical level, this study provides population-based empirical support for the Dual-Mode Theory. Previous applications of the Dual-Mode Theory have largely relied on experimental designs or intensity manipulation scenarios. By identifying naturally occurring profiles in a real student population, this study indirectly demonstrates that differences in exercise affective experiences can be understood not only as situational outcomes but also as reflections of stable individual characteristics, extending the theory from situational contexts to the level of individual traits.

Despite these strengths in research design and theoretical integration, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study employed a cross-sectional design. Although associations among variables were identified, causal pathways cannot be firmly established. For instance, it remains unclear whether positive affective experiences are the result of better matching with higher exercise intensity, or whether they function as antecedents that shape such matching. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to clarify these causal relationships. Second, all variables were measured using self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to common method bias and subjective reporting errors. In particular, measures of physical activity and affective experiences could be enhanced in future research by incorporating objective activity monitoring and real-time affective assessment to improve measurement accuracy. Third, the sample was drawn from a single university, resulting in a relatively homogeneous sample structure that may limit the generalizability of the findings. Individuals of different age groups or with different exercise backgrounds may exhibit distinct combinations of exercise intensity preference and tolerance, as well as different patterns of affective experiences and exercise behavior. Future research should therefore extend this work to more diverse populations to further validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

This study focused on university students’ exercise intensity preference and tolerance, and examined their association patterns with physical activity behavior and exercise affective experiences. The findings revealed five distinct latent profiles within the student population based on combinations of exercise intensity preference and tolerance. These profiles showed significant differences in physical activity levels as well as in multiple dimensions of exercise affective experiences, including pleasure, energy, competence, relaxation, and negative affect. More specifically, individuals characterized by high exercise intensity preference and high exercise intensity tolerance demonstrated higher levels of physical activity and more positive exercise affective experiences. In contrast, those with low exercise intensity preference and low exercise intensity tolerance were more likely to experience discomfort during exercise and exhibit avoidance tendencies. These results provide a new explanatory perspective for understanding insufficient physical activity among university students and further confirm the important role of exercise affective experiences in the process of exercise participation and maintenance. Overall, the findings offer empirical support for developing more targeted exercise interventions, differentiated instruction in physical education, and personalized exercise guidance in future practice.
