ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexual desire is important for sustaining relationships during periods of vulnerability such as when undergoing medically assisted reproduction (MAR). Desire problems are the most common sexual issue experienced by couples during MAR and while there is evidence of variability, distinct classes and predictors of dyadic trajectories have not been established.AimsTo establish distinct classes of sexual desire trajectories among couples undergoing MAR over 2-years and to examine biopsychosocial predictors of classes.MethodsCouples (N = 217) seeking MAR completed an online survey assessing their demographics (age, education), biological (eg, infertility duration, type of MAR treatments), and psychosocial factors (partner support, relationship satisfaction, and perceived financial burden) at baseline. Hypotheses and analyses were pre-registered. Data were analyzed with dyadic latent class growth analysis and with multinomial logistic regression to examine predictors.OutcomesSexual desire was assessed at baseline, 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-months with two items from the Female Sexual Function Index and two nearly identical items from the International Index of Erectile Function, for female and male participants, respectively.ResultsWe identified four distinct classes of dyadic trajectories of (mostly declining) sexual desire: Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire (10%), Moderate Sexual Desire (27%), Discrepant Sexual Desire (37%), and High Sexual Desire (26%). In univariable models, six predictors were significantly associated with class membership: male-factor infertility, relationship satisfaction (both partners), MAR receiver’s perception of partner support, MAR receiver’s age and education. In the multivariable model, education and relationship satisfaction remained significant predictors.Clinical ImplicationsTrajectories of sexual desire during MAR are not uniform; clinical practice should take this heterogeneity into account in psychoeducation, assessment, and intervention. By emphasizing the potential protective nature of relationship satisfaction, findings may inform couple-based interventions to promote sexual desire during MAR.Strengths and LimitationsThis study is a large dyadic, longitudinal investigation of couples’ patterns of change in sexual desire during MAR. The sample excluded those with social infertility, was characterized by high education and employment and was predominantly White and heterosexual, limiting generalizability. Absence of a control group not undergoing MAR limits our ability to determine whether patterns are unique to MAR.ConclusionWe identified four distinct dyadic trajectories of sexual desire across 2 years of MAR; membership in some classes were predicted by education and relationship satisfaction. Findings provide more nuanced information about couples’ sexual desire during this vulnerable period, which may facilitate early assessment and intervention.

BODY
Sexual desire, or interest in sexual activity,1 is a critical feature of romantic relationships as it is often what initially draws partners to each other, and it continues to influence their relationship quality and function once in an established partnership. Higher sexual desire in relationships tends to be associated with greater overall relationship satisfaction and predicts more relationship maintaining behaviors such as expressions of affection and appreciation.2,3 According to the relationship development model of sexual desire,4 sexual desire is particularly important for sustaining relationships during periods of vulnerability (ie, when facing a perceived threat to the relationship) such as infertility, because it can cultivate intimacy and buffer again attachment insecurities.5,6 However, sexual desire is also known to be highly sensitive to contextual factors such as chronic stress or hormonal changes, and to the relational environment such as conflict and poor communication on the one hand and partner responsiveness on the other hand see4,7 for reviews. Such contextual factors may be even more salient during a major life event such as undergoing medically assisted reproduction (MAR) for medical infertility, contributing to desire difficulties and heterogeneity in experiences of sexual desire over time.

Indeed, 42% of women and 28% of men report that infertility negatively affects their sexual desire.8 Moreover, sexual desire problems are the most common sexual issue experienced by both women (58%) and men (29%) seeking MAR9,10 and individuals with medical infertility report lower sexual desire compared to controls.11 Couples seeking MAR for medical infertility report that their sexual experiences become mechanized, scheduled around ovulation cycles, and lack spontaneity and pleasure,12,13 all of which interfere with sexual desire and could compound over time for some couples. There is likely to be variability in experiences of sexual desire during MAR over time, as has been seen during other major reproductive events such as the transition to parenthood,14 but such trajectories have not yet been established. In a rare longitudinal study, Lo, Ching-Yin Wong et al.15 found a significant improvement in orgasm (but not in other domains of sexual function) in women across a cycle of intrauterine insemination (IUI) and observed no change in men’s erectile function throughout treatment. This study was limited by a small sample size (N = 66 couples) that followed participants through only one IUI cycle and the authors did not take a dyadic approach to the analyses. Given the inherently interpersonal nature of sexual desire and the shared experience of MAR, a dyadic approach is critical to obtaining an accurate and functionally relevant picture of changes in sexual desire for both partners over a longer period of MAR.

Prior studies examining predictors of sexual function during MAR have primarily been retrospective and cross-sectional, limited by small sample sizes that focus primarily on women rather than couples, and have lacked long-term follow-up,16 for review. Moreover, most focused on overall sexual function and rarely examined predictors of sexual desire specifically, despite theoretical and empirical evidence of its unique contributions to health, well-being, and relationships.4,7 Nonetheless, biopsychosocial factors appear to play a crucial role in predicting overall sexual function in the context of MAR, thus providing guidance into potentially relevant predictors of classes of desire.10,16 In terms of biological or infertility-related factors, results are often mixed, with some studies reporting associations with sexual function and other not. Some studies have found that a higher number of treatment cycles and more invasive treatments, such as in-vitro fertilization (IVF) or IUI (versus hormonal treatments only), are linked with lower sexual function and satisfaction.15,17 Both longer infertility duration and being the partner with medical infertility have also been linked with lower sexual function for women and men,18,19 though some studies have not found these correlations,16 for review. In terms of psychological and social factors, a greater perceived financial burden of MAR has been linked to lower sexual desire in women and men.20 Lower relationship satisfaction is linked to poorer sexual function during MAR,21,22 whereas feeling more supported and coping together with a partner was linked to higher sexual satisfaction and lower sexual distress.23 Demographics such as lower education and older age are also linked to poorer overall sexual function in both women and men during MAR,21,24 although again some studies did not find these associations,16 for review.

Taken together, problems with sexual desire are common during MAR, though this may not be true of everyone and changes in desire may differ between partners and over time; such heterogeneity has not been captured before in a dyadic, longitudinal study. Past research has also emphasized biomedical and infertility-specific or demographic predictors of sexual function and neglected psychosocial factors, the latter of which are known to be more consequential for predicting sexual desire in non-MAR contexts25,26 and are more amenable to change. Enhanced knowledge of the trajectories and predictors of classes of sexual desire may assist clinicians in identifying who is most at risk for experiencing desire challenges. Addressing these gaps is crucial for informing interventions aimed at enhancing the sexual health of couples navigating MAR.

The aims of the current study were to establish distinct classes of trajectories of sexual desire among couples seeking MAR for medical infertility over a 2-year period (five assessments) and to examine biopsychosocial predictors of the classes. We did not have a hypothesis regarding the specific number of distinct classes of couples’ desire trajectories; however, we expected more than one class given the many contextual factors (eg, stress, different treatments) at play during MAR, contributing to variability in sexual desire, as well as the different prevalence rates for sexual desire difficulties reported in the literature during MAR. We hypothesized that the following biopsychosocial factors would predict classes of sexual desire trajectories that reflect low or declining sexual desire over time: longer duration of infertility, a higher number of treatment cycles, more invasive MAR treatments (ie, IVF, relative to IUI, or other treatments), no pregnancy or pregnancy loss, discontinuation of treatment, a higher perceived financial burden, lower educational level, higher age, lower relationship satisfaction, and lower partner support. We also explored infertility diagnosis (ie, male-factor, female-factor, mixed, and unexplained) as a predictor.

Method

Our predictions and analysis plan were preregistered prior to analyzing (but after collecting) the data. This information is available on the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/rv85y/overview?view_only=ec77afe1cb5b419898e443ea2e34b586, along with the de-identified data and syntax.

Participants

Couples who recently initiated MAR treatment were recruited as part of a longitudinal study examining treatment burden and well-being. To ensure that couples were recruited at the beginning of their current MAR process, eligibility was restricted to those meeting one of the following criteria: (1) their first MAR clinic visit occurred within the past 6 months; (2) their first clinic visit occurred within the past 12 months, but they postponed further steps and only began actively pursuing MAR within the past 6 months; or (3) they had previously engaged in MAR, but had not attended a clinic for at least one year prior to initiating a new MAR process within the past 6 months. Both members of the couple had to agree to participate. Participants also had to have internet access, be proficient in French or English, and be 18 years or older. Participants with self-reported unmanaged major psychiatric disorders were excluded due to the potential exacerbation of their symptoms while undergoing MAR. Lastly, if a couple ended their relationship during the study, they were no longer eligible for future follow-ups; their data up until this point was retained and estimated using Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML). In addition to the criteria for the larger study, to be included in the current study, participants had to have initiated at least one MAR treatment and one partner needed to have medical infertility. A total of 355 couples were enrolled in the larger study and 138 were excluded for the following reasons: one or both partners did not complete the baseline survey (48 couples), failed two out of three attention checks embedded in the questionnaires (3 couples), reported no medical infertility (38 couples), and had not received at least one MAR treatment 6 months before baseline or over the course of the study (49 couples). A final sample of 217 couples were included in the current analyses (see Table 1 for demographics). Figure S1 shows the flow of participant recruitment and attrition at various time points.

Measures

Sexual desire

Sexual desire was assessed using two items from the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI; eg, “Over the past 4 weeks, how often did you feel sexual desire or interest?”) and two nearly identical items from the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF), for female and male participants, respectively.27,28 The two items were summed to give a score ranging from 2 to 10, where higher scores reflect greater desire; interpretation of the scores as low, moderate or high desire was relative, with a score around the mid-point considered to be moderate. Scoring for the desire subscale followed the IIEF (ie, summing) and not the FSFI, which uses a weighting approach relative to other facets of sexual function.

Demographic and MAR-related factors

Demographic and MAR-related factors were assessed with single items including age, education level, duration of infertility (months), infertility diagnosis (male-factor, female-factor, mixed, unexplained), type of treatment (IVF, IUI, both or other treatment), number of MAR treatment cycles, MAR outcome during the study period (pregnancy vs no pregnancy), and treatment discontinuation (yes/no).

Psychological and relationship factors

The financial burden of MAR was assessed using a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “not at all difficult,” and 7 represents “extremely difficult”.20 Participants could also select “not applicable” if they did not experience financial burden. Relationship satisfaction was assessed via the brief 4-item version of the Dyadic Adjustment Scale DAS-4; eg, “How often do you discuss or have you considered divorce, separation, or termination of your relationship?”.29 The four items are summed to give a score (0-21), where higher scores reflect higher satisfaction. Partner support was assessed via the Revised Support in Intimate Relationships Rating Scale R-SSIRS; eg, “My partner helped me to think about a situation in a new way”.30 The R-SSIRS has 25 items that are summed to give a score (5–100), where higher scores reflect higher partner support. For the R-SSIRS questionnaire, participants answered for “actual” and “desired” behaviours in their partner, but for the purpose of this study we used the “actual” scores only.

Procedures

Patient partners were engaged in the development and implementation of the study by providing feedback on all study procedures and materials. The current study recruited participants across two sites (masked for review). Recruitment followed two approaches. First, couples were recruited via online advertisements and social media. Second, couples were recruited in-person at a fertility clinic in (masked site). Patients were pre-screened for eligibility using patient charts and then were invited to meet with a research assistant after their appointment, if interested in learning more about the study. One member of a couple completed a brief eligibility screener, either by telephone or in-person. If eligible, participants completed informed consent at the start of the baseline survey. Surveys were completed online via Qualtrics Research Suite at five timepoints: baseline, 6-months, 12-months, 18-months, and 24-months. Compensation was provided in gift cards of increasing value for each subsequent survey to a maximum of $108 (CAD) per participant. All study procedures were approved by each university’s Research Ethics Boards.

Data analyses

The analyses were conducted with Mplus version 8.10 31 using the maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) estimator. The trajectories of the couples were assessed with Dyadic Latent Class Growth Analysis (DLCGA), a combination of the principles of Latent Class Growth Analysis32 and Dyadic Growth Mixture Modeling.33 DLCGA accounts for heterogeneity in longitudinal patterns of sexual desire because the latent classes represent unique trajectories34,35 with the couple as the unit of analysis rather than the individual. We constrained to zero the variance of the intercepts, linear slopes, and quadratic terms (when applicable) within a class, but allowed them to vary across classes.36,37 Individuals were distinguished based on the participant who received the MAR treatment and their partner. The distinguishing variable overlapped with sex such that everyone who received MAR treatment was female and partners were all male; there were two couples in which both partners received treatment. Both partners’ trajectories were assessed simultaneously across five time points assessed in months, where the initial baseline level was the intercept (0, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months). Missing data for sexual desire in the DLCGA were handled the (FIML) function.

The results from the class solutions were inspected until there were three class solutions in a row with poorer model fit.38 The fit indices were compared across models; lower Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion, and sample-size-adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion indicated better fitting models. Entropy values closer to one indicated a better fit.39,40 Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test and bootstrap likelihood ratio test, compared n vs. n - 1 classes to estimate comparable model fit.41 Finally, the size of classes (>5% sample per class), parsimony, and interpretability of classes were considered in the selection of the final solution.40

Once a final class solution was selected, the R3STEP method implemented in Mplus42 was used to predict latent class membership from the biopsychosocial variables. This approach accounts for classification uncertainty. First, multinomial regression analyses were performed with the predictors individually. Differences between classes were assessed by examining odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. Then, the significant predictors were entered into a multivariable multinomial logistic regression. Given the novelty of this work, aspects of which were exploratory, and to have a better understanding of the differences between the classes, all possible comparisons between classes were examined and interpreted. Because the R3STEP method in Mplus does not currently handle FIML for the covariates, missing data on the predictors were handled using multiple imputation (using 10 datasets) to avoid listwise deletion and increase power. Of all the predictors, only relationship satisfaction of the partners (1.38%) and relationship support of the MAR receivers (4.60%) and partners (5.53%) had missing data; these data were missing completely at random (Little's MCAR: x2(8) = 7.49, P = .485).

Results

Dyadic trajectories

The one-class linear solution suggested that initial values for sexual desire were moderate for the person who received MAR treatment (intercept = 5.896, SE = 0.120, P < .001) and for their partners (intercept = 7.776, SE = 0.128, P < .001) at baseline and that sexual desire significantly declined over time for both members (people who received MAR mean slope = -0.066, SE = 0.007, P < .001; partners’ mean slope = -0.060, SE = 0.009, P < .001). The DLCGA uncovered significant heterogeneity; we retained the four-class solution for the linear models because it best fit our pre-determined criteria (ie, from the preregistration and as described above in data analyses). Class 1 (Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire Couples) comprised 10% of couples (n = 22). Both partners started with moderate sexual desire but reported a steep decline over time and very low sexual desire at the end of the study. The intercept for sexual desire for people who received MAR treatment was 5.441 (SE = 0.495, P < .001) and had a significant steep decline over time (ESTS = -0.223, SE = 0.037, P < .001). Their partners’ intercept was 5.708 (SE = 0.440, P < .001) and also had a significant steep decline over time (ESTS = -0.292, SE = 0.025, P < .001).

Class 2 (Moderate Sexual Desire Couples) included 27% of couples (n = 59). Both partners started with moderate sexual desire and reported a significant decline over time, though they still reported moderate-low levels of desire at the end of the study. In this class, the intercept for sexual desire of individuals who received MAR treatment was 5.749 (SE = 0.210, P < .001) and their trajectory significantly declined over time (ESTS = -0.054, SE = 0.011, P < .001). Partners’ intercept was 6.215 (SE = 0.319, P < .001) and their slope also significantly declined over time (ESTS = -0.077, SE = 0.025, P = .002).

Class 3 (Discrepant Sexual Desire Couples) was the most prevalent group, with 37% of couples (n = 80). In this class, individuals who received MAR reported moderate levels of sexual desire that declined to moderate-low levels over time, and their partners reported a relatively high sexual desire that remained stable throughout the study period. In this class, the intercept for individuals who received MAR treatment was 4.959 (SE = 0.273, P < .001) and had a significant decrease in sexual desire over time (ESTS = -.062, SE = 0.010, P < .001). Partners’ intercept was 8.658 (SE = 0.146, P < .001) and had no significant change over time (ESTS = -0.009, SE = 0.011, P = .413).

Class 4 (High Sexual Desire Couples) comprised 26% of couples (n = 56) wherein both members of the couple reported relatively high sexual desire that declined on average throughout the study period, though it remained relatively high. In this class, the intercept for sexual desire for individuals who received MAR was 7.531 (SE = 0.301 P < .001) and declined significantly over time (ESTS = -0.026, SE = 0.011, P = .019). Partners’ intercept was 8.789 (SE = 0.158, P < .001) and also had a significant decline over time (ESTS = -0.025, SE = 0.011, P = .027).

Biopsychosocial predictors of class trajectories

Table 3 presents the associations between predictors and trajectory class. The R3STEP approach, in which each predictor was examined in a separate model, revealed six predictors that were associated with significant differences between classes: male-factor infertility, both partners’ relationship satisfaction, the MAR receivers’ perception of their partner’s support, and the MAR receivers’ age and education. In contrast, MAR-related factors including the duration of infertility, infertility diagnosis, type of MAR treatment (IVF, IUI, or both vs other treatment), number of treatment cycles, outcome of MAR treatment(s) (pregnancy vs no pregnancy), and discontinuation of treatment were not significant predictors of class membership. Likewise, partners’ age and education, perceived financial burden (by either partner) and the partner’s perception of the MAR receiver’s support were not significant predictors of class membership.

Subsequently, all significant predictors were examined together simultaneously in a multivariable model and regressed onto trajectory class membership (see Table 4). We interpreted the pairwise comparisons between all the classes using three reference groups. We started with the group that reported the lowest sexual desire and the steepest decline over time: (1) Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire versus all other classes (compares low vs moderate to high initial sexual desire trajectories). Then, the reference group was the second lowest sexual desire for both partners: (2) Moderate Sexual Desire versus Discrepant and High Sexual Desire trajectories (compares couples where both partners had moderate initial values to the couples with at least one partner with high initial value). Finally, a reference group with one partner (the MAR receiver) that reported moderate initial value: (3) Discrepant Sexual Desire versus High Sexual Desire (compares the two groups with the highest sexual desire). Because all pairwise comparisons were interpreted, the reference groups selected did not affect the results presented. Specifically, choice of the reference group only affects the odds ratio interpretation: an odds ratio greater than 1 was interpreted as following: as the predictor value increases, there was an increased odds of being in the non-reference group (vs the reference group); and an odds ratio less than 1 reflected that, as the predictor increased, there was a decreased odds of being in the non-reference group.

Multivariable logistic regression with significant biopsychosocial predictors of sexual desire trajectories during MAR treatment (N = 217 couples).

Abbreviations: MAR = medically assisted reproduction; OR = Odds ratio; CI = confidence interval; significant ORs are displayed in bolded.

In the multivariable model, male-factor infertility, the age of the person who received MAR treatment, and the perception of partner support reported by the MAR-receiver were no longer significant predictors of class membership. More educated individuals receiving MAR had increased odds of being in the Moderate Sexual Desire or the Discrepant Sexual Desire class than the Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire class. More educated individuals receiving MAR also had decreased odds of being in the High Sexual Desire class compared to the Moderate Sexual Desire and the Discrepant Sexual Desire classes. Among individuals who received MAR treatment, reporting higher relationship satisfaction increased the odds of being in the High Sexual Desire class than the Moderate Sexual Desire and the Discrepant Sexual Desire class. For partners of those who received MAR treatment, reporting higher relationship satisfaction was associated with increased odds of membership in the High Sexual Desire class than the Moderate Sexual Desire class.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of couples undergoing MAR, we identified four distinct trajectories of dyadic sexual desire across 24 months: 10% of couples were in the Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire class, 27% in the Moderate Sexual Desire class, 37% in the Discrepant Sexual Desire class, and 26% in the High Sexual Desire class. In univariable models, six predictors were significantly associated with class membership: male-factor infertility, relationship satisfaction (for both partners), the MAR receiver’s perception of partner support, and the MAR receiver’s age and education. However, in the multivariable model, only education and relationship satisfaction of both partners remained significant predictors of trajectory class. Our results uncovered significant heterogeneity in couples’ sexual desire over 2 years. Across classes, most participants—including all those who were the recipient of MAR treatment—reported a decline in sexual desire over time, which is consistent with prior cross-sectional studies and reflects the adverse sexual impacts resulting from the physical and emotional burden of MAR.11 Yet, within the dyad-level classes, change was not always consistent within the couple (eg, MAR receiver declined, partner remained stable), underscoring the importance of a dyadic approach. This heterogeneity is important for conceptualizing changes to sexual function among couples undergoing MAR and identifying subgroups of couples including those who are more resilient and those who might benefit most from early assessment and intervention.

The smallest class of couples (10%)—Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire—started with moderate levels of desire but both partners demonstrated steep reductions in desire, ending with very low desire 2 years later. This class appears to reflect a high-risk group that may benefit from early assessment and treatment to support maintaining sexual interest and the potential benefits that it can bring for mental and physical health, couple intimacy, and coping.4,43 Beyond the role of education (discussed further below), future research might examine additional risk factors such as mental health (eg, depression, anxiety), emotion regulation, and dyadic coping that could predispose this class,16 for review. In contrast, just over a quarter of couples fell into the Moderate Sexual Desire class in which both partners showed parallel declines starting from moderate levels, suggesting couples may have experienced similar treatment-related strain and adaptation.

The most common class, Discrepant Sexual Desire (37%), was marked by stable, high partner desire alongside declining desire in MAR receivers. This pattern may reflect asymmetrical treatment burden and is consistent with evidence that those undergoing MAR procedures experience more desire difficulties,10,12 potentially due to treatment side effects. Moreover, in the current study, all MAR receivers were women, whose sexual desire can be more susceptible to contextual factors eg, fatigue.44 The discrepancy in sexual desire was evident in this class at baseline, which is a common pattern for many couples,45 but the difference became more pronounced across the 2 years. Sexual desire discrepancies can be distressing46 and have been linked to lower sexual and relationship well-being.45 Future research should examine potential consequences, if any, of desire discrepancies during MAR.

The High Sexual Desire class, which represented about one quarter of all couples, maintained relatively elevated desire in both partners, suggesting a resilient subset. Potential declines in sexual desire may have been buffered by higher relationship satisfaction in this group. Indeed, for both individuals receiving MAR and their partners, reporting higher relationship satisfaction at baseline increased the odds of being in this class relative to the Moderate Sexual Desire class and the Discrepant Sexual Desire class (for MAR receivers only). Such findings echo prior research that highlights the role of relationship quality in sustaining sexual desire,7 and likewise that higher relationship satisfaction is linked to higher sexual function in women with infertility or undergoing MAR.21,22 Given that many couples report MAR to be highly stressful for their relationship,47,48 higher relationship satisfaction at treatment onset may buffer against disruptions to sexual intimacy, foster stronger emotional closeness, and support adaptive dyadic coping. Indeed, prior work in MAR found that couples who manage treatment-related stress collaboratively reported more satisfying sexual relationships and lower sexual distress and infertility-related sexual concerns.23 These findings highlight the importance of early screening for relationship quality and integrating couple-based relational support into MAR.

Education remained as a significant predictor in the multivariable model, showcasing a complex pattern. For individuals receiving MAR, higher education was associated with increased odds of being in the Moderate Sexual Desire or the Discrepant Sexual Desire class than the Steep Decline with Low Sexual Desire class. In this case, education may serve as a protective factor against the most severe mutual declines in sexual desire, possibly by facilitating greater health literacy, access to resources, or adaptive coping strategies.49 However, more educated individuals receiving MAR also had decreased odds of being in the High Sexual Desire class compared to the Moderate Sexual Desire and the Discrepant Sexual Desire classes. These findings are inconsistent with prior literature linking higher education to better sexual function during MAR21,50; it is possible that higher education corresponds to more work-related stress, delayed childbearing (hence lower MAR treatment success), or heightened awareness and reporting of relationship or sexual difficulties,51,52 thus making it less likely to be in the highest desire class.

While previous studies have reported mixed associations between sexual function and infertility and MAR-related variables such as duration of infertility, infertility diagnosis, type of treatment, number of treatment cycles, outcome of the treatment(s), and discontinuation of treatment,16 for review, none of these characteristics were significant predictors of sexual desire trajectory classes in our sample. Our results provide novel evidence that such factors do not predict dyadic classes of trajectories of sexual desire. It may be reassuring to couples undergoing MAR that these largely non-modifiable or less modifiable factors do not appear to be consequential for their dyadic patterns of desire over time.

Strengths and limitations

The large sample size, longitudinal design, and use of a validated measure of sexual desire are significant strengths of this study as they enabled us to conduct rigorous analyses that accounted for more variability in both partners’ sexual desire across 2 years of MAR (eg, multiple treatment cycles, varied treatment outcomes). In addition, using DLCGA revealed meaningful dyadic subgroups that would have been obscured in variable-centered approaches. Finally, the inclusion of biopsychosocial predictors of sexual desire trajectory classes supports a multidimensional conceptualization of sexual desire, endorsed by most experts and consistent with theoretical models of sexual function and MAR.10,16

Our limitations include the potential for recall and social desirability bias, given that our findings rely on retrospective self-reports of sexual desire over the past four weeks. The sample excluded individuals with social infertility (eg, same-sex couples, single parents by choice), limiting generalizability beyond couples with medical infertility. Despite broad recruitment efforts, the sample demographics may limit the generalizability of findings. Specifically, our sample was characterized by relatively high education and employment and was predominantly White and heterosexual. Most of our sample had IVF treatment, which may further constrain representativeness. A more diverse sample with respect to race, sexual orientation, and infertility characteristics may yield different trajectories. The absence of a control group of couples not undergoing MAR limits the ability to determine whether observed patterns are unique to MAR or reflect broader relationship trends. Future research should aim for greater demographic diversity and include couples with social infertility, particularly given our lack of findings related to infertility characteristics.

Implications and conclusions

Our findings suggest that trajectories of sexual desire during MAR are not uniform; clinical practice should take this heterogeneity into account in psychoeducation, assessment and intervention.

Health care professionals can share information about the multiple trajectories of sexual desire and normalize variability, which may allow couples to adjust their expectations and alleviate feelings of inadequacy or distress about the sexual relationship. Given that most participants reported declines in sexual desire over time, routine follow-up should include screening for changes in sexual desire and opportunities to discuss whether such changes are causing distress or conflict for the couple. Those reporting distressing consequences might benefit from referral to psychological interventions to enhance sexual function, including sexual desire, in the context of MAR such as psychoeducational, mindfulness-based, and cognitive-behavioral therapies.53–56 Moreover, health care professionals can emphasize the potential protective nature of relationship satisfaction. Promoting relationship quality through couple-based interventions, such as encouraging couple time that is not focused on infertility and the MAR process and the pursuit of other, non-parenthood related goals, may help couples preserve intimacy and engage in more adaptive dyadic coping with MAR stressors,57 with benefits to their sexual desire. Recognizing sexual desire during MAR as a dynamic and dyadic experience that does not change in the same way for everyone, can guide tailored, couple-based interventions to improve and/or preserve sexual intimacy throughout treatment.
