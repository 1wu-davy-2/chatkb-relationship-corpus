TITLE: Neural responses to conflicting self- and partner-directed feedback correlate with trait emotion regulation and daily support.

ABSTRACT
Empathy lies at the core of social interactions, particularly in intimate relationships, yet few laboratory tasks are designed to capture empathy in interpersonal contexts—especially those requiring individuals to simultaneously process social information directed toward both the self and a romantic partner. In this exploratory study, 131 adults (from 71 romantic couples) completed a neuroimaging paradigm that manipulated whether positive or negative partner-directed feedback was presented alone or alongside self-directed feedback. The partner-alone feedback contrasts (partner-directed feedback alone versus a neutral control condition) robustly engaged brain regions associated with empathic responding, including the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, anterior insula, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction. Notably, during the incongruent feedback contrasts—simultaneous conflicting self- and partner-directed feedback versus self-directed feedback alone—participants exhibited diminished affective and brain responses but evidenced unique brain activity in frontoparietal areas frequently associated with cognitive control (e.g., dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and supramarginal gyrus). Furthermore, when responding to positive partner-directed feedback in the presence of negative self-directed feedback, enhanced activity in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex was associated with lower self-reported difficulties in emotion regulation and more partner-rated support provided in everyday life. Taken together, these findings suggest that salient self-focused information may dampen individuals’ emotional and neural responses to their partners’ uplifting and critical feedback, that emotion regulation tendencies may be an important between-subjects moderator of this effect, and that individual differences in spontaneous neural responses during this task were associated with meaningful real-world outcomes, although further validation and replication are warranted.

BODY
Introduction

Empathy exerts a profound influence on prosocial behavior1–3, social connectedness4, and mental health5, particularly in the context of close relationships6,7. While definitions of empathy vary in the literature, recent interdisciplinary efforts describe empathy as the ability to experience others’ affective states while maintaining a distinction from one’s own emotional experiences8. Despite differences in terminology, there is broad agreement that empathy implies three processes: experience sharing, mentalizing, and prosocial concern9,10. Although many laboratory-based tasks succeed at eliciting empathy in controlled experimental settings2,11–13, capturing the spontaneous empathic responses that occur in everyday life has proven more challenging14,15. In daily life, acute stress and salient emotional states can make spontaneous empathy toward others difficult, if not impossible, in some circumstances16–18. In these situations, regulating self-focused thoughts and feelings to understand and share the experience of another may require notable cognitive effort. One understudied context in which these kinds of challenging situations often occur is in the context of close relationships. For example, imagine you receive negative feedback from your supervisor, but your partner tells you they received a wonderful promotion at work. How do you respond? In this manuscript, we use functional MRI to examine how participants in romantic relationships respond to their partners’ experiences in the presence of incongruent self-related social feedback (e.g., when they are rejected but their partner is liked). We hope to bridge the gap between controlled laboratory settings and the complexities of everyday social interactions that can interfere with empathic behavior.

A key starting point for our exploration is the challenge associated with managing self-relevant information and, at times, regulating self-relevant thoughts and feelings. Prioritizing self-relevant information is believed to be evolutionarily advantageous, and behavioral studies demonstrate that self-referential stimuli produce a stronger interference on the processing of other-related stimuli than the reverse situation19,20. Failing to regulate one’s emotions or inhibit self-relevant responses can lead to egocentricity bias, defined as the interference caused when one’s own thoughts or emotions influence judgments about another person, which may ultimately reduce empathic responses toward others21,22. For example, acute stress and negative mood can have detrimental consequences on one’s affective sharing and mentalizing16,18,19. Despite the central role of self- and emotion-regulatory processes in many models of empathy23–26, relatively little is known about how the brain processes other-relevant information in the presence of simultaneous self-relevant information, how these mechanisms vary across individuals in emotion regulation tendencies, and whether they are relevant for explaining real-world prosocial behavior.

Although a large body of research has established the role of several interacting brain regions in supporting the capacity to share others’ emotions and understand others’ mental states–including the anterior insula (AI), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DMPFC), temporoparietal junction (TPJ), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and ventromedial prefrontal cortex10–12—relatively few studies have directly manipulated emotion- and self-regulatory demands during empathic processing. Many laboratory paradigms involve simply observing or processing stimuli being encountered by others1,2,12, which cannot fully capture the complexity of empathy in the real world where multiple sources of emotionally salient, self- and other-relevant information must be processed simultaneously. Silani et al. investigated the neural underpinnings of overcoming the egocentricity bias in the emotional domain, primarily under highly controlled and instructed judgment conditions21. The authors introduced a visuo-tactile paradigm, where participants were explicitly instructed to make judgments about either their own or a stranger’s affective state upon presumably interacting with the tactile stimuli, while simultaneously viewing self- and other-related stimuli that could be affectively congruent or incongruent. Their findings highlighted the role of the right supramarginal gyrus (SMG) in overcoming the emotional egocentricity bias21. Despite the contribution of this work, there remains a need for paradigms that capture spontaneous empathic responses, without explicitly instructing participants to empathize, especially in challenging interpersonal situations.

Empathy is often elicited in the context of close (especially romantic) relationships15 and is considered vital to relationship quality and mental well-being6,7,27. However, most of the existing social neuroscientific research focuses on understanding and empathizing with strangers1–3,11, which may diverge to some degree from the neural mechanisms in the context of relationships28,29. Although some studies have examined empathy within romantic relationships, they primarily focus on empathy for pain30,31, which overlaps with but may be different from empathic responses evoked by social negative states (e.g., anger or distress)32. Studying empathy in social contexts can therefore provide additional insights into how empathic responses unfold in everyday interpersonal situations. Furthermore, empathy for positive emotions, which also plays a functional role in prosocial behavior, social closeness, and well-being33,34, has received limited attention. Addressing these gaps will enhance our understanding of empathy within close relationships in everyday life.

To better approximate empathic contexts in everyday life and to investigate individuals’ spontaneous responses to their romantic partners’ social experiences during challenging interpersonal situations, the current study employed a dyadic functional magnetic resonance imaging paradigm—the Connected Lives: Overcoming the Self through Empathy (CLOSE) task35. The CLOSE task is adapted from social feedback paradigms, where participants receive positive, neutral, or negative feedback about their desirability or likability36,37, and empathy paradigms which involve observing stimuli being encountered by others2,12. The task displays a photograph of a “rater” along with the rater’s presumed likability ratings of the participant in the scanner (“YOU”) and/or the participant’s partner (“PNR”), who the participant believes will also view the same stimuli in another room, based on a cover story to facilitate spontaneous empathy. The rater’s likeability ratings vary in valence, consisting of positive (POS, indicated by “YES”), negative (NEG, indicated by “NO”), and absent (no ratings, indicated by “XX”) ratings (Fig. 1A). To create a more naturalistic context capturing spontaneous empathic responses while minimizing demand characteristics inherent to many existing paradigms, the task avoids explicit instructions to focus on oneself or to empathize with one’s partner. Rather, participants’ affective responses were assessed following half of the trials in each condition. Although the CLOSE task yields several task conditions for analysis in other ongoing work, we isolated brain and behavioral contrasts linked to low versus high self-regulatory demands. Specifically, to measure spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback under low demand for regulating responses to self-directed feedback, we compared partner-only conditions—where the self (S) received no feedback and the partner (P) received positive or negative feedback (SXXPPOS or SXXPNEG)—to a neutral control condition matched on visual properties but lacking self-directed or partner-directed feedback (SXXPXX) (see Methods for cover story behind feedback-absent conditions). These partner-alone feedback contrasts (SXXPPOS-SXXPXX and SXXPNEG-SXXPXX) are similar to conventional empathy contrasts used in previous research. Additionally, to evaluate spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback under higher demand for regulating responses to self-directed feedback, we contrasted simultaneous incongruent conditions—where opposing positive and negative feedback was directed toward the self and partner (SPOSPNEG or SNEGPPOS)—against self-only conditions involving positive or negative self-directed feedback (SPOSPXX or SNEGPXX). We refer to these contrasts (SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX and SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX) as incongruent feedback contrasts in our study.

In this report, we first examined whether the partner-alone feedback contrasts (SXXPPOS-SXXPXX and SXXPNEG-SXXPXX) elicited behavioral (self-reported affect) and BOLD activity consistent with prior empathy research. We hypothesized that we would observe activity in regions previously observed in empathy studies, such as the AI, dACC, DMPFC, TPJ, PCC, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex10–12. We then expanded our analysis to the incongruent feedback contrasts (SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX and SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX) to investigate participants’ spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback in the presence of incongruent self-directed feedback. Since the task places high demands on regulating responses to self-directed feedback, we hypothesized that participants would also recruit regions associated with emotion regulation, such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)38,39, and regions previously linked to overcoming the emotional egocentricity bias (such as the right SMG)21, beyond regions associated with empathy. Of particular importance, we aimed to test the hypothesis that between-person variability in BOLD activity during the incongruent feedback contrasts would be related to individual differences in emotion regulation tendencies and trait empathy, as well as with empathic behavior in everyday life, which we assessed via support provided to one’s partner using a week-long daily diary, completed by the partner to boost the validity of the findings. Our work was not preregistered, and we thus consider it exploratory and discovery-oriented despite our a priori hypotheses.

Methods

Participants

Seventy-three couples (N = 146 participants) in monogamous romantic relationship were recruited from the Tucson community using flyers and advertisements on social media. Couples were required to have been together for at least 18 months and to have lived together for at least 6 months. This study is part of a larger ongoing study involving the collection of neural, physiological, and behavioral data among romantic couples. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Arizona and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Fifteen participants were excluded from the subsequent analysis due to either the absence of task or structural neuroimaging scans (n = 10), or compromised neuroimaging data resulting from severe coverage loss or asynchrony between the experimental procedure and brain scanning (n = 5). Thus, the final neuroimaging sample contained 131 subjects from 71 couples. Among these participants, 76 individuals were married. The mean age of the final sample was 31.8 years (SD = 8.4, range = 19.1–56.1), the mean relationship duration was 8.3 years (SD = 6.3, range = 1.7–30.0), and the mean cohabiting duration was 6.5 years (SD = 6.2, range = 0.5–29.5). Detailed demographic characteristics are displayed in Supplementary Table 1.

Experimental procedure

The experimental procedure included two laboratory sessions, a daily diary completed by participants’ partners each night between the two sessions, and a 6-month follow-up survey (not included in the current study). Participants were compensated $15 for completing the online interview, $35 for Session 1, $40 for completing the experience sampling between sessions, $70 for Session 2, and $40 for completing the 6-month follow-up survey, for a total of $200 per person ($400 per couple).

In Session 1, participants completed demographic questionnaires, three social interaction tasks not analyzed in this manuscript, and a face rating task designed to build a cover story to increase believability of the social feedback ratings in the CLOSE task. In the face rating task, participants rated the likability of 30 photos thought to be other participants who completed Session 1 (these photos differed from those shown in Session 2). Participants were told: “We have a separate ongoing study in which hundreds of participants have already had their pictures taken. The selection of people that you just rated were drawn from this other study”. Participants pressed the right arrow if the person appeared likable to them (displaying the word “YES” on the screen) and the left arrow if the person did not appear likable (displaying the word “NO”). If participants did not press an arrow key within 2 s, the text “XX” was displayed and the task advanced to the next trial. Additionally, an experimenter also took the participant’s own photo in Session 1 to convince them that their photos would be distributed to other participants to rate their likability.

In Session 2, both partners completed MRI scanning, including a resting state scan, a structural T1 scan, the CLOSE task, and a field map. While one participant was being scanned, their partner completed a battery of questionnaires in a different room assessing several constructs (see the measurement list available on https://osf.io/63vnc/files/c4t7r). These measurements included trait empathy (e.g., the Empathy Assessment Index and the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI)), trait emotion regulation (e.g., the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS-18)), relationship quality (e.g., the Revised Dyadic Adjustment Scale and the Couples Satisfaction Index), psychological well-being and symptoms of mental health disorders (e.g., the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System—Depression and Anxiety, and the UCLA Loneliness Scale), and trauma exposure (e.g., the Adverse Childhood Experiences scale). While several of these metrics will be the focus of future analyses, the IRI and DERS-18 were selected for the purposes of current study as commonly used metrics of trait empathy and emotion regulation tendencies (see below).

The CLOSE task—a social feedback task–was used to assess spontaneous empathy as demands to regulate the self-focused thoughts and feelings vary across conditions. Participants were informed that the task involves viewing likability ratings made by other participants in the study about themselves and their partner. Critically, participants were told that “You and your partner will each be viewing feedback from the same participants during your scans”, setting up a context to facilitate a sense of their partner’s presence and promote spontaneous empathy. Prior studies using the CLOSE paradigm on different subsamples have provided initial support for its validity, but they did not examine neural responses under incongruent feedback conditions. Specifically, previous work has demonstrated behavioral associations between relational interdependence and the subjective valuation of partner-related feedback35, as well as task-related engagement of self- and other-mentalizing neural signatures during feedback processing compared to a no-feedback control condition40.

The CLOSE task

Experimental conditions and trial structure

In each trial of the CLOSE task, a different photograph of a “rater” was displayed for 3.9 s, along with the rater’s presumed likability ratings of the participant in the scanner (“YOU”) and the participant’s partner (“PNR”). The rater’s likeability ratings varied in valence, consisting of positive (POS, rating = “YES”), negative (NEG, rating = “NO”), and absent (rating = “XX”) ratings. For absent ratings, which served as a control condition for the CLOSE task, participants were told that the rater did not view their photo or failed to make a response to their photo fast enough (Fig. 1A). The CLOSE task included the following task conditions: a) Self-directed feedback but no partner-directed feedback (SNEGPXX and SPOSPXX), b) Partner-directed feedback but no self-directed feedback (SXXPNEG and SXXPPOS), c) Simultaneous congruent feedback (SNEGPNEG and SPOSPPOS), d) Simultaneous incongruent feedback (SNEGPPOS and SPOSPNEG), and e) No feedback for either self or partner (SXXPXX). Although we told participants they would receive likability ratings made by other participants, all of the “raters” were photos taken from the Chicago face database41. All photographs were black and white headshots of individuals making a neutral expression, and conditions were matched on perceived attractiveness, age, and race/ethnicity of the photos (using normed ratings of the Chicago face database).

Task timing and affective ratings

To create a more naturalistic context that minimizes demand characteristics and captures participants’ propensity to evoke empathy in a spontaneous fashion, we avoided explicitly instructing individuals to empathize with their partner. Following half of the trials within each condition, participants were asked to rate their current affective state by referring to the question “Right now, how do you feel, with -3 being very negative and 3 being very positive?”. There was a 1.3-second interval following the presentation of stimuli presenting likability ratings, and then participants were then given 3.9 s to supply their response with a button press placed in their dominant hand.

Participants completed 6 runs of the CLOSE task, and each run consisted of 36 trials with 4 trials per condition. The trial order was counterbalanced with a 1.3–7.8 s jittered fixation inter-trial interval, which was determined using the fMRI trial timing optimization program optseq242. In total, the duration of each run was approximately 5 min and 35 s. Two runs from one participant and one run from another were removed due to scanning issues.

Post-scan ratings and funnel debriefing

After the scan, participants completed separate retrospective questionnaires in which they rated their emotions and speculated about their partner’s emotions during each condition in the CLOSE task (from -3 (very negative) to 3 (very positive)). Two participants didn’t complete this questionnaire (N = 129).

Because of the deception in this study, participants also completed a funnel debriefing procedure after scanning in Session 2. They were asked to rate the following questions on a scale from 1 to 5: (1) “During the task, to what degree did you feel like the feedback you and your partner received about your likability was based on the photos taken of you and your partner in the first study session?” and (2) “During the task, to what degree did you feel like the feedback you and your partner received about your likability was made by other study participants?” Two participants did not report on these two questions (N = 129). The mean values of these two belief ratings were 3.6 (SD = 1.2) and 3.6 (SD = 1.3), respectively.

Trait and daily diary measures

Interpersonal reactivity index and difficulties in emotion regulation scale

On the scanning day, participants completed selected subscales from the IRI and DERS-18 to assess participants’ self-reported trait empathy and emotion regulation tendencies. Specifically, we administered the 21-item IRI, which includes three of the four standard subscales: (a) Empathic Concern, (b) Perspective Taking, and (c) Personal Distress. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from “Does not describe me well” to “Describes me very well.” In the current study, we focused on two key subscales most relevant to empathic responding: Empathic Concern (e.g., “I am often quite touched by things that I see happen”) and Perspective Taking (e.g., “I try to look at everybody’s side of a disagreement before I make a decision”)43.

The DERS-18 is a validated brief version of the original 36-item Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale44. It includes 18 items spanning six subscales: (1) Awareness, (2) Clarity, (3) Goals, (4) Impulse, (5) Nonacceptance, and (6) Strategies. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Almost Never” to “Almost Always” (e.g., “When I’m upset, I have difficulty getting work done”). Sum scores range from 18 to 90 with higher total scores indicate greater difficulties in emotion regulation44.

Partner-rated daily support provided in a daily diary survey

Participants completed an end-of-day online survey for the consecutive days spanning from Session 1 to Session 2, including questions assessing aspects of empathy and relationship characteristics. For the present study, to index daily support processes within couples, we assessed support provided to the partner using partner reports, an approach that helps increase accuracy and reduce self-reported biases45,46. Specifically, participants’ daily support provided to their partner was indexed by their partner’s responses to the item: “How much support did you receive from your partner today?” (0 = none, 4 = a great deal). Responses were averaged across daily surveys to obtain each participant’s mean daily supportive behaviors as reported by their partner. We excluded surveys that were not completed within 24 h of participants receiving the survey email to enhance the reliability of the daily diary data. In cases where ratings for certain items were missing, the mean scores were computed using only the available, non-missing data. On average, the time interval for this survey was 7.8 days (SD = 1.4), and participants submitted a mean of 7.2 diary entries during this period (SD = 1.5).

MRI data acquisition

Functional MR imaging was conducted with a 3-Tesla Siemens MRI scanner. Forty-two interleaved axial slices were acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging sequence with repetition time = 1300 ms, echo time = 30 ms, flip angle = 68°, field of view = 252 mm × 252 mm, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, matrix size = 84 × 84, and acceleration factor = 2. One hundred seventy-six high-resolution slices were obtained using a T1-weighted sagittal 3-D magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo sequence: repetition time = 2300 ms, echo time = 2.95 ms, inversion time = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, field of view = 270 × 270 mm, voxel size = 1.1 × 1.1 × 1 mm3, and matrix size = 256 × 256. Forty-two axial field map slices were acquired using a gradient-echo sequence: repetition time = 467 ms, echo time1 = 4.92 ms, echo time2 = 7.38 ms, flip angle = 60°, field of view = 224 × 224 mm, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm3, and matrix size = 74 × 74.

Data analysis

Behavioral analyses

At first, we examined participants’ affective ratings during scanning. Specifically, we tested the affect changes associated with the partner-alone feedback contrasts involving viewing partner-directed feedback alone (i.e., SXXPNEG-SXXPXX and SXXPPOS-SXXPXX), as well as the affective responses to incongruent feedback contrasts involving social feedback directed at one’s partner when receiving simultaneously incongruent social feedback about the self (i.e., SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX and SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX). Next, we conducted additional analyses to investigate the interference effect of incongruent self-directed feedback on spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback. Specifically, we compared affective ratings between the SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX and SXXPPOS-SXXPXX contrasts, as well as between SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX and SXXPNEG-SXXPXX contrasts.

Additionally, to assess affective experience sharing, we computed emotional similarity with one’s partner under each feedback condition by comparing a participant’s self-reported affective rating with their partner’s self-reported affect under the corresponding feedback condition during scanning. Specifically, emotional similarity with one’s partner in the SXXPPOS condition was computed as the negative value of absolute differences between a participant’s affective rating in the SXXPPOS condition with their partner’s self-reported affective rating in the SPOSPXX condition. A similar approach was taken with the SXXPNEG condition. Emotional similarity with one’s partner in the SNEGPPOS condition was computed as negative value of absolute differences between a participant’s affective rating in the SNEGPPOS condition with their partner’s self-reported affective rating in the SPOSPXX condition. Finally, emotional similarity with one’s partner in the SPOSPNEG condition was computed as negative value of absolute differences between a participant’s affective rating in the SPOSPNEG condition with their partner’s self-reported affective rating in the SNEGPXX condition. Next, we examined the associations between scores on the empathic concern subscale of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (EC_IRI), reflecting the affective component of empathy, and emotional similarity with the partner in partner-alone and incongruent feedback conditions, as well as the associations between DERS-18 scores and emotional similarity with the partner in incongruent feedback conditions.

Furthermore, to test the cognitive related process during the task, we investigated whether participants’ affective responses during scanning were associated with how they believed their partner felt in response to the positive or negative feedback, which assessed using retrospective questionnaires after scanning (i.e., inferred partner affect in the SXXPPOS or SXXPNEG condition). To account for the nested structure of contrasts within couples, linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept were employed to analyze all affective ratings described above, implemented using R software (R Core Team, version 4.4.0, https://www.r-project.org), with the lmerTest package.

Neuroimaging preprocessing

Functional imaging data were preprocessed using the robust open-source preprocessing pipeline, fMRIPrep (v23.0.1)47. Preprocessing steps included field map-based distortion correction (FUGUE), motion correction (MCFLIRT), slice timing correction (3dTShift), co-registration to T1-weighted structural images (bbregister), normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute 152NLin6Asym template with a 2-mm isotropic resolution through nonlinear registration (antsRegistration), spatial smoothing using an isotropic Gaussian kernel with a full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm, and automatic removal of motion artifacts using independent component analysis (ICA-AROMA)48.

General linear model analyses of empathy-related neural responses

The cleaned data from fMRIPrep were passed to FSL’s fMRI Expert Analysis Tool (FEAT, version 6.00, FMRIB’s Software Library, www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) for task-related general linear model analyses. The BOLD signal was modeled by convolving the onset and duration of 3.9-s feedback presentation phases for each of the 9 experimental conditions with a double-gamma hemodynamic response function. A separate regressor was also modeled to account for the affect ratings. Then, a second-level fixed-effects analysis was used to compute the average of the parameter estimates across the runs within each subject, and a third-level analysis with mixed effects (FLAME1) was used to test generalization to the population from which our sample was drawn.

We performed a series of whole-brain analyses to examine the BOLD correlates of empathy. First, we performed whole-brain analyses for contrasts approximating those used in previous research (i.e., SXXPPOS-SXXPXX and SXXPNEG-SXXPXX) to examine whether viewing positive and negative feedback directed toward one’s partner was associated with activations in canonical empathy regions. Next, we conducted whole-brain analyses to investigate the BOLD responses to social feedback directed at the partner in the presence of simultaneous incongruent social feedback about the self (i.e., SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX and SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX contrasts). Finally, we further investigated the disparities between the incongruent feedback contrast vs. partner-alone feedback contrasts (i.e., [SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX]-[SXXPNEG-SXXPXX] or [SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX]-[SXXPPOS-SXXPXX]) to investigate the effect of incongruent self-directed feedback on spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback. For these analyses, a voxel-wise threshold of z > 3.1 (p < 0.001) combined with a cluster level threshold of pGRF < 0.05 was used. As a post-hoc control analysis, considering variability across participants in belief in our cover story, or age and relationship duration, we reran the whole-brain analyses using participants’ reported belief, or age and relationship duration as covariates.

Relationships between brain activity, affective responses, trait empathy, difficulties in emotion regulation, and daily empathic behavior

We next investigated whether variability in brain activity during the incongruent feedback contrast involving the greatest demand for regulating responses to self-directed feedback (SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX) was related to individual differences in affective ratings, emotional similarity with the partner, inferred partner-affect, trait empathy, difficulties in emotion regulation, and real-world empathic behavior. Primary analyses focused on activity in spherical regions of interest (ROIs) (radius = 6 mm), masked to include voxels within the brain, spanning the AI, dACC, TPJ, and DMPFC, which are typically found in empathy literature10–12, the middle frontal gyrus (MFG), associated with emotion regulation39, and right SMG, identified as crucial for overcoming emotional egocentricity bias21. ROIs within the brain were defined based on the peak Montreal Neurological Institute coordinates obtained using the terms “empathy” and “emotion regulation” from Neurosynth (https://neurosynth.org), as well as the coordinates (68, -38, 36) reported by Silani et al. for overcoming emotional egocentricity bias21. We extracted values from individualized z-statistic maps from these ROIs and then standardized BOLD activity and behavioral data across subjects. Linear mixed-effect models (participants nested within couples) with a random intercept yielded standardized coefficients predicting affective responses, trait empathy, difficulties in emotion regulation, and partner-rated daily support provided to the partner. Model assumptions were evaluated using simulation-based residual diagnostics implemented in the DHARMa package, including tests of residual uniformity and dispersion. While most models showed no major violations of model assumptions, deviations from residual uniformity were observed in several cases. For these models, bootstrap-based confidence intervals and p values (5000 iterations) were additionally estimated to assess the robustness of the results.

Secondary exploratory analyses involving whole-brain multiple regression analyses allowed us to examine whether regions beyond our hypothesized ROIs were associated with affect-, empathy- and emotion regulation-related measurements. To provide more comprehensive information, we reported results using two multiple-comparison correction approaches: (1) a voxel-wise threshold of z > 2.58 (p 3.1 (p < 0.001) combined with cluster-level GRF correction (pGRF < 0.05), given that statistical models estimating between-person effects are generally more underpowered than those used for within-subject contrasts based on group-averaged responses49.

Results

Behavioral results

Affective ratings were associated with inferred partner affect and trait empathy

Behavioral data acquired from the CLOSE task provides converging evidence that the task was successful at evoking spontaneous empathy. We first examined participants’ self-reported affect associated with partner-alone feedback contrasts commonly used in previous research. As expected, linear mixed-effects models showed that participants reported more positive affect in the partner-positive feedback condition than the no feedback condition (SXXPPOS-SXXPXX, B = 0.58, SE = 0.05, t(130) = 12.91, 95% CI: [0.494, 0.671], p < 0.001, d = 1.19), and more negative affect in the partner-negative condition than the no feedback condition (SXXPNEG-SXXPXX, B = -0.57, SE = 0.05, t(130) = -11.69, bootstrapped 95% CI: [-0.670, -0.479], bootstrapped p < 0.001, d = -1.12) (Fig. 1B).

Next, we investigated how individuals responded emotionally to feedback directed at their partner, while simultaneously receiving conflicting feedback about themselves. Participants reported feeling more positive when self-negative feedback was accompanied by partner-positive feedback versus experienced alone (SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX: B = 0.40, SE = 0.05, t(130) = 8.41, 95% CI: [0.310, 0.498], p < 0.001, d = 0.64) and more negative when self-positive feedback was accompanied by partner-negative feedback versus experienced alone (SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX: B = -0.59, SE = 0.05, t(130) = -11.22, 95% CI: [-0.695, -0.488], p < 0.001, d = -1.03) (Fig. 1B).

As a preliminary test of cognitive process during the paradigm, we also investigated whether participants’ affective responses during the task were associated with how they believed their partner felt when observing their own feedback (i.e., post-scan inferred partner affect; Supplementary Fig. 1). Self-reported affect in the SXXPPOS and SXXPNEG condition during scanning was significantly positively correlated with post-scan inferred partner affect in the SXXPPOS and SXXPNEG condition, respectively (SXXPPOS: β = 0.27, SE = 0.09, t(127) = 3.20, 95% CI = [0.106, 0.441], p = 0.002, partial η² = 0.07; SXXPNEG: β = 0.26, SE = 0.09, t(127) = 3.06, 95% CI = [0.095, 0.430], p = 0.003, partial η² = 0.07; Fig. 1C). In other words, when participants felt that their partner felt worse (or better) across trials, participants felt correspondingly worse (or better). However, consistent with the hypothesized interference effect of simultaneous feedback trials, we didn’t find significant associations between affective responses in the SNEGPPOS (β = 0.07, SE = 0.09, t(126.29) = 0.84, 95% CI = [-0.098, 0.247], p = 0.400, partial η² = 0.006) or SPOSPNEG condition (β = 0.11, SE = 0.09, t(127) = 1.23, 95% CI = [-0.065, 0.281], p = 0.221, partial η² = 0.01) during scanning and post-scan inferred partner affect in the SXXPPOS or SXXPNEG condition.

Finally, we computed a metric of affective sharing (or emotional similarity) for each participant, defined as the negative absolute difference between participants’ own affect ratings and their partner’s affect ratings for the corresponding partner-directed feedback condition. For example, we calculated the differences between participants’ own ratings for SXXPNEG and SPOSPNEG (or SXXPPOS and SNEGPPOS) and their partner’s ratings when their partner observes SNEGPXX or SPOSPXX, respectively (Fig. 1D). This metrics reflects the degree to which participants’ feelings during the CLOSE task aligns with their partner’s actual feelings during corresponding feedback conditions. Relatedly, we examined whether individual differences in emotional similarity with one’s partner were associated with scores on EC_IRI, reflecting the affective component of empathy43. Results showed that emotional similarity with one’s partner in the SXXPPOS condition (β = 0.21, SE = 0.09, t(126) = 2.42, 95% CI = [0.040, 0.382], p = 0.017, partial η² = 0.04; Fig. 1E), but not the SXXPNEG condition (β = 0.01, SE = 0.09, t(126) = 0.16, 95% CI = [-0.160, 0.189], p = 0.874, partial η² < 0.001), was positively associated with scores on EC_IRI. That is, individuals with higher empathic concern tended to show greater emotional similarity with their partner’s feelings when their partner received positive feedback. We also conducted exploratory analyses examining the relationship between scores on perspective taking subscale of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (PT_IRI) and emotional similarity. These analyses did not reveal significant associations for both SXXPPOS (β = 0.06, SE = 0.09, t(126) = 0.66, 95% CI = [−0.115, 0.233], p = 0.508, partial η² = 0.003) and SXXPNEG condition (β = 0.06, SE = 0.09, t(126) = 0.710, 95% CI = [−0.111, 0.237], p = 0.479, partial η² = 0.004).

Self-directed feedback reduced affective responses to partner-directed feedback

We further examined whether simultaneous incongruent self-directed feedback interfered with spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback, by comparing incongruent feedback contrasts with partner-alone feedback contrasts. Participants’ affect was less improved by positive partner-directed feedback when participants themselves received negative feedback, compared to when negative self-directed feedback was absent ([SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX]-[SXXPPOS-SXXPXX], B = −0.18, SE = 0.04, t(130) = −4.10, 95% CI: [−0.264, −0.093], p < 0.001, d = −0.34). However, participants reported similar decreases in affect when their partners received negative feedback, irrespective of whether they received positive or no feedback ([SPOSPNEG-SPOSPXX]-[SXXPNEG-SXXPXX], B = -0.02, SE = 0.04, t(130) = −0.39, 95% CI: [−0.103, 0.068], p = 0.695, d = −0.03). These findings suggest that the presence of negative self-relevant feedback may be particularly disruptive for empathy.

Supporting these findings, emotional similarity with participants’ partners was significantly lower in the SNEGPPOS than the SXXPPOS condition (β = −0.26, SE = 0.05, t(127) = −4.89, bootstrapped 95% CI = [−0.357, −0.154], bootstrapped p < 0.001, d = −0.46), and in the SPOSPNEG than the SXXPNEG condition (β = −0.12, SE = 0.05, t(127) = −2.38, bootstrapped 95% CI = [−0.223, −0.022], bootstrapped p = 0.016, d = −0.19; Fig. 1D). As a final analysis, we investigated whether affective sharing with a partner during incongruent feedback conditions was linked to self-reported emotion regulation tendencies. Results showed that emotional similarity with one’s partner in the SNEGPPOS condition was negatively associated with total score on DERS-18 (β = −0.28, SE = 0.09, t(126) = −3.19, 95% CI = [−0.448, −0.107], p = 0.002, partial η² = 0.07; Fig. 1E), indicating that individuals with higher difficulties in emotion regulation showed lower affective sharing with their partner during the SNEGPPOS condition. To further clarify which aspects of emotion regulation were most closely linked to this effect, we conducted exploratory analyses on DERS-18 subscales. Significant associations emerged for the Goals (β = −0.29, SE = 0.09, t(116.08) = −3.36, 95% CI = [−0.455, −0.120], p = 0.001, pFDR = 0.006, partial η² = 0.09), Impulse (β = −0.24, SE = 0.09, t(126) = −2.70, bootstrapped 95% CI = [−0.409, −0.066], bootstrapped p = 0.007, pFDR = 0.014, partial η² = 0.05), and Strategies subscales (β = −0.25, SE = 0.09, t(119.96) = −2.84, bootstrapped 95% CI = [−0.419, −0.075], bootstrapped p = 0.004, pFDR = 0.012, partial η² = 0.06), but not the Awareness (β = −0.04, SE = 0.09, t(124) = −0.43, 95% CI = [−0.215, 0.137], p = 0.666, pFDR = 0.686, partial η² = 0.002), Clarity (β = −0.04, SE = 0.09, t(125.61) = −0.43, bootstrapped 95% CI = [−0.213, 0.141], bootstrapped p = 0.686, pFDR = 0.686, partial η² = 0.001), and Nonacceptance subscales (β = −0.17, SE = 0.09, t(125.05) = −1.93, 95% CI = [−0.337, 0.002], p = 0.056, pFDR = 0.083, partial η² = 0.03).

Discussion

Responding empathically in everyday life can be challenging, particularly when others’ emotional experiences conflict with one’s own mood or emotional state. These situations raise questions that are fundamental to a deeper understanding of our sociality: how do individuals navigate conflicting self- and other-related emotional information, and how does this process shape daily empathic responding? This exploratory study extends prior research by investigating spontaneous neural responses to partner-directed feedback within romantic relationships, when accompanied by incongruent self-directed feedback, and their associations with daily support provided to the partner. Notably, our study makes several contributions by examining empathy for both positive and negative partner’s social experiences, integrating laboratory-based neuroimaging with real-world behavioral assessments, and leveraging a diverse and relatively large sample of romantic partners. Results showed that when responding to partner-directed feedback paired with simultaneous incongruent self-directed feedback, participants evidenced activity in regions typically observed in empathy research, as well as frontoparietal regions frequently implicated in cognitive control, emotion regulation, and the mitigation of egocentric bias—especially when participants receive negative self-directed feedback. Recruitment of these regions was associated with greater affective empathy, higher trait empathy and lower difficulties in emotion regulation, and more frequent partner-rated daily support. These preliminary results are consistent with prior research reflecting engagement of empathic and emotion-regulatory processes10,12,38,39 and indicate that individual differences in emotion regulation tendencies relate to variability in spontaneous responses under conflicting social feedback situations. Notably, such spontaneous responses were also associated with real-world variability in empathic responding in romantic relationships.

During partner-alone feedback contrasts, we observed robust behavioral changes and BOLD activity in broad brain regions found in prior empathy-related investigations10,12. At the behavioral level, we found corresponding changes in self-reported affect for partner-directed feedback, and individuals with higher empathic concern exhibited greater alignment of emotional experiences with their partner, consistent with patterns commonly discussed in the context of empathic responding10,12. In addition, participants’ self-reported affective responses in the SXXPPOS or SXXPNEG condition during scanning were positively correlated with post-scan inferred partner affect in the SXXPPOS or SXXPNEG condition. This finding suggests that participants’ reactions to partner-directed feedback were influenced, at least in part, by inferences about their partner’s responses. Alternatively, individuals may use their own affective responses to simulate their inferred partner’s experiences, in line with self-projection51. At the neural level, the findings are consistent with the behavioral observations. The observed brain activity aligns with prior empathy research, which has shown that observing others’ pain or emotional experiences recruits not only brain regions involved in affective state sharing including the dACC and AI, but also regions involved in mentalizing including the TPJ, temporal pole, DMPFC, PCC, and precuneus10,12,52. Importantly, prior literature indicates that vicarious pain may rely upon imagining the state of the other, especially in cue-based paradigms where visual depictions of the body are absent53,54. Within this framework, mentalizing is widely recognized as a key pathway to empathy, as it enables individuals to imagine the condition of others, infer others’ internal states and, in turn, support empathic responses54,55. Taken together, while additional processes may be at play (see Limitations), these behavioral and neural findings provide strong converging evidence that our experimental task engages empathic processes within the context of social feedback35,40 and replicates similar neural activations observed in alternative contexts, such as those involving pain and social exclusion2,10,12.

During the incongruent feedback contrasts, when partner-directed feedback was accompanied by incongruent self-directed feedback, BOLD activity patterns also encompassed many of the regions mentioned above. However, recruitment of these regions was statistically weaker than in the partner-alone feedback contrasts, accompanied by lower affective responses and emotional similarity with the partner. Drawing on prior research, one interpretation of these findings is that incongruent self-directed feedback may interfere with spontaneous responses to partner-directed feedback, thereby resulting in attenuated empathic responses. Such incongruent conditions may impose greater cognitive and emotional demands, requiring participants to process two streams of information that evoke potentially conflicting affective states. This added complexity may further constrain the capacity for empathic engagement. For instance, immersing one arm into ice water tends to enhance self-focused attention56, which may inhibit affective sharing of others’ pain as individuals pay less attention to others’ feelings13. Likewise, self-relevant negative mood and stress can suppress empathic resonance for others’ pain16, and dampen the mentalizing process17,18. On the other hand, we observed activity in the DLPFC and right SMG, particularly when participants received negative self-directed feedback. These regions have previously been associated with cognitive control, emotion regulation38,39, and overcoming emotional egocentricity bias21. The recruitment of activity in these areas may reflect increased regulatory and attentional demands to direct attention, interpret or elaborate on emotional states when processing incongruent self-directed feedback. Furthermore, our results revealed that incongruent negative self-directed feedback primarily disrupted participants’ affective responses to partner-directed feedback. Moreover, higher emotional similarity with the partner in the SNEGPPOS condition was associated with lower self-reported difficulties in emotion regulation, particularly in goal-directed emotion regulation, impulse control, and access to adaptive regulation strategies. These preliminary findings are consistent with the notion, though speculative, that effective empathic engagement in this context may rely on individuals’ capacity to regulate their own emotional and behavioral responses, thereby allowing greater attunement to others’ experiences and feelings. This finding also aligns with prior evidence showing that regulating negative emotions is typically more challenging than regulating positive emotions38. Together, these findings suggest that the task manipulation effectively modulates demand for control, including emotion regulation, by varying the presence of incongruent self-directed feedback. Particularly, negative self-directed feedback may place higher cognitive and emotional demands on individuals’ responses to positive partner-directed feedback.

Our sample was relatively large for a brain imaging study, thus enabling us to explore sources of between-subject variability in BOLD activity during the incongruent-feedback situations. Results from both ROI and whole-brain analyses demonstrated that the scores on EC_IRI were associated with activity in brain regions commonly reported involved in affective sharing in empathy literature, especially the dACC10,12,52. These findings suggest that individual differences in empathic concern may contribute to between-subject variability in spontaneous responses in these conflicting self- versus partner-directed feedback situations. Furthermore, we also observed that, during the SNEGPPOS-SNEG contrast, greater self-reported difficulties in emotion regulation—particularly in awareness, goal-directed regulation, and access to adaptive strategies—were associated with lower activity in the DMPFC. This finding suggests that individual differences in self-reported emotion regulation tendencies are associated with variability in spontaneous responding to partner-directed feedback, when navigating incongruent feedback situations. In prior literature, the DMPFC has been linked to mentalizing10,57 and prosocial behaviors2,3,9,10. One possible interpretation is that individuals with greater difficulties representing internal affective states, regulating their own emotions, and maintaining goal-directed control under emotional conflict may show reduced engagement of higher-order integrative processes required for shifting between self- and other-oriented perspectives. This, in turn, may attenuate DMPFC recruitment and constrain the adaptive interpretation of partner-directed feedback. Additionally, DMPFC may also reflect emotion-regulatory control processes when managing affectively conflicting self-referential information58,59, suggesting a potential link to individual differences in emotion regulation difficulties. Converging with this neural finding, we also observed that when responding to positive feedback directed at their partner while simultaneously receiving negative feedback about themselves, individuals who showed stronger MFG engagement—a region involved in emotion regulation—tended to infer that their partner felt more positive affect following the partner’s positive feedback. Together, these findings align with prior research highlighting the link between self-reported emotion regulation and perspective-taking or empathy60,61. Therefore, we speculate that, when navigating incongruent feedback situations, individuals with stronger emotion regulation abilities may be more capable of managing their response tendencies to negative self-directed feedback, adopting their partner’s perspective, and showing affective resonance with their partner.

Of particular note, higher BOLD activity in the DMPFC was also associated with real-world supportive behavior, as reported by their partners in the daily diaries completed over the course of 7–10 days outside of the laboratory. This result is consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of MPFC during empathy in predicting prosocial behaviors2,3,9,10. Moreover, BOLD activity emerging in the SNEGPPOS-SNEGPXX contrast showed markedly stronger associations with daily support provided to the partner than the SXXPPOS-SXXPXX or SXXPNEG-SXXPXX contrast (Supplementary Table 10). Together, these findings suggest that the brain activity in this more challenging and ecologically valid context may be informative for understanding individual variability in empathic behavior in daily life.

Limitations

Although this study has several strengths—including a dyadic fMRI paradigm simulating everyday empathic contexts, a diverse and sizable sample, and partner-reported daily data—its findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations and opportunities for extension in future studies. First, although covariate analyses did not show evidence for a significant effect of participants’ belief in the cover story, future studies could adopt strategies to further enhance believability and minimize potential confounding processes, such as confusion or skepticism about the validity of the feedback received. Second, while the task aimed to elicit spontaneous empathic responses, the simultaneous feedback may have evoked other psychological states. Accordingly, our interpretation of the neuroimaging results is guided by the task design and observed behavioral patterns within an empathy framework, rather than assuming that each activated region reflects a single, process-specific function. We further note that these neural circuits, while commonly recruited during empathic engagement, are multifunctional and may also support broader social and cognitive processes62. For instance, engagement of the DMPFC may reflect not only mentalizing about the partner’s emotions, but also judgments about the rater’s intentions, or contempt toward someone rejecting one’s partner. Third, although we measured trial-by-trial variation in participants’ affective responses and post-experimental self-report measures, these can provide only indirect and potentially inaccurate accounts of participants’ experiences and therefore should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future studies could include direct self-report measures capturing different facets of empathy and participants’ perceptions of the difficulty of expressing empathy to better understand and interpret the empathic processes engaged during the task. Fourth, as daily support ratings were collected one week prior to scanning, it is possible that neural responses were shaped by recent interpersonal experiences. This interpretive ambiguity underscores the need for tighter temporal alignment between behavioral and neural measures, and ideally, the use of causal or longitudinal designs to assess directionality. Fifth, whether these findings extend beyond intimate relationships in social contexts remains an important avenue for future work. Incorporating additional conditions, such as familiar or neutral others, as well as non-social conditions would allow future work to disentangle partner-specific effects from broader social-cognitive or more domain general executive processes. Future studies could also employ real-time reciprocal interactions to better illuminate how empathy unfolds in dyadic contexts. Finally, it is worth noting that consistently suppressing one’s own needs in favor of supporting others may have negative mental health consequences63,64, while meeting one’s own needs may enhance one’s capacity to support others. Future research should explore how individuals navigate this balance, to inform a more nuanced understanding of how to sustain both well-being and effective interpersonal support.

Conclusions

In summary, this paper investigated spontaneous empathic responses during challenging social situations within romantic relationships and their associations with trait empathy, emotion regulation tendencies, and daily empathic behaviors. Using the CLOSE task, we found that responding to partner-directed feedback in conflicting social contexts, especially in the presence of incongruent negative self-related experience, was associated with regions within the frontoparietal areas (e.g., DLPFC, frontal pole, and SMG) beyond those typically observed in the empathy literature. When responding to positive partner-directed feedback in the presence of negative self-directed feedback, individual differences in self-reported difficulties in emotion regulation were related to BOLD activity in the DMPFC. These findings suggest that individual differences in self-reported emotion regulation tendencies may contribute to variability in empathic responding under incongruent feedback situations. Moreover, greater engagement of the DMPFC was associated with greater partner-rated daily supportive behaviors assessed outside the laboratory. Collectively, these findings shed light on within and between-person factors behind everyday empathy in close relationships, with implications for interventions targeting emotion regulation and empathic processes in romantic relationships. Future studies should further validate the present paradigm and replicate these findings.
