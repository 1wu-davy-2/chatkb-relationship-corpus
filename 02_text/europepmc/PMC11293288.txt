ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Women with schizophrenia have a high risk of adverse sexual health outcomes. The chronic course of schizophrenia draws attention to assessing the quality of life and marital relationships of patients with schizophrenia. However, there is a crunch of data in the Indian setting. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of sexual dysfunctions (SDs) and determine the marital relationship and quality of life among women with schizophrenia.Material and Methods:The study followed an analytical case-control design. The study was conducted at the inpatient psychiatric department of one of the apex tertiary healthcare centers located at the foothills of the Northern Himalayas. The study purposively included 136 participants (68 cases and 68 controls). The control group included healthy women aged 18–45 currently staying with sexual partners. Data were collected using pretested and standardized self-report questionnaires.Results:The median age of the case group was 36 (interquartile range (IQR), 29–42) years, while that of the control group was 29 (IQR 26–34) years. The prevalence of SD was significantly higher (P = 0.001) in cases (95.3%) than in controls (73.4%). Compared to healthy controls, women with schizophrenia have a significantly higher rate of SD in sexual desire (P = 0.001), getting arousal (P < 0.001), lubrication (P < 0.001), achieving orgasm (P < 0.001), and sexual satisfaction (P < 0.001) (except pain). In addition, the marital relationship was better managed (P < 0.001) in healthy controls compared with cases. The healthy control group significantly expressed a better quality of life in physical (P < 0.001), psychological (P < 0.001), and maintaining social (P < 0.001) relationships (except environment domains). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, employment status significantly predicted SDs (odds ratio (OR), 25.78; 95% confidence interval (CI), 2.09, 318.10; P < 0.05) in participants.Conclusion:The prevalence of SD is significantly high among female participants. Hence, treating physicians should pay attention to sexual function during the screening and treatment of patients with schizophrenia.Keywords: Marital relationship, quality of life, schizophrenia, sexual dysfunction, women

BODY
BACKGROUND

Sexual health has been considered a fundamental element of health, along with physical, psychological, and social relationships.[1] Having a pleasurable sexual relationship is crucial to marital satisfaction.[2] In a country like India, due to cultural barriers, societal taboos, and social stigma, patients often feel uncomfortable discussing their sexual issues, and physicians also avoid discussing sexual issues, fearing that it may cause trouble forming doctor-patient relationships.[3,4]

Sexual dysfunction (SD) is common in patients with schizophrenia and may be attributed to the chronic course of the disorder, the effects of psychotropic medications, and psychological and physical factors.[3] The prevalence of SD is reported to be 70% among women with schizophrenia.[5] SD may appear as low desire or poor sexual drive, erectile dysfunction, inadequate lubrication, pain during sexual intercourse, delayed or retrograde or premature ejaculation, and anorgasmia in people with mental illness.[6,7] Inadequate and chronic SD may lead to lower self-esteem, poor intimate partner relationships, poor partner satisfaction, frustration, and poor social and occupational functioning.[1] Also, impaired sexual functioning in women with schizophrenia may affect family creation and, thus, become barriers to fulfilling traditional and cultural expectations of family and society.[8] Also, SDs may impact the quality of life, cause difficulty forming and maintaining a family, adhering to treatment, or even discontinuing treatments, and may develop depression and suicidal ideations.[3,7,9,10] Studies on rates of SD among patients with schizophrenia are underreported by patients due to feelings of embarrassment or because they do not consider it a treatable problem.[11] Also, patients may feel uncomfortable discussing sexual problems because of cultural barriers or trust issues with the clinicians.[12] However, psychiatrists or treating physicians undervalue or often neglect the issue, considering it is not important or lacks time, which makes it more challenging to treat.[10]

A marital relationship is an overall feeling in the wife and husband of pleasure, happiness, and satisfaction with their marriage and relationship.[13] It appears to be a significant component of psychosocial well-being. Poor marital satisfaction adversely affects physical and psychological health and overall quality of life.[14,15] Few studies have reported poor marital functioning and higher divorce rates among those with schizophrenia.[16] Quality of life is a subjective and multidimensional component that refers to the perception of an individual about his health and well-being in the context of culture, value systems, expectations, standard of living, and fulfillment of desire.[17] Patients with schizophrenia and SD reported a lower quality of life as compared to patients without SDs.[18] The findings on the association between SD and quality of life are inconsistent and explored in very few studies in our country.[3] The lack of enough evidence, gap in the literature, and inconsistent findings indicate the need for more studies on the variables to generate a data pool. This was one of the reasons to take this study.

Despite the vital aspect of patient care, sexual functioning and poor marital relationship often get little attention, are underestimated, and are poorly managed. Additionally, our country has a crunch of research exploring all three variables in a single study: sexual functioning, marital relationships, and their impact on quality of life among patients with schizophrenia. Hence, this study was designed to examine the prevalence of SD, determine the marital relationship and quality of life in female participants with schizophrenia, and compare it with healthy controls. Also, it is not clear from the available studies whether there is an association between sexual functioning and marital relationship and impact on overall quality of life. The study findings will fill some of these gaps by providing information on the association of sexual functioning on marital relationship and quality of life in female participants. The authors assumed that women with schizophrenia may have marked SD, poor marital relationships, and poor quality of life.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Research Design and Setting: The study was conducted within the outpatient department of psychiatry at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Rishikesh, North India. An analytical case-control design was used to recruit married women aged 18–45 years diagnosed with schizophrenia as per the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), and under remission and attending regular follow-ups.

Participants: Participants were screened using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) to confirm the remission stage during follow-up at the outpatient department.[19] The women participants who scored less than 3 were considered in the remission phase to include in the study. In addition, women staying with a partner for more than six months, willing to participate, and able to speak Hindi or English were included in the study. In contrast, women with a history of other major psychiatric illnesses such as mania, depression, and delusional disorder, and medical conditions such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cancer, and cardiac problems such as a history of myocardial infarction, fibroids, prolapse, congenital Mullerian anomalies, vulvovaginal atrophy, premature ovarian failure, drug and alcohol abuse, hormonal imbalances, and medications other than psychotropic were excluded from the study. The control participants were in the same age group (18–45 years) with no history of medical illness such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer, cardiovascular and neurological issues, and psychiatric illness and verbally affirmed to stay with sexual partners.

Sample Size: The sample size was determined to obtain a representative sample using an effect size of 0.5 (type 1 error) and a power of 80% with an allocation ratio of 1:1 sample. The final sample size was 128 (64 cases and 64 controls) in the study.[20]

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Ethics Committee of All India Institute of Medical Sciences Rishikesh, Uttarakhand, approved (173/IEC/M.Sc./2022) the study. In addition, researchers collected individual consent from each participant and ensured the privacy and confidentiality of the information at every stage of the research.

Questionnaires

Standardized pretested instruments were used to collect information on the study variables. The details of the instruments used in the study are provided in the section.

Sociodemographic profile sheet: A pretested semi-structured pro forma was used to enquire about sociodemographic details such as age, education status, marital status, age of menarche, habitat, economic status, age at first pregnancy, number of children, and age of the oldest child.

Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI): A brief Hindi version of the self-report scale is used to study the main elements of sexual function in women. The scale consists of 19 questions on six areas of sexual functions: desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain, with a cutoff score of 26.55 to distinguish SD. The scale has excellent internal consistency (r = 0.83–0.96) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.74–0.86).[21]

Perceived Relationship Quality Component Scale (PRQC): A self-report Likert scale consists of 18 questions, which are further classified into six dimensions: satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, and love.[22] This 7-point Likert rating scale ranges from 1 (not at all) to 7 (completely) with a score ranging from 18 to 126, with lower scores interpreted as poor marital relationship quality and vice versa. The standard scale has good internal consistency (α = 0.78) reliability.[23] The authors translated the scales into the Hindi language for the interest of the participants. Experts validated the scale, and the split-half (r = 0.93) and Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.94) methods were employed to calculate reliability. In addition, the content validity of the Hindi scale was determined using the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) (13.64) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) (0.75) in the study. The tool was found valid and reliable for this study.

World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF Scale: A 5-point Likert rating scale was used to measure health-related quality of life in a healthcare setting in India.[24] The 26 items are further subdivided into four domains: physical relationship (seven items), psychological relationship (six items), social relationship (three items), and environmental health (eight items). The scale has been shown to have good reliability and validity in previous literature to measure the quality of life; in the Hindi version, the value of Cronbach’s alpha coefficients is 0.87–0.94.[24,25]

Data analysis

The data are transferred to a Microsoft Excel sheet and analyzed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P-value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. The Shapiro-Wilk test and the whisker plot were used to show the data distribution, and considering the nonnormal distribution of data, the median and quartiles were used to report the findings. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify the predictors of SDs among female participants.

Univariate logistic regression analysis indicated a significant association between SD and senior secondary education (odds ratio (OR), 0.66; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.18–2.41), employment status as a student and homemaker (OR, 7.25; 95% CI, 1.87–28.12), and marital relationship quality domains: satisfaction (OR, 0.36; 95% CI, 0.18–0.70), intimacy (OR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.13–0.59), trust (OR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.28–0.70), passion (OR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.22–0.64), and love (OR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.05–0.44). In addition, SD shows a significant association with quality-of-life domains, including physical (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–0.99), psychological (OR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.92–0.98), and social (OR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.93–0.99) relationships.

DISCUSSION

Female SD is reported to be one of the most common sexual disorders globally. Chronic SDs may lead to poor marital satisfaction, impaired relationships, especially between couples, lack of sexual satisfaction, poor libido, and performance anxiety, which can further lead to detrimental consequences, including violence, divorce, and ultimately poor mental health and quality of life.[26,27]

The findings revealed 95.3% of SD in female participants, higher than the previous study, which reported a prevalence of 84.2% in the studied women with schizophrenia.[4] Furthermore, the findings on SD agree with previous studies, which reported that more than 90% of women attending psychiatric departments suffer from one type of SD.[28] However, the prevalence of SDs reported varies from 40 to 73.3% among female participants in previous studies with different instruments.[29,30] Furthermore, a study conducted on Iranian women reported the prevalence of SD as 68.4% to 75.9%, which is slightly lower than our study findings.[31,32] Likewise, a Turkish and Chinese study on married women reported that the prevalence of SD was 47% and 43%, respectively.[26,33] These variations in the findings may be explained by sociocultural differences, different study populations, subject selection criteria, and the use of different instruments to measure SDs.

In our study, the FSFI score of healthy control group was significantly higher than the case group. This finding is consistent with other studies that SD is more frequent in patients with psychiatric illnesses.[34,35,36,37] In a study conducted with patients with depression, 68% of participants expressed dissatisfaction with their sexual life, 86% reported sexual intercourse does not arouse them, 68% reported impaired or retrograde ejaculation, 54% expressed a problem with lubrication, and 32% had pain during sexual intercourse.[30] In another prospective hospital-based study, female participants reported that not attaining enough lubrication (53%), painful intercourse (49%), poor sexual satisfaction, and failure to arouse during sexual intercourse are common SDs.[37] The authors speculate that lack of awareness about SD, cultural barriers restricting open discussion about sexuality, and stigma to seek treatment could be possible reasons for the high prevalence in the healthy control group. The reasons for the high prevalence of SD need to be explored by qualitative studies in the future.

Employment status emerged as the significant predictor of SD in female participants, which is consistent with the findings of the previous study.[38] Previous studies reported unemployment as a significant predictor of overall SD, erectile dysfunction, orgasmic disorder, arousal, and sexual pain disorder.[38] The same study has reported that being unemployed or employed as unskilled workers has higher odds of SDs, consistent with our findings. Regarding the marital relationship, our findings demonstrated that the healthy controls have a better marital relationship, which goes with the study that reported a lower satisfaction in spouses of schizophrenia. The findings reported poor satisfaction in relationships, regrets about the relationship, less love, and more problems.[39] Similarly, another hospital-based study from India concluded that spouses of patients with schizophrenia have poor marital functioning.[16] Additionally, it has been reported that patients with schizophrenia have poorer dyadic adjustment, marital quality, and higher marital maladjustment.[40] In addition, previous studies explored the concept of marital adjustment using a non-standardized scale[41] or different standardized scales,[40] making comparison challenging for the authors.

Examining the subscales of the World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQoL) score and evaluating the quality of life in our study sample demonstrate that the quality of life of healthy controls is better, especially in physical, psychological, and social relationships, than in women with schizophrenia. The findings on quality of life are consistent with the study conducted on patients with depression who reported a lower quality of life compared with healthy controls.[36] Furthermore, the quality of life was reported to be low in many previously published studies in patients with schizophrenia with SDs.[3,18,36,42] The findings on quality of life are consistent with previous Indian studies conducted on patients with depression and major mental illness with SD who reported poor quality of life.[42,43,44] Surprisingly, another study from India reported better quality of life in cases than controls, where controls scored significantly lower on domains of physical health, psychological status, and environmental domains of QoL.[42]

Evidence on the association or relationship of quality of life with SD in patients with severe mental disorders varies and is contradictory. There is ample evidence that patients with severe mental disorders with SDs have a poor quality of life, consistent with our study findings.[3,45] However, in patients with schizophrenia, the findings on quality of life range from poor quality of life to no association with SD.[18,46] In our study, the quality of life is significantly associated with SD except in the environmental domain, which agrees with many previous studies in patients with severe mental disorders.[47,48]

Such a variation in findings may be attributed to using different methodologies such as design, settings, and selection of younger dyads than patients, or other factors such as role and family responsibilities of dyads, which recommend multicenter and homogenous sample selection in future studies to reach a specific conclusion.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY

The present study is a modest attempt to compare and study the prevalence of SD, marital relationship quality, and quality of life among women with schizophrenia. The study included married, exclusively included patients with schizophrenia, and adhered to strict exclusion criteria, especially excluding patients with comorbid conditions, and the selection of standardized instruments and choosing a healthy control are some of the merits of the study.

However, the study result should be appraised under many limitations; findings with the selection of only female patients with schizophrenia could not be generalized for the whole population. In addition, the nonnormal distribution of data may be considered a statistical weakness in applying robust statistical tests to reach a specific conclusion. Furthermore, the sample was not matched to age, education, employment status, and other variables of the cases and controls, which could have affected the actual comparison and reporting of SD and quality of life. In future studies, matched cohorts’ male and female populations should be included so that gender differences in study variables can be studied. These limitations need to be addressed by future studies. Therefore, a study with a matched sample is recommended to understand the actual reporting on SD, marital relations, and quality of life. Despite these limitations, the study findings have expanded knowledge about SDs in women with schizophrenia.

CONCLUSION

The study reported a high prevalence of SDs among women with schizophrenia. The marital relationship quality and quality of life are significantly better in healthy controls than in cases. Hence, it is recommended to make routine screening for SD in psychiatric patients to provide tailored psychosocial interventions to improve marital relationships and quality of life.
