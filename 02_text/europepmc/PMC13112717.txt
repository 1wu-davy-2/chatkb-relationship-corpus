ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterpersonal attraction, encompassing the positive feelings, perceptions, and behavioral tendencies toward others, plays a fundamental role in the formation and maintenance of romantic relationships. Despite its importance, validated measures of this construct remain scarce outside English-speaking contexts. The Interpersonal Attraction Scale (IAS), measuring social, physical, and task attraction, has been widely used but never validated in Central Europe. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the IAS for Slovak couples, develop shortened versions, and establish population norms.MethodsThe sample comprised 1,090 individuals forming 545 married or cohabiting couples from Slovakia. The original 30-item IAS was adapted into a 21-item Slovak version (IAS-21), from which a 9-item version (IAS-9) was derived. The sample was randomly split for exploratory factor analysis (EFA; n = 544) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; n = 546). Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega. Construct validity was examined through correlations with the Couples Satisfaction Index (CSI-16). Mokken scale analysis evaluated scalability and unidimensionality of subscales. Normative data were calculated separately for men and women.ResultsEFA of the IAS-21 revealed a four-factor structure, with reverse-coded items forming a separate method factor. CFA of the IAS-9 confirmed the original three-factor model with satisfactory fit (CFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.973, RMSEA = 0.069, SRMR = 0.039). Both versions demonstrated excellent internal consistency (IAS-21: α = 0.95; IAS-9: α = 0.89) and strong correlations with relationship satisfaction (r = .80 for IAS-21 total score). Mokken analysis confirmed scalability of both versions (H = 0.481 for IAS-21; H = 0.502 for IAS-9), supporting independent subscale use.ConclusionsThe Slovak IAS-9 provides a psychometrically robust and efficient measure of interpersonal attraction in romantic dyads, while the IAS-21 offers a more comprehensive alternative. This study represents the first validation of the IAS in a non-English-speaking European context, contributing to cross-cultural comparability in attraction research. Both versions are recommended for use in research and clinical practice with couples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04379-8.Keywords: Confirmatory factor analysis, Factor structure, IAS, IAS-21, IAS-9, Interpersonal attraction, Mokken analysis, Psychometric properties, Validation

BODY
Introduction

Interpersonal attraction

Attraction drives interpersonal interaction in many ways in the context of creating or strengthening various types of relationships. Interest in this construct surged in the 1960s, spurred by a wave of studies, theoretical explorations, and the development of measurement instruments [1–3].

In psychological research, attraction is often conceptualized as a multifaceted attitude encompassing three key components: (1) an evaluative or emotional component, which reflects the strength and quality of one’s feelings toward another person; (2) a cognitive component, referring to the beliefs and thought processes through which one forms perceptions of the other; and (3) a behavioral component, which concerns the inclination to seek or avoid interaction with the person and the ways in which this tendency is expressed in actions [4]. More broadly, the construct is generally understood as a favorable perception of another person, encompassing emotional, cognitive, and behavioral dimensions. Several definitions in the literature describe this phenomenon as a positive perception of another person on multiple levels. Some perspectives further empathize that attraction can be understood as a phenomenon that varies in intensity toward another person [5]. Other definitions frame it as an affective evaluation of another person, shaped by the interplay between the characteristics of both individuals, the target’s behavior, the situational context, and the perceiver’s personality [6]. In addition, interpersonal attraction has been described as a motivational state that inclines individuals to think, feel, and behave positively toward another person [7].

Interpersonal attraction has commonly been conceptualized as consisting of three distinct dimensions: social attraction, physical attraction, and task attraction [1]. Of these, physical attraction has received the greatest scholarly attention.

Physical attraction is viewed as the extent to which an individual is perceived as appealing based on outward appearance, including dress and physical features [1]. From an evolutionary perspective, features such as facial symmetry, averageness, and sexual dimorphism serve as important indicators of partner attractiveness [8, 9]. Empirical findings suggest that people are remarkably quick to form impressions of another person’s attractiveness. For example, research has shown that individuals can assess their perceived attractiveness to a potential partner in a single glance, taking as little as 100 milliseconds [10]. Such perceptions can influence behavior, or instance, women may be more likely to use makeup or adjust their clothing when anticipating an encounter with someone they find attractive [11], while men are generally more willing than women to engage in sexual activity with a partner they do not find physically appealing [12]. Physical attraction is further shaped by relationship dynamics, such as conflict, dishonesty, or controlling behavior, which may reduce perceived attractiveness [13]. Importantly, it has also been shown to predict both sexual and relationship satisfaction [14]. Recent research suggests that interpersonal attraction is dynamically co-regulated within real-time interactions. For instance, physiological and behavioral synchrony between partners has been shown to be associated with increased perceived romantic attractiveness, highlighting the role of interactive, moment-to-moment processes in attraction formation [15].

Social attraction refers to the degree to which individuals are perceived as desirable companions, emphasizing the desire to establish social relationships and engage in interaction based on perceptions of friendliness and likability [1]. This dimension has frequently been examined in relation to social media interaction [16, 17] as well as processes of self-dislosure [18, 19]. Research also shows that responsiveness plays a role in shaping social attraction, with interaction partners who respond more promptly being perceived as more socially attractive than those who are less responsive [20]. Recent conceptual and review studies emphasize that the foundational social mechanisms of attraction—such as familiarity, similarity, and perceived responsiveness—remain key in modern digital and romantic contexts [21, 22]. These findings underscore that interpersonal attraction is influenced not only by static traits but also by ongoing social exchanges and mutual attunement.

Task attraction is defined as the degree to which an individual is perceived as a desirable partner for accomplishing tasks, reflecting how easy, effective, or worthwhile it would be to work with that person [1]. Research involving previously unacquainted young adults paired in a speed-dating context has shown that task attraction was the only dimension significantly associated with behavioral synchrony [23].

Measuring attraction

Interpersonal attraction has been assessed using a range of psychometric instruments, each designed to capture different aspects of the construct. For the present study, we employed the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] which is described in more detail later. Other measures include the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [3], a six-item tool assessing liking and attraction toward another person across dimensions such as intelligence, knowledge of current events, morality, adjustment, personal feelings, and cooperation, with responses provided on a 7-point scale. The Measure of Initial Attraction [24] focuses on unilateral initial attraction and comprises 31 items, divided into arousal and interest components, also rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Another instrument, the Physical Attraction Scale [13] measures attraction across cognitive, emotional, and behavioral domains using 30 items and a 5-point Likert format. In addition, several instruments conceptualize attraction in closer connection with romantic love. For example, the Love and Liking Scale [25] consists of 30 items divided evenly between love and liking, while the Passionate Love Scale [26] contains 30 items reflecting emotional, cognitive, and behavioral components of passionate love, the latter employing a 9-point response scale.

The development of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale

The Interpersonal Attraction Scale (IAS) [1] was developed in the 1970s in the United States by McCroskey and McCain. Its conceptual foundation is rooted in earlier research on interpersonal communication, which emphasized that individuals tend to engage more frequently in communication with those to whom they feel attracted [2]. In addition, previous work suggested that the degree of interpersonal attraction is associated with the extent to which another person can influence our behavior and communication patterns [2].

Prior studies also highlighted the multidimensional nature of interpersonal attraction. For instance, earlier research identified several components of attraction, including socioemotional qualities, physical attractiveness, personal traits, and perceived considerateness [27, 28]. Building upon these findings, McCroskey and McCain conceptualized interpersonal attraction as a multidimensional construct comprising three distinct dimensions: (1) social attraction referring to likability and the desire to interact socially, (2) physical attraction relating to outward appearance and physical appeal, and (3) task attraction reflecting perceptions of competence and the ability to collaborate effectively [1].

The original validation study of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] was conducted on a sample of 215 undergraduate students from Illinois State University. Participants were instructed to complete the instrument with reference to a classmate with whom they were familiar. The scale, comprising 30 self-report items rated on a Likert-type scale, was designed to measure three distinct dimensions of interpersonal attraction: social attraction, physical attraction, and task attraction. Each subscale included ten items, five of which were negatively worded to reduce response bias. In addition to the original 30-item version, the authors also introduced shorter forms of the IAS. An 18-item version was created by selecting the highest-loading items identified through factor analysis, comprising five items each for the social and task attraction subscales and seven items for physical attraction. From this set of items, a further refined 15-item version was developed by retaining the five strongest-loading items for each of the three dimensions.

To examine the internal structure of the instrument, the authors conducted a factor analysis using varimax rotation, which confirmed the presence of the three theoretically proposed dimensions of interpersonal attraction. The 15-item version [1] demonstrated acceptable internal consistency across all subscales. The social attraction subscale, which reflects personal likability, was best represented by the item “I think he (she) could be a friend of mine,” and showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84. The physical attraction subscale, which pertains to physical appearance and clothing, included the representative item “I think he (she) is quite handsome (pretty),” with a reliability coefficient of 0.86. Finally, the task attraction subscale, referring to the perceived ease and effectiveness of working with someone, was exemplified by the reverse-scored item “I couldn’t get anything accomplished with him (her),” yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81. Subsequent studies employing the IAS also replicated the same three-factor structure, even when modified versions of the original 30-item scale were used e.g. [29, 30].

After three decades, McCroskey and colleagues developed second-generation measures of the attraction scales, which were administered alongside the homophily scales [31] originally proposed in earlier research [32]. As noted earlier, interpersonal attraction represents a central aspect of interpersonal communication [2], while homophily, defined as perceived similarity between individuals, constitutes another important component of interpersonal relationships. The homophily scale consists of two dimensions: attitude and background [32]. In this line of work, the original 30-item IAS was expanded by adding additional items, resulting in a revised instrument with 12 items for social attraction, 12 items for physical attraction, and 14 items for task attraction. This expanded version demonstrated high internal consistency, with reliability coefficients ranging from 0.91 to 0.96 31. Intercorrelations between this 38-item version of the IAS and the revised homophily scale provided empirical support for the theoretical assumption that perceptions of homophily account for a substantial portion of variance in perceived attraction. Factor analysis using oblique rotation again supported the presence of a three-factor solution. Although each item loaded most strongly (≥ 0.70) on its intended factor (social, physical, or task attraction), many items also showed secondary loadings (0.40–0.60) on other dimensions. These findings suggest that, while the three factors can be regarded as distinguishable, they are not entirely independent and may covary across different interpersonal contexts.

Based on these findings, the authors advised researchers to approach item reduction with caution. They emphasized that shortening these scales may threaten internal reliability and has been empirically shown to reduce both reliability and validity. Such reductions may attenuate observed effects and lead to underestimation of explained variance and effect sizes [31].

Psychometric properties and factor analysis of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale

A comprehensive review of studies employing the IAS since its original development was conducted in earlier work [31]. This review identified more than 40 representative studies that utilized the IAS in various contexts to examine interpersonal attraction. Across these studies, numerous adaptations of the original 30-item scale were reported, with some studies focusing on selected subscales rather than the full measure. Notably, the physical attraction dimension was used less frequently compared to the social and task attraction subscales. Overall, the authors concluded that the IAS demonstrated generally good, though not consistently excellent, internal reliability across studies. Lower reliability was observed particularly in studies that employed abbreviated versions of the scale. Reported internal consistency for the social attraction subscale ranged from 0.67 to 0.93 e.g., see studies with highest reliability [33–35]. For the physical attraction subscale, Cronbach’s alpha values ranged from 0.66 to 0.95, with the highest reliability reported in [35–37]. The task attraction subscale showed internal consistency ranging from 0.69 to 0.93, indicating acceptable reliability in several studies [37–39]. More recent investigations using the IAS have also reported acceptable to high internal consistency across its subscales, further supporting the scale’s reliability in diverse contexts and populations [40, 41].

The same review also examined evidence regarding the validity of the IAS across a wide range of research applications [31]. Interpersonal communication emerged as the construct most commonly assessed in relation to interpersonal attraction, with numerous studies demonstrating significant associations, particularly within the dimensions of social and task attraction e.g., [39, 42, 43]. Although less frequently studied, the IAS has also been applied in contexts involving dyadic or romantic relationships, such as cross-sex dyads [44, 45], romantic partners [36], and family relationships [46]. In these contexts, greater attention has been paid to the physical attraction subscale, which was otherwise less commonly examined in many of the studies included in the review. In terms of construct validity, several studies have used the Interpersonal Judgment Scale (IJS) [3] to examine the convergent validity of the IAS, including work by [47, 48]. More recent work has continued to employ the IAS in diverse contexts, providing additional support for its validity. For instance, one study reported evidence of adequate convergent and discriminant validity [49], while another investigation demonstrated satisfactory convergent validity of the IAS [50].

With regard to the factorial structure of the IAS, earlier research summarized in the review indicated that the original three-factor structure has been examined in numerous studies [36, 51, 52]. These investigations consistently supported the existence of three distinct subscales: social attraction, physical attraction, and task attraction. However [31], also pointed out that, although these dimensions appear to be stable and distinguishable, some earlier research questioned their orthogonality [53]. Consequently, it has been suggested that the dimensions of attraction should be treated as multidimensional constructs, without presupposing either orthogonality or obliquity in their interrelationships [31]. In contrast, relatively few recent studies have examined the factorial structure of the IAS. For example, one study adapted the 15-item version of the IAS and evaluated its structure using confirmatory factor analysis [50]. The results indicated a poor model fit (CFI = 0.817, RMSEA = 0.084, SRMR = 0.073, χ²(87) = 566.21, p < .001). However, after the removal of two poorly performing items, the model fit improved substantially, yielding satisfactory fit indices and suggesting that a slightly shortened version of the scale may provide a more robust representation of the underlying three-factor structure.

Variations and language adaptations of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale

Although the original IAS [1] consisted of 30 items across three subscales, numerous studies have employed modified versions differing in length. For instance, a 15-item form [50, 54] or an 18-item adaptation [41, 49]. In addition to these shortened formats, researchers have applied the scale in various ways: some have utilized all three subscales [40, 41], others have focused on particular combinations, such as social and task attraction [55] or physical and social attraction [56], while certain studies have examined only a single dimension, for example social attraction [57] or physical attraction [58].

Despite its widespread use in English-speaking countries, linguistic adaptations of the IAS remain relatively limited. One such adaptation [59] was conducted in India to examine the role of religion in interpersonal attraction, specifically whether individuals were more inclined to perceive attraction toward members of their own religion or another religion. The study involved 827 participants identifying as either Hindu or Muslim and employed the 15-item IAS in both English and Hindi. Participants completed the scale twice: once for a target of the same religion and once for a target of a different religion. Reported Cronbach’s alpha values for attraction toward same-religion targets were α = 0.73 (social), α = 0.71 (physical), and α = 0.74 (task). For different-religion targets, the corresponding values were α = 0.74, α = 0.72, and α = 0.77. However, the authors did not conduct additional psychometric analyses to further validate the Hindi adaptation.

Another study conducted with a sample of 109 Hong Kong youths examined the relationship between ingratiation, perceived attractiveness, and interpersonal relationships [60]. Although the research took place in a Chinese context, the authors did not specify the language in which the IAS was administered, making it unclear whether this can be considered a true linguistic or cultural adaptation. The study employed 11 items from the IAS (not specified in detail) and reported results only for the social and physical attraction subscales. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.73 for social attraction, 0.85 for physical attraction, and 0.73 for the total score. For validity, IAS scores were correlated with measures of ingratiation and interpersonal relationship quality [61]. An exploratory factor analysis with varimax rotation revealed a two-factor structure.

A further investigation employed a Bengali adaptation of the 15-item IAS [54]. This research focused on examining differences in interpersonal attraction among 300 Bangladeshi students with respect to gender, religion, and socioeconomic status. However, apart from reporting results related to the study’s primary objectives, the authors did not provide information regarding the psychometric properties of the scale or its underlying factor structure.

Research rationale and aim of the study

Interpersonal attraction represents a central aspect of human relationships. It plays an important role in several domains of social functioning, including interpersonal communication [2], perceptions of homophily with others [45], as well as within social [17, 20], physical [11, 12], and task-related contexts [23]. Although several instruments exist that either directly measure interpersonal attraction [1, 31] or capture specific facets of the construct [3, 25], the Interpersonal Attraction Scale (IAS) was selected for the present study, as it is considered one of the most comprehensive measures currently available. Previous research has demonstrated solid psychometric properties of the IAS across various studies [40, 41, 49, 50].

At the same time, earlier work has cautioned against excessive reduction of items in these scales, emphasizing the potential negative consequences for reliability, validity, and effect size estimation [31]. However, contemporary psychological research frequently relies on extensive questionnaire batteries, which may reduce participants’ motivation and engagement when completing lengthy assessments [62]. Taking these considerations into account, we opted for a balanced approach in adapting the IAS, as described below, to ensure both sufficient coverage of the construct and greater feasibility for participants.

Despite several language adaptations of the IAS [54, 59, 60], evidence on its psychometric properties remains largely limited to English-speaking contexts. Cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation are essential when applying psychological instruments in different linguistic and cultural contexts, as cultural factors may influence the interpretation of items and underlying constructs [63]. To our knowledge, no adaptation of this scale has been conducted within the European context outside of English-speaking countries. Moreover, in Slovakia, no standardized measures of interpersonal attraction currently exist. This gap highlights a clear need for the adaptation and validation of the IAS in the Slovak setting. In addition, given the multidimensionality of interpersonal attraction, the scale holds promise for application across diverse research samples and contexts within Slovak psychology.

Moreover, the IAS [1] was not originally developed specifically for romantic couples, therefore, examining its psychometric properties in a couple-based sample is important to ensure its suitability within the relational context of intimate partnerships. Thus, the aim of this study was to translate the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] into Slovak and to adjust a shortened version consisting of 21 items, with seven items representing each of the three subscales: social, physical, and task attraction. Subsequently, the study aimed to examine the psychometric properties and factor structure of the 21-item Slovak version within a representative sample of Slovak couples. Reliability was expected to be within an acceptable range, and validity was examined through correlations with the Couples Satisfaction Index (CSI-16) [64], with the expectation of significant positive associations between the IAS-21 total score, its subscales, and the CSI-16. The factor structure was also assessed, with the hypothesis that three factors would emerge, consistent with the original study [1]. Furthermore, Mokken scale analysis was conducted to examine the scalability of the IAS-21, and normative data for the Slovak population were established.

Method

Participants

The present sample comprised 1,090 individuals forming 545 couples from Slovakia. Participants included 551 men (50.55%), 538 women (49.36%), and one individual (0.09%) who preferred not to disclose their gender. The sample consisted predominantly of heterosexual couples (n = 528), with 11 male same-sex couples and 5 female same-sex couples. In one couple, the gender of one partner was not reported. Ages ranged from 18 to 88 years, with a mean age of 46.12 years (SD = 13.45).

Eligibility criteria for participation required individuals to be adults (i.e., 18 years or older) and to be currently involved in a romantic relationship. Regarding relationship status, the majority of participants (78.44%; n = 855) were married, while 21.56% (n = 235) were in a non-marital cohabiting partnership. The average duration of participants’ current relationships was 19.60 years (SD = 13.51). The mean number of children per couple was 1.35 (SD = 1.04). On average, couples spent 21.20 h per week together (SD = 24.63). In terms of educational attainment, 0.92% (n = 10) had completed only primary education, 55.32% (n = 603) held a secondary school diploma, and 43.76% (n = 477) had obtained a university degree.

The sample size was determined based on recommendations for scale validation studies, suggesting at least 10 participants per item [65]. Given that the Interpersonal Attraction Scale (IAS-21) includes 21 items, a minimum of 210 participants was required. Our final sample of 1,090 respondents thus exceeded this threshold and provided sufficient statistical power for all planned analyses.

Measuring instrument

The Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] is designed to assess interpersonal attraction across multiple dimensions. The authors emphasize that interpersonal attraction is not a unidimensional construct, therefore, the scale comprises three distinct subscales: (1) social attraction, which refers to personal liking or the desire for friendship (e.g., “I would like to have a friendly chat with him/her”); (2) physical attraction, which pertains to perceived physical appeal, including appearance and clothing (e.g., “He/She is very sexy looking”); and (3) task attraction, which involves perceptions of someone’s dependability and competence in collaborative efforts (e.g., “If I wanted to get things done, I could probably depend on him/her”). The IAS uses a 7-point Likert-type response format, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). While the original version contains 30 items, shorter forms have also been used. For instance [30], employed a 22-item version, whereas [1] reported a rotated factor matrix for the 18 best-fitting items of the IAS. Furthermore, in a replication of the original IAS study [1], was used 15 items from the original scale, with five items representing each subscale [66].

For the purposes of our study, we created a 21-item Slovak version of the IAS. This version includes all 15 items from the short form identified by the authors [66] as the highest-loading items based on factor analysis. To this core, we added two additional items per subscale from the original 30-item version, resulting in 7 items per subscale. For each subscale, we selected the two additional items most relevant to romantic relationships, given that our sample consisted of Slovak couples and we plan to apply the scale in future couple-based research. The selection was conducted by the second author (JH), an experienced couples psychotherapist, to ensure practical relevance of the items to romantic relationships. From the social attraction subscale, we included the items ‘He (she) would be pleasant to be with’ and ‘I feel I know him (her) personally’. The physical attraction subscale was composed of the items ‘She (he) is well groomed’ and ‘He (she) is repulsive to me’. Finally, for the task attraction subscale, we selected ‘If we put our heads together I think we could come up with some good ideas’ and ‘He (she) would be fun to work with’. Thus, each subscale of the IAS-21 includes three reverse-coded items, in accordance with the original scale. Additionally, to ensure conceptual and linguistic alignment with romantic relationships, we made minor wording adaptations to several items. Specifically, we reformulated items originally written in the subjunctive (e.g., “I think he (she) could be a friend of mine”) into the indicative mood (e.g., “I think my partner is a friend of mine”), as the scale was administered to individuals currently in committed romantic relationships. Furthermore, to enhance clarity and personal relevance, we replaced pronouns “he/she” with the term “my partner” across all items. These adjustments maintained the original meaning while increasing the applicability and face validity of the items for partnered participants. Total scores on IAS-21 can range from 21 to 147, with higher scores indicating greater interpersonal attraction. In our sample, the total scores ranged from 37 to 147 (M = 123.39, SD = 20.21). The mean scores for each subscale were as follows: social attraction (M = 41.85, SD = 7.79), physical attraction (M = 42.45, SD = 7.20), and task attraction (M = 41.09, SD = 7.69).

To assess the construct validity of the IAS-21, we used the 16-item Slovak version of the Couples Satisfaction Index [67]. The CSI, originally developed by [64], is a unidimensional instrument of relationship satisfaction that uses a Likert-type format, with the first item rated on a 7-point scale and all remaining items rated on a 6-point scale. Sample items include statements such as “Our relationship is strong” and questions like “How rewarding is your relationship with your partner?”. [64] reported excellent internal consistency for the CSI-16, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.98, while the Slovak adaptation demonstrated the same level of reliability. In the original study, validity was evaluated using several instruments, including the Dyadic Adjustment Scale [68], whereas in the Slovak validation it was examined through correlations with the Revised Adjustment Scale [69], both yielding significant associations. The CSI-16 produces scores ranging from 0 to 81, with higher values indicating greater relationship satisfaction. Scores below 51.5 typically reflect notable dissatisfaction within the relationship.

Procedure

The Slovak version of the IAS-21 was translated and back-translated following Brislin’s [70] method. Discrepancies between the translations were resolved through consensus by two co-authors and an expert panel with the entire process overseen by the second author. The full English version of the IAS-21 is provided in Appendix 1, and the full Slovak translation is provided in Appendix 2. Appendix 3 presents a comparison of the original items and our adjusted items adapted to fit couple relationships.Data collection took place online between November and December 2024, conducted by the Slovak research agency Go4insight. All participants provided informed consent electronically before participating in the study. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the affiliated university and was conducted in line with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical frameworks.

Participants completed the 21-item version of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] as part of a larger set of standardized self-report questionnaires focused on couple relationships. This battery included several instruments used in other ongoing research projects, among them the Couples Satisfaction Index [64], which was used in the present study to examine the construct validity of the IAS-21.

Participant recruitment was carried out using a representative sampling strategy organized by Go4insight. Participants received a financial incentive upon completing the questionnaire; however, the incentive was only awarded once both partners in the couple had fully completed the survey. This approach ensured data collection from both members of each couple and contributed to the integrity of dyadic data.

Data analysis

For the data analysis, we employed two statistical software packages. Data entry and descriptive analyses were carried out using JASP (version 0.19.3.0). This program was also used to assess reliability and validity, and to perform factor analyses. Additional procedures, including Mokken scale analysis and the establishment of norms, were conducted using R (version 4.3.1). The data were initially analyzed by the first author and subsequently reviewed and verified by the second author. Each analytical step was chosen to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the psychometric properties of the Slovak version of the scale.

Results

Descriptive analysis

We conducted a descriptive analysis of the Slovak version of the IAS-21, reporting means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis for each item, as presented in Table 1. Given the large sample size (N = 1090), we did not perform formal tests of normality, as such tests tend to be overly sensitive with large datasets. Instead, skewness and kurtosis values were examined to assess the distributional characteristics of the data. Based on established criteria [71], absolute skewness values below 2 and kurtosis values below 7 were considered indicative of acceptable univariate normality. Except for IAS14, which showed slightly elevated kurtosis, all items were within the recommended thresholds for univariate normality. Thus, the item distributions can be considered adequately distributed for subsequent analyses.

Factor structure

Since we shortened the original 30-item version of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] to 21 items (as described in the Methods), and because this shorter version has not yet undergone a formal factor validation, we decided to perform both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). EFA was conducted first because the factorial composition of the new 21-item version was unknown — we needed to empirically identify the latent structure without imposing any prior constraints. In addition to the item reduction, the wording of items was slightly adapted to better reflect to context of romantic relationships, and the scale was administrated to a sample of romantic couples. Because the IAS [1] was not originally developed specifically for romantic partners, these modifications and the specific relational context could potentially influence the underlying factor structure. Therefore, conducting EFA as an initial step allowed us to explore the latent structure of the adapted instrument. CFA was then used to test whether the factor structure suggested by the EFA (and theoretically expected based on the original IAS) provided a satisfactory fit to the data. To avoid overfitting, the full dataset was randomly divided into two subsamples, with 272 couples used for the EFA and 273 couples used for the CFA. This two-step approach (EFA → CFA on independent samples) ensures that the structure discovered in one dataset is cross-validated in another, thereby enhancing the robustness and generalizability of the model. Given the overall large sample size and balanced distribution across both subsamples, the analyses retained adequate statistical power and precision, ensuring stable and reliable factor solutions. This methodological strategy aligns with psychometric recommendations emphasizing validation on independent or randomly split samples to enhance robustness and minimize sample-specific bias [72, 73].

Exploratory factor analysis

The exploratory factor analysis was performed using oblique promax rotation, allowing for correlations between factors. The analysis resulted in the extraction of four factors, together explaining 61.30% of the total variance (Factor 1 = 17.40%, Factor 2 = 15.70%, Factor 3 = 15.30%, and Factor 4 = 13.00%). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was excellent (KMO = 0.935), indicating that the data were well-suited for factor analysis. Furthermore, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant, χ²(210) = 7,788.85, p < .001, indicating that the correlations among items were sufficiently large and that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor extraction. However, the results of the EFA indicated that the items of the IAS-21 loaded onto four factors, which is inconsistent with the original factor structure of the IAS scale. Although all items belonging to the task attraction subscale (Factor 3) corresponded to the original structure, two items from the social attraction subscale (IAS3 and IAS5) loaded on Factor 4. Similarly, three items from the physical attraction subscale (IAS11, IAS12, and IAS14) also loaded on Factor 4. The fourth factor was composed entirely of reverse-worded items reflecting both social detachment (e.g., difficulty meeting and talking with the partner, lack of social fit) and negative physical evaluations (e.g., perceiving the partner as unattractive, ugly, or repulsive). Conceptually, this factor represents a disattraction or aversion dimension, capturing the opposite pole of interpersonal attraction rather than a distinct positive construct. Consistent with prior psychometric findings that reversed items may cluster together due to their wording direction rather than substantive content [74, 75], this factor was interpreted as a method-related “disattraction” factor.

Given the emergence of a fourth factor, we selected three items from each subscale that showed the highest loadings on their respective factors, thereby removing the items associated with the fourth factor. Although the task attraction subscale was consistent with the original structure, we retained only three items from this subscale as well, in order to ensure an equal number of items across all subscales. This procedure resulted in a shortened version of the IAS scale comprising nine items, referred to as the IAS-9. The factor structure of the IAS-9 was subsequently examined using confirmatory factor analysis.

Confirmatory factor analysis

To examine whether the original three-dimensional structure of the IAS [1], comprising social, physical, and task attraction, would be replicated in our data, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis on the IAS-9. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), with values above 0.90 generally indicating good fit [76]. Additionally, we reported the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), with recommended cut-off values below 0.08 [77].

The resulting fit indices demonstrated an adequate overall fit to the data (CFI = 0.982, TLI = 0.973, RMSEA = 0.069, 90% CI [0.054, 0.085], SRMR = 0.039), thereby supporting the three-factor structure of the IAS-9. Reliability analysis

Considering that the IAS-21 consists of ordinal items due to its Likert-type format, in addition to reporting Cronbach’s alpha, as commonly used measure of scale reliability [78], we also calculated McDonald’s omega [79]. Some authors regard omega as a superior reliability estimate for scales with ordinal data [80].

Both alpha and omega coefficients for the IAS-21 were 0.95, indicating excellent internal consistency. Reliability estimates for the subscales were as follows: social attraction (α = 0.88, ω = 0.87), physical attraction (α = 0.90, ω = 0.89), and task attraction (α = 0.87, ω = 0.87), all demonstrating good reliability. Based on the results of the factor analysis, we also calculated internal consistency for the IAS-9. Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale was 0.89 and McDonald’s omega was 0.87, indicating good reliability. Regarding the subscales, social attraction showed coefficients of α = 0.88 and ω = 0.88, physical attraction demonstrated higher reliability (α = 0.94, ω = 0.95), while task attraction showed the lowest yet still acceptable values (α = 0.77, ω = 0.77). These findings support the IAS-9 as a reliable measure of interpersonal attraction.

To further assess internal consistency, we examined item-rest correlations for both versions of the IAS scales, which represent the correlation of each item with the total scale score excluding that item. As shown in Tables 5 and 6, all items exhibited strong positive correlations with the total score, thereby contributing to the overall reliability of the scale.

Validity analysis

The construct validity of the Slovak versions of the Interpersonal Attraction Scale (IAS-21) was examined using Pearson’s correlations among total scores, subscales, and the Couples Satisfaction Index (CSI-16) [67] a unidimensional measure of relationship satisfaction. As shown in Table 7, the IAS-21 total score demonstrated strong positive correlations with its subscales (r = .88–0.90), indicating high internal coherence. The IAS-21 also correlated strongly with the CSI-16 (r = .80, p < .001), supporting its convergent validity. Similarly, the IAS-9 total score showed a very strong correlation with the IAS-21 (r = .95) and a robust association with the CSI-16 (r = .77, p < .001). Correlations between the IAS-9 subscales and relationship satisfaction were substantial (r = .57–0.73, all p < .001), with social attraction again showing the strongest link. These consistent and high correlations across both versions indicate that interpersonal attraction, as measured by the IAS-21 and IAS-9, is closely related to overall couple satisfaction, providing strong evidence for the scales’ construct validity.

Mokken analysis

To evaluate local independence, homogeneity, and the unidimensionality of each IAS-21 subscale, as well as the overall scale, we conducted Mokken scale analysis. Considering the satisfactory result of the CFA for the IAS-9, we also present Mokken scaling for this version of the scale. This method assesses whether items form a hierarchical and scalable structure, thereby supporting the use of summed scores [81]. This step was essential because, if the subscales did not meet Mokken’s criteria, the raw scores for each subscale would not be psychometrically justified.

All item scalability coefficients (H), along with their standard errors (SE), exceeded the recommended minimum threshold of 0.30 [82]. The overall scalability coefficient for the IAS-21 was H = 0.481 (SE = 0.015). Subscale coefficients were as follows: social attraction (H = 0.591, SE = 0.021), physical attraction (H = 0.606, SE = 0.021), and task attraction (H = 0.519, SE = 0.018). For the IAS-9, the overall scalability coefficient was H = 0.502 (SE = 0.018). At the subscale level, the coefficients were H = 0.717 (SE = 0.025) for social attraction, H = 0.876 (SE = 0.014) for physical attraction, and H = 0.544 (SE = 0.026) for task attraction. The scalability results of all items are listed in Tables 8 and 9.

Discussion

This work responds to the lack of validated measures of interpersonal attraction in the Slovak context, thereby addressing an important methodological gap in the field. The primary aim of this study was to translate and adapt the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] into Slovak and to evaluate its psychometric properties within a representative sample of Slovak couples. To address practical considerations regarding scale length while retaining sufficient construct coverage, we employed a shortened 21-item version, comprising seven items for each of the three dimensions of interpersonal attraction: social, physical, and task attraction. In doing so, this study also sought to examine the factor structure of the IAS-21, assess its reliability and validity, explore its scalability through Mokken scale analysis, and provide normative data for the Slovak population.

As noted above, the original Interpersonal Attraction Scale comprises three subscales: social, physical, and task attraction [1]. This three-factor structure has been examined and confirmed in several previous studies e.g. [36, 51]. To evaluate whether this structure would hold for the Slovak IAS-21, we conducted both EFA and CFA on randomized subsamples.

Considering the exploratory factor analysis, the three-factor structure of the IAS was originally examined using varimax rotation [1], whereas a later study employed oblique rotation, which also confirmed the three-factor solution [31]. In our study, we performed an EFA with oblique promax rotation, which yielded a four-factor structure for the IAS-21. This solution explained 61.30% of the total variance and was supported by excellent sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.935) and a significant Bartlett’s test, indicating suitability for factor analysis.

The emergence of a fourth factor appears to reflect a methodological rather than a substantive phenomenon. All items loading on this factor were reverse-worded statements expressing social distance or physical aversion toward the partner. This cluster likely represents a “disattraction” or “aversion” dimension, capturing the opposite pole of interpersonal attraction. Similar findings have been reported in other psychometric studies, where negatively phrased items tend to form separate factors due to shared method variance, cognitive processing differences, or response bias rather than true construct distinctions [74, 75, 84]. Furthermore, given that the IAS was originally developed to measure attraction in general interpersonal contexts rather than specifically within romantic relationships, participants may have interpreted negatively worded items about a partner’s appearance or sociability with greater emotional salience, amplifying their intercorrelations. In romantic contexts, evaluations of a partner’s attractiveness or social compatibility are strongly tied to affective and relational processes such as attachment security, self–other representation, and relational satisfaction [85]. Negatively framed statements about one’s partner may thus activate defensive or self-protective emotional responses, eliciting stronger, more uniform reactions than neutral or positively framed items [86]. This phenomenon may lead to artificial clustering of such items, as participants respond to the negative emotional tone rather than the construct being measured. Additionally, affective priming and valence effects are known to influence how individuals process negative wording, particularly when questions target emotionally significant relationships [87]. Consequently, the emergence of a “disattraction” factor in this study may partly reflect heightened emotional sensitivity to negative partner evaluations, which could magnify shared variance among reverse-coded items and obscure the theoretically expected factor structure.

Although the EFA did not confirm the original three-factor solution of the IAS-21, the CFA findings for the shortened IAS-9 provided support for this theoretically expected structure. This result suggests that the discrepancies observed in the EFA were likely due to the presence of several reverse-coded items. Moreover, it is plausible that this outcome was also influenced by the fact that the items forming the fourth factor were strongly negatively worded in the context of romantic relationships. Since the IAS was originally designed to assess general interpersonal attraction rather than attraction specifically within romantic partnerships, these negatively worded items might have been interpreted differently by participants, which could have contributed to the emergence of the additional factor. By selecting items with the strongest loadings on their respective dimensions and ensuring balanced representation across subscales, the IAS-9 appears to capture the core content of the original construct more clearly and consistently. The satisfactory fit indices obtained in the CFA indicate that the three-factor model provides an adequate representation of the data, supporting the conceptual distinction among social, physical, and task attraction. These results demonstrate that the shortened IAS-9 retains the theoretical structure and psychometric robustness of the original scale [1]. Overall, the findings indicate that the IAS-9 provides a reliable and structurally valid measure of interpersonal attraction within the Slovak cultural context.

The Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] has consistently demonstrated good reliability across previous studies, as noted above. In the present research, we additionally computed McDonald’s omega, yielding an internal consistency of 0.95 for both Cronbach’s alpha and omega for the IAS-21, indicating excellent reliability of the instrument. Examination of the subscales showed similarly strong reliability, with values ranging from 0.87 to 0.90, confirming that the social, physical, and task attraction dimensions are each measured consistently. Notably, these subscale reliabilities are slightly higher than those reported for the IAS-15 [1], as well as higher than values obtained in studies adapting the IAS into Hindi [59] and in the Hong Kong sample [60], and they closely align with values summarized in the review by [31]. We assume that the large sample size in our study contributed to these high reliability estimates, and it is possible that reliability could decrease with smaller samples. Overall, the findings demonstrate that the Slovak 21-item version of the IAS exhibits very good internal consistency, which is further supported by the item-rest correlations presented for the scale. Furthermore, the shortened IAS-9 also showed satisfactory internal consistency (α = 0.89, ω = 0.87), indicating that the reduced version maintains reliable measurement properties comparable to the full scale.

With regard to the validity of the IAS, previous studies have typically examined correlations with various instruments related to interpersonal communication, most often relying on the social and task attraction subscales [39, 43]. Convergent validity has also been demonstrated in other research contexts, generally confirming satisfactory validity [49, 50]. However, validity has been assessed less frequently in relation to cross-sex dyads or romantic partners. Given that our sample consisted of Slovak couples, the present study contributes to filling this research gap by extending the evidence on the scale’s validity within romantic relationships. Construct validity of the IAS-21 was supported through strong positive associations with the Slovak version of Couples Satisfaction Index [67]. Consistent with our expectations, higher levels of interpersonal attraction were linked to greater relationship satisfaction, with social attraction emerging as the strongest predictor. Importantly, each of the three subscales also showed substantial and statistically significant associations with relationship satisfaction. Similarly, the shortened IAS-9 version demonstrated strong construct validity, showing robust correlations with both the IAS-21 and the CSI-16, further confirming its suitability for assessing interpersonal attraction in romantic contexts. These findings extend previous research, which has primarily examined validity in broader interpersonal contexts, by demonstrating that both the IAS-21 and IAS-9 are meaningful and psychometrically sound instruments for studying romantic relationships, with the IAS-9 particularly suited for assessing attraction within romantic partnerships.

As noted earlier, the IAS is frequently used in research not only in its full form but also in varying combinations of subscales, and in some cases individual subscales are applied independently. To examine whether the Slovak version of the IAS-21 and IAS-9 allows for the valid and reliable use of its subscales in isolation, we conducted a Mokken scale analysis. This nonparametric IRT approach enabled us to evaluate local independence, homogeneity, and the unidimensionality of both the overall scale and its three subscales, thereby testing whether summed scores are psychometrically justified. The results indicated that all item scalability coefficients exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.30, supporting the assumption of unidimensionality within each subscale. These findings suggest that the Slovak IAS-21 and IAS-9 not only function well as a whole scale but also permit the use of its subscales independently. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to apply Mokken scaling to the IAS, which we consider a methodological strength that adds further robustness to our validation process and provides useful evidence for future Slovak research utilizing this measure.

In this study, we also established normative data for the IAS-21 within the Slovak population. The original validation study did not provide any norms, and to our knowledge, no prior research on the IAS has reported population-specific reference values. This represents an important contribution of the current study, as providing local norms allows for more accurate and meaningful interpretation of scores in both research and applied settings. Norms are particularly valuable in cross-cultural contexts, as they account for potential differences in language, culture, and demographics that may influence responses. By offering percentile scores based on a representative sample of Slovak couples, our study provides a robust framework for evaluating interpersonal attraction in the Slovak context and lays the groundwork for future research using the IAS in Slovakia.

The present study successfully translated and adapted the Interpersonal Attraction Scale [1] and tested the 21-item version (IAS-21) for use in the Slovak context, providing a comprehensive evaluation of its psychometric properties, factor structure, scalability, and normative data. The factor structure of the IAS-21 revealed four factors; however, shortening the 21-item version to the IAS-9 resulted in a satisfactory model consistent with the original three-factor structure of the scale. The Slovak IAS-21 demonstrated excellent internal consistency, both overall and across its subscales (social, physical, and task attraction), while the IAS-9 also showed good reliability. Construct validity was further supported through strong positive correlations with the Couples Satisfaction Index. Mokken scale analysis confirmed the scalability of both the overall scale and its subscales, supporting their independent use in research for both the IAS-21 and IAS-9. By establishing population-specific norms, this study provides a robust reference framework for interpreting IAS-21 scores in Slovak couples and addresses a notable gap in cross-cultural research on interpersonal attraction.

Limitation and future research

Despite the strengths of the present study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we employed a shortened 21-item version of the IAS rather than the original 30-item scale, which may slightly affect comparability with previous research and other adaptations. Second, the adaptation was conducted specifically for the Slovak context, and the findings may not be fully generalizable to other cultural or linguistic settings. Third, our sample consisted exclusively of couples, which limits the generalizability of the results to other relationship types, such as single individuals, same-sex couples, or non-romantic social dyads. Finally, the confirmatory factor analysis for the IAS-21 did not yield an optimal model fit, suggesting that the scale’s factor structure may vary depending on cultural or methodological factors. Moreover, divergent validity was not examined in the present study, which represents an important limitation in terms of comprehensive assessment of construct validity [88].

We recommend that future research examine the IAS in additional non-English-speaking contexts to further validate its cross-cultural applicability. Given the presented findings, we recommend the use of the IAS-9 in future research, particularly when studying couples or measuring attraction in romantic contexts. The shorter version is also advantageous in studies employing multiple questionnaires within a single quantitative battery, as it reduces participant burden while maintaining reliable measurement properties. The results of the Mokken analysis indicate that the subscales can be used independently, providing flexibility for researchers in various study designs. Future validation studies should include the assessment of divergent validity to strengthen evidence for the construct validity of the scale. Future research could further examine the IAS-21 in more diverse populations beyond couples, which may help to confirm the original three-factor structure of the scale.

Conclusion

Overall, the present study successfully translated and adapted the IAS for the Slovak context, demonstrating that both the IAS-21 and IAS-9 are reliable, valid, and versatile tools for assessing interpersonal attraction. The Slovak IAS-9 represents a concise, psychometrically sound measure suitable for research on romantic relationships, while the full IAS-21 provides a more comprehensive assessment of interpersonal attraction.
