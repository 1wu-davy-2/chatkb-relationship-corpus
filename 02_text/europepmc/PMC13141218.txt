ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPartner involvement plays a critical role in shaping women’s contraceptive choices, yet evidence on its influence remains limited in Nigeria, where contraceptive prevalence is low. This study explored how partner support and communication affect women’s family planning decisions.MethodsA descriptive phenomenological qualitative study was conducted over 6 weeks at the family planning clinic of a tertiary healthcare facility in southwest Nigeria, using in-depth interviews, with data analysed through inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework. Using purposive sampling, 20 women aged 21–42 years were recruited for in-depth interviews. Trustworthiness was ensured through member checking, independent coding and peer debriefing.ResultsParticipants were mostly married (90%) and had secondary or tertiary education; they showed varied awareness of contraceptive methods, with knowledge gaps mainly around side effects. Partner influence ranged from encouragement and financial or emotional support to neutral acceptance, shaping women’s autonomy and method choice. Frequent and open spousal communication facilitated shared decision-making, while cultural expectations for large families and health concerns acted as barriers. Healthcare providers were key sources of information; however, participants emphasised the need for greater male engagement in family planning education.ConclusionThese findings suggest that male partners play an important role in shaping contraceptive decision-making through attitudes, support and communication. Strengthening male engagement and couple-centred approaches may improve family planning uptake in similar settings.Keywords: Public Health, Communication, Sexual Health

BODY
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Contraceptive prevalence remains low in Nigeria despite widespread awareness of modern methods.

Male partners often influence women’s reproductive health decisions, yet their engagement in family planning remains inconsistent. Cultural expectations, misconceptions and gendered power dynamics frequently hinder open discussions on contraception between couples.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Women’s contraceptive choices are strongly shaped by their partners’ attitudes, practical support and quality of communication.

Supportive partners contribute not only approval but also financial and emotional support that facilitates method uptake and continuation.

Cultural expectations and fear of side effects remain key barriers, but these can be mitigated through shared decision-making and provider engagement.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Family planning programmes should integrate male-focused education and routine couple-centred counselling to strengthen shared decision-making.

Healthcare providers can enhance uptake by adopting culturally sensitive approaches that actively engage men without undermining women’s autonomy.

Policies aligned with FP2030 commitments that position men as partners in reproductive health may reduce unmet need and improve maternal and child health outcomes in Nigeria.

Introduction

Family planning refers to the deliberate determination of the number and spacing of children through contraceptive use.1 Contraceptives are broadly classified into reversible and irreversible methods, with reversible options varying by duration and hormonal composition.2 3 Beyond pregnancy prevention, contraception improves women’s health, enhances quality of life and contributes to social and economic development.4

Globally, about 874 million of the 1.9 billion women of reproductive age use modern contraceptive methods, while 92 million rely on traditional methods.5 Modern methods include oral pills, injectables, implants, intrauterine devices and condoms, while traditional methods include practices such as withdrawal and periodic abstinence.1 Despite this, knowledge gaps persist, and contraceptive use remains low in sub-Saharan Africa, and particularly in Nigeria where prevalence is about 17% among married women, barring widespread awareness.6,11

While demographic and structural determinants of contraceptive use, including women’s education, household wealth, geographic region, access to services and exposure to family planning information, are well documented in Nigeria and other sub-Saharan African contexts,8,10 interpersonal dynamics, especially partner involvement, are less consistently explored. Globally, men often perceive contraception as primarily a woman’s responsibility,12 yet their influence on women’s reproductive choices remains substantial, particularly in patriarchal contexts.13,16 Male engagement in family planning has been associated with reductions in maternal and child mortality, improvements in women’s autonomy and broader socioeconomic benefits. It also contributes to population regulation with implications for climate change mitigation.17

In Nigeria, evidence suggests that partner support and spousal communication strongly shape women’s contraceptive decision-making, yet fewer studies have adopted a phenomenological approach to explore how women themselves experience, interpret and negotiate partner support and communication within clinical family planning settings. By foregrounding women’s lived experiences in a tertiary-care context, this study provides nuanced insight into the interpersonal, cultural and institutional dynamics shaping contraceptive decision-making, with implications for gender-transformative family planning policy.

Methods

Study design

Our study adopted a descriptive phenomenological approach grounded in Husserlian phenomenology, which seeks to describe and distill the essence of participants’ lived experiences while minimising interpretive assumptions. Analysis focused on participants’ explicit accounts of partner support, communication and decision-making, allowing shared experiential patterns to emerge inductively rather than imposing pre-existing theoretical interpretations. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist to ensure transparency and rigour. It was conducted over 6 weeks, from November to December 2024, at the Federal Medical Centre, Abeokuta, Ogun State, Southwest Nigeria.

Study setting

The research was conducted at the Family Planning Clinic of the Federal Medical Centre (FMC), Idi-Aba, Abeokuta, a tertiary hospital established in 1993. FMC Abeokuta has a capacity of over 1000 beds and offers multidisciplinary medical and surgical services to residents of Ogun State and its neighbouring states. The Family Planning Clinic serves women from Abeokuta and surrounding local government areas, with several hundred clients accessing services annually. Services are provided by trained nurses, midwives and medical doctors (especially Gynaecologists and Public Health Physicians) offering counselling, method provision and follow-up care. This tertiary-care setting provides an appropriate context for examining partner communication and support at a point of active contraceptive decision-making.

Participants

The study population consisted of women of reproductive age (15–49 years) attending the family planning clinic for contraceptive services during the study period. Eligible participants were those who consented to participate and could read and write in English or Yoruba; they were approached consecutively after clinic visits, screened for eligibility before invitation to participate and provided with study information in a private space. Three women declined participation due to time constraints. Interviewers were not involved in participants’ clinical care and all interviews were conducted by trained female research assistants to minimise power imbalance and encourage open discussion on sensitive reproductive issues. Exclusion criteria included women who declined consent, were critically ill or were unable to read or write.

Sampling and sample size

A purposive sampling technique was employed to ensure diversity in participant characteristics, including age, educational attainment and reproductive history. This maximum variation approach enhanced representativeness and ensured the inclusion of women from diverse backgrounds and experiences.

A total of 20 women were recruited and interviewed. Saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection and analysis, with recruitment ceasing when no new codes or thematic insights emerged across successive interviews.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised public health physicians and social scientists with prior experience in reproductive health studies. The researchers’ professional backgrounds in public health may have shaped participant responses and interpretation of findings. To mitigate this, interviewers emphasised neutrality, used open-ended prompts and reflected on positionality during team debriefings to minimise interpretive bias.

Data collection

Data were gathered through in-depth interviews using a semistructured interview guide developed after reviewing relevant literature. The semistructured interview guide is provided as online supplemental file 1. The guide was prepared in both English and Yoruba, translated and back-translated to ensure accuracy and cultural appropriateness. Interviews were conducted in private consultation rooms within the facility at times convenient to participants, minimising disruption to clinical services. Each interview lasted between 45 and 60 min and was audio-recorded with participant consent. Probing was used to elicit detailed narratives on partner communication, support and family planning decisions.

Interviews were conducted by trained qualitative researchers who were not part of the participants’ clinical care, minimising social desirability bias and enhancing the trustworthiness of the responses. Training was delivered over 2 days, with a duration of 4 hours per day, covering areas such as the study protocol, ethical considerations, interviewing skills and communication strategies to ensure methodological rigour and participant comfort.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke, was used to analyse the data. Both inductive and deductive coding were applied, and multiple researchers cross-checked coding decisions to enhance dependability. The analysis consisted of six stages: familiarisation with the data, generation of initial codes, development of themes, review of themes, defining and naming themes and report production. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, checked for accuracy against the original recordings and supplemented with relevant field notes. Manual coding was carried out, and NVivo software was used to facilitate the organisation of codes and the identification of emerging patterns. Initial coding was conducted inductively to capture participants’ meanings, followed by deductive organisation guided by the socioecological framework, enabling grouping of themes at interpersonal, cultural and health-system levels. Field notes were integrated into analysis to enhance contextual interpretation. An independent coder was engaged to enhance reliability, and peer debriefing with qualitative researchers was undertaken to strengthen analytical rigour.

Trustworthiness and rigour

Credibility was ensured through prolonged engagement with participants, verbatim transcription and member checking of selected responses, which was conducted by summarising key points at the end of interviews and confirming accuracy with participants rather than re-contacting participants post-interview. Dependability and confirmability were achieved through detailed documentation of procedures and independent verification of codes and themes. Transferability was enhanced by purposive inclusion of women with diverse sociodemographic characteristics, ensuring representation of varied experiences across the study population.

Ethical considerations

Participants received complete information on the study objectives and procedures, and both verbal and written informed consent were obtained. Anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained by removing personal identifiers during data analysis and reporting. Given the sensitivity of spousal relations, interviews were conducted privately, participation was voluntary, and interviewers were trained to recognise discomfort and pause or terminate discussions to protect participant well-being. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without any consequences to their care.

Results

A total of 20 young women attending a family planning clinic participated in the qualitative component of this study, providing rich insights into how partner support and communication influence method preferences and use. Participants were all women, aged 21–42 years (mean 33.65±3.7 years). As summarised in table 1, 8 (40%) were aged 25–30, 10 (50%) were above 35 and the remainder fell within the younger and mid-30s brackets. Most participants were married (18; 90%), and educational attainment was evenly split between secondary (50%) and tertiary (50%) qualifications. Parity varied (nulliparous to ≥4), offering perspectives across different reproductive life stages. This diversity in age, marital status, education and parity provided a nuanced context for understanding decision-making around family planning.

Demographics of participants (n=20)

The thematic analysis revealed five overarching themes aligned with the interview guide and research objectives, as summarised in figure 1. For Question 1: ‘What do women know about available methods, and how do they form preferences?’, Theme 1 addresses awareness, knowledge and method preferences. For Question 2: ‘In what ways do partners influence women’s family planning choices?’, Theme 2 examines partner attitudes and forms of support. For Question 3: ‘How does partner communication affect method selection and continuation?’, Theme 3 explores communication dynamics between partners. For Question 4: ‘What barriers and facilitators shape partner involvement in family planning?’, Theme 4 covers barriers and facilitators of partner involvement. Finally, for Question 5: ‘How do healthcare providers and services support informed choice and continuity?’, Theme 5 focuses on healthcare provider roles and service improvement needs.

Thematic map of study findings.

Theme 1: awareness, knowledge and method preferences

Awareness of family planning methods varied among participants, highlighting exposure to modern and traditional contraceptive options. While most participants demonstrated familiarity with modern methods, some knowledge gaps were evident.

Subtheme 1.1: awareness and knowledge of contraceptive methods

Many participants identified methods such as intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs), implants, injections and condoms. P4/42/Tertiary/ stated, ‘I’m aware of all the family planning methods, including the IUD, implant, condom, etc.’ Similarly, P026/Tertiary added, ‘I’m aware of the injection, the pill, and the IUCD.’ These responses highlight a broad understanding of available methods, reflecting the impact of education and exposure to healthcare services. A few participants mentioned traditional methods, but often lacked specifics. For instance, P3/29/Secondary shared, ‘I know about implants and condoms.’ This suggests a narrower awareness spectrum, possibly due to limited community-based education or engagement in healthcare. Overall, the range of awareness expressed by participants reflects both the progress of health education in some communities and the persisting gaps that hinder fully informed contraceptive decision-making.

Subtheme 1.2: method choice influencers

Participants’ preferences for specific methods were shaped by health considerations, lifestyle and perceived ease of use. Many participants emphasised health as a primary factor in their method choice. P3/40/Secondary noted, ‘I’m using the IUCD because it works well for me and suits my body.’ Similarly, P4/42/Tertiary chose the implant over the IUCD due to fears of heavy bleeding, stating, ‘I was afraid of the IUD due to the heavy bleeding and other side effects I heard about.’ For some participants, convenience influenced their preferences. P3/40/Secondary explained, ‘The implant suits my nature and is easier to manage.’ P026/Tertiary echoed this sentiment, ‘The IUCD is okay for me as I don’t have to remember to take the pill daily.’ These accounts illustrate that method choice is rarely arbitrary; instead, it is actively negotiated between perceived health risks, convenience and personal suitability.

Theme 2: partner attitudes and forms of support

Partners played a central role in women’s family planning decisions, ranging from active encouragement to emotional and financial support.

Subtheme 2.1: partner attitudes towards family planning

The attitudes of partners ranged from supportive to neutral, with most participants reporting encouragement from their spouses. Several participants highlighted their partners’ encouragement. P4/42/Tertiary said, ‘He is supportive of it. He even encouraged me to go and get it done.’ P2/40/Secondary added, ‘He is in support and even encouraged me to do it.’ These responses demonstrate how positive attitudes can empower women in their family planning decisions. Others reported neutral attitudes, where partners deferred decisions to them. P1/21/Secondary shared, ‘He said if I'm okay with it, then it’s okay by him.’ This highlights an absence of active engagement but no opposition. Taken together, these findings suggest that even when men are not directly engaged, their attitudes can either validate or undermine women’s autonomy in contraceptive choices.

Subtheme 2.2: practical support from partners

Beyond attitudes, tangible support from partners had a significant influence on method preferences. Several participants noted that their partners provided financial assistance. P1/25/Secondary explained, ‘He gave me money to do it.’ Similarly, P3/40/Secondary stated, ‘He gave me money for this family planning.’ Some participants described their partners’ active involvement in making decisions about family planning. P2/40/Secondary, remarked, ‘He made the payment for the procedure and helped me check the string of the IUD.’ These examples demonstrate that practical forms of support can transform intention into action, reducing financial and procedural barriers to contraceptive uptake.

Subtheme 2.3: partner’s role beyond support

Participants highlighted the importance of emotional and practical support from their partners. P0/26/Tertiary stated, ‘It’s very important for partners to support each other. It keeps you safe and happy.’ P4/42/Tertiary added, ‘It’s important for both partners to be involved and agree on family planning decisions.’ This underscores that partner involvement goes beyond logistics, shared decision-making fosters mutual trust and strengthens relationship satisfaction.

Theme 3: communication dynamics between partners

Communication emerged as a key mechanism through which family planning decisions were negotiated.

Subtheme 3.1: frequency and quality of communication

Communication patterns varied, ranging from regular discussions to occasional exchanges. Some participants frequently discussed family planning with their partners. P4/42/Tertiary said, ‘We discuss it often.’ Similarly, P4/42/Tertiary mentioned, ‘Always.’ Others had less frequent conversations. P1/25/Secondary shared, ‘We discuss it occasionally.’ P1/21/Secondary noted, ‘We've discussed it once.’ These variations reflect how the regularity of dialogue influences the extent to which family planning becomes a normalised and shared responsibility within couples.

Subtheme 3.2: discussion topics between partners

The focus of discussions varied, often centred on health concerns and the benefits of family planning. Many participants discussed potential side effects. P3/42/Tertiary shared, ‘We discuss the type of family planning to use, its side effects, and the importance of using it.’ P2/32/Secondary added, ‘Usually centres around what the side effects could be, ensuring that it is not affecting me.’ Family size and child spacing were also prominent topics. P1/25/Secondary explained, ‘We talk about child spacing and the general importance of family planning.’ This suggests that partner conversations are not only practical but also strategic, balancing immediate health concerns with long-term reproductive goals.

Theme 4: barriers and facilitators of partner involvement

Cultural expectations, health experiences and economic realities shaped the degree of partner involvement.

Subtheme 4.1: barriers to communication and support

Cultural expectations and health challenges emerged as key barriers. Participants highlighted the cultural preference for large families as a barrier. P4/42/Tertiary noted, ‘Yes, culturally, there’s a lot of pressure to have many children.’ P3/40/Secondary echoed, ‘Culture plays a role, especially in emphasising the importance of many children in some traditions.’ Some participants faced health complications. P4/42/Tertiary shared, ‘The challenge has been dealing with excessive bleeding, which affects our decisions.’ These insights reveal how sociocultural norms and medical side effects can complicate open communication, limiting women’s freedom to act on their preferences.

Subtheme 4.2: facilitators of positive interactions

Mutual understanding and economic considerations facilitated productive discussions. Many participants attributed positive interactions to shared goals. P3/29/Secondary stated, ‘Understanding each other and mutual support have facilitated our discussions.’ P0/26/Tertiary added, ‘The economy and the decision that three children are enough for us.’ Financial constraints encouraged discussions about limiting family size. P2/26/Tertiary explained, ‘The economy and the decision that three children are enough for us.’ These accounts illustrate how shared aspirations and financial realities can motivate couples to engage constructively, thereby reinforcing cooperation in family planning decisions.

Theme 5: healthcare provider roles and service improvement needs

Healthcare providers influenced both awareness and decision-making, while participants also identified gaps in service delivery.

Subtheme 5.1: provider engagement and education

Healthcare providers played a crucial role in raising awareness and facilitating effective communication. Many participants emphasised the need to engage men. P2/40/Secondary remarked, ‘Health workers need to engage more with men, not just women, to educate them about family planning benefits.’ P3/40/Secondary suggested, ‘They should provide more orientation and public awareness.’ Some participants acknowledged the work being done by healthcare providers. P1/25/Secondary said, ‘They are already doing a lot by creating awareness about family planning.’ This highlights the dual reality that while providers play a crucial role in shaping awareness, more targeted, male-focused strategies are still necessary to enhance family-level decision-making.

Subtheme 5.2: addressing myths and misconceptions

Providers also played a role in dispelling myths surrounding family planning. P4/42/Tertiary shared, ‘Cultural beliefs often hinder communication; education is key.’ P3/40/Secondary added, ‘Awareness can change the mindset that large families mean more prosperity.’ This emphasises the crucial role of health education in dismantling cultural myths and shifting norms towards more sustainable reproductive choices.

Subtheme 5.3: desired improvements in family planning services

Participants suggested ways to improve family planning services. P4/42/Tertiary shared, ‘Finding a solution to reduce excessive bleeding associated with some family planning methods would be beneficial.’ P2/32/Secondary suggested, ‘Let’s create awareness for most of our married couples these days.’ P2/40/Secondary echoed, ‘More efforts should be made to educate couples, especially men.’ Collectively, these recommendations highlight women’s desire for more responsive services that address side effects, expand education, and actively involve men in the family planning process.

Discussion

This qualitative study examined how partner support and communication influence family planning decisions among Nigerian women. Partner involvement ranged from active encouragement and financial assistance to neutral acceptance, shaping contraceptive uptake and continuity. While supportive attitudes and practical contributions facilitated use, cultural expectations, health concerns and limited male engagement acted as key barriers.

Our findings corroborate evidence from Nigeria and other sub-Saharan African settings showing that spousal approval, financial support and logistical help are positively associated with contraceptive use.18 19 In this study, women whose partners provided money or encouraged method choice were more likely to adopt contraception, consistent with reports from Southeast and North Central Nigeria.18 19 These findings underscore how male partners often hold greater decision-making power in fertility matters, shaping women’s reproductive autonomy.18 20 Similar dynamics have been documented in Ghana, South Africa and Uganda, where supportive partners facilitated method initiation, continuation and clinic attendance.9 21 22 Conversely, survey analyses from Nigeria and elsewhere suggest that disapproval or indifference can deter use, particularly where patriarchal norms reinforce unequal power relations.23 Education, awareness and open communication have been identified as critical to transforming male attitudes and promoting supportive behaviours.9 16 24

Spousal communication also emerged as a pivotal factor. Frequent and open discussions about methods, side effects and family size promoted shared decision-making and sustained contraceptive use. However, applying a gender lens suggests that couple communication is often embedded within broader social norms and power relations that shape whose preferences ultimately prevail.25 This aligns with prior studies in Nigeria, Malawi and some West African countries, where enhanced communication has improved uptake and reduced unmet need.1026,29 However, evidence from Ghana, Chad and Bangladesh suggests that communication alone may not guarantee uptake when structural barriers, such as cost, access and side effects, persist.30 31 Our findings similarly suggest that supportive attitudes and dialogue must be complemented by interventions that address broader sociocultural and health-related obstacles.

Barriers highlighted by participants, including cultural expectations that favour large families, side-effect concerns and community stigma surrounding male participation, are consistent with earlier research.32 33 Fear of partner disapproval or community judgement may deter women from using contraception.23 Myths, mistrust of modern medicine and concerns about reduced sexual pleasure also remain influential.16 17 34 These barriers illustrate how deeply gender norms are entrenched and misconceptions continue to hinder FP uptake in Nigeria and similar contexts.

Healthcare providers were consistently recognised as essential actors in promoting contraceptive awareness and dispelling myths. Participants emphasised the need for greater male-focused strategies, echoing studies advocating for couple-centred counselling and culturally sensitive communication to enhance male involvement.35,38 Evidence suggests that interventions such as male-friendly clinic models, community education and flexible service delivery can reduce misinformation, build trust and normalise shared reproductive responsibility.39 40

Overall, this study highlights the central role of partner involvement through attitudes, communication and practical support in shaping contraceptive behaviour. Interventions that integrate male engagement with broader health system responsiveness are likely to yield more sustainable improvements in FP uptake. By aligning service delivery with Nigeria’s FP2030 commitments and global reproductive health goals, policies that actively involve men as stakeholders can reduce unmet needs, enhance reproductive autonomy and improve maternal and child health outcomes. Operationalising male involvement within Nigerian family planning programmes could include routine couple-centred counselling, community-based male engagement initiatives and collaboration with religious and community leaders in line with FP2030 commitments.

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study provides nuanced insights into how partner support and communication shape women’s contraceptive choices in Nigeria, a setting where male involvement remains underexplored. The use of a phenomenological qualitative design allowed for rich, in-depth narratives that highlight lived experiences across diverse sociodemographic groups. Rigorous methods, including purposive sampling, independent coding and peer debriefing, enhanced trustworthiness and credibility. However, findings are limited by the single-site setting in a tertiary hospital, which may not reflect the perspectives of women in rural or community-based contexts. Additionally, reliance on self-reported accounts may introduce recall or social desirability bias, as participants may have presented their partners in a more positive light, potentially influencing participant responses. Potential loss of nuance during translation from Yoruba to English may have occurred despite back-translation. Recruiting participants from a family planning clinic may introduce selection bias, as participants were already engaged with contraceptive services. Also, participants were clinic attendees and may not reflect experiences of women not accessing family planning services, limiting transferability.

Implications for practice and future research

This study demonstrates that women’s contraceptive decisions are strongly shaped by partners’ attitudes, financial support and the quality of spousal communication. For practice, family planning services should incorporate structured couple-centred counselling that explicitly addresses communication around method choice, side effects and family size. Given that financial support from partners facilitated uptake, programmes may benefit from engaging men in discussions on the economic implications of child spacing and contraceptive use.

Healthcare providers should strengthen targeted male engagement strategies, including inviting partners to counselling sessions and designing clinic-based or community outreach activities that directly address common concerns such as side effects and misconceptions. Addressing culturally driven expectations for large families should be integrated into counselling through context-specific messaging that links reproductive choices to household well-being and economic realities.

Future research should examine partner perspectives alongside women’s experiences to better understand how attitudes, communication patterns and decision-making power interact within couples. Comparative studies across facility-based and community settings are needed to assess whether similar dynamics exist among women not currently accessing family planning services. Additionally, mixed-methods studies could further explore how partner support influences contraceptive continuation over time and evaluate interventions designed to improve male engagement in routine family planning services.

Conclusion

This study provides qualitative insights into how partner attitudes, financial and emotional support and spousal communication shape women’s contraceptive decisions in a tertiary-care setting in Nigeria. Cultural expectations and concerns about side effects emerged as important barriers, while mutual understanding and economic considerations facilitated decision-making. These findings suggest that incorporating male engagement and couple-centred approaches into family planning services may support more informed and shared decision-making in similar contexts.
