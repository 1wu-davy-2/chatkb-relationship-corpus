ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the prevalence of disordered eating and the persistence of traditional gender roles in China, this study examined the relationship between ambivalent sexism (including both benevolent and hostile forms) and disordered eating in Chinese women. Hostile sexism refers to overtly negative and antagonistic attitudes toward women, while benevolent sexism involves subjectively positive but patronizing beliefs. The research examined whether women’s endorsement of these sexist beliefs is associated with self-objectification and increased appearance comparisons on social networking sites (SNSs), which link to disordered eating. Furthermore, the research explored the moderating role of partner intimacy in romantic relationships.MethodsA sample of 761 Chinese college women completed the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory, subscales of the Eating Disorder Inventory, the Self-Objectification Questionnaire, and the Social Network Site Appearance Comparison Scale. Among these participants, 253 women in romantic relationships provided additional self-reports on partner intimacy. Data were analyzed using Hayes’ Process Macro Model 6 to test the serial mediation effects (via self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs) and Model 85 to test the moderated serial mediation model, where partner intimacy was specified as a moderator.ResultsThe results revealed that both benevolent sexism (b = 0.252, 95% CI [0.14, 0.36]) and hostile sexism (b = 0.162, 95% CI [0.05, 0.27]) were positively associated with disordered eating. These associations were mediated by self-objectification and sequentially through self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs. Notably, partner intimacy moderated the path from benevolent sexism to self-objectification (b = 0.095, 95% CI [0.01, 0.18]) and the effects from benevolent sexism to appearance comparison on SNSs (b = − 0.023, 95% CI [-0.04, -0.00]).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that women’s endorsement of ambivalent sexism is associated with disordered eating through self-objectification and appearance-related social comparison, and that romantic relationships are a critical context in which benevolent sexism is linked to women’s psychological processes. By integrating ambivalent sexism with objectification theory, this study advances understanding of how gendered beliefs intersect with relationship contexts in relation to women’s disordered eating in contemporary China.Keywords: Ambivalent sexism, Self-objectification, Disordered eating, Body image comparison, Romantic relationship, Benevolent sexism

BODY
Background

Disordered eating (DE) encompasses maladaptive eating behaviors that fall short of clinical diagnoses, which is prevalent among women, particularly during adolescence and young adulthood. Population-based studies conducted in the USA indicated that disordered eating behaviors, such as dieting and unhealthy weight-control behaviors [1], are reported by approximately 45% of adolescent girls [2]. Female college students are particularly at heightened risk for a range of disordered eating behaviors [3]. A survey of female college students in China found that the incidence of eating disorders is comparable to that in western countries, suggesting that in some groups, eating disorders may be more common than thought [4]. These findings underscore the necessity of examining etiological models of disordered eating within the context of gender and cultural experiences.

In recent years, researchers have sought to expand the scope of studies on women’s internalization of specific societal discourses and disordered eating, marking a new trend in the field. Disordered eating is often shaped by sociocultural norms surrounding appearance and thinness [3, 5]. When these sociocultural ideals are internalized, they become a significant risk factor for the development of disordered eating [6]. Building on this perspective, recent work has highlighted that such internalized ideals are not limited to body image standards but can also encompass gendered belief systems. Among these, subtle and often unrecognized sexist beliefs have also been found to exert an influence on disordered eating [7].

In contemporary Chinese societies, gender-based discrimination and traditional gender role norms often appear in subtle forms. One such form is benevolent sexism, which comprises seemingly positive yet patronizing beliefs about women—for instance, that women are nurturing, morally pure, and in need of protection. While these beliefs may appear flattering, they reinforce traditional gender roles and restrict women’s autonomy [8, 9]. Ambivalent sexism theory distinguishes between hostile sexism (HS) —overtly antagonistic attitudes—and benevolent sexism (BS), which subtly supports male dominance through idealizing women’s dependence on men [10].

Research has found ambivalent sexism is associated with body dissatisfaction, and the endorsement of beauty ideals such as thin bodies [11, 12]. Specifically, women’s dissatisfaction with their bodies is significantly more related to benevolent sexism than to hostile sexism [7]. This dissatisfaction with their bodies can further contribute to psychological issues of women, including eating disorder [11, 13]. Although benevolent sexism is often overlooked as a psychological risk factor because it is less likely to be recognized as prejudicial, emerging research suggests that benevolent sexism contributes to more negative mental health outcomes, including low self-esteem, increased anxiety, and reduced cognitive performance [11, 14]. However, direct research linking internalization of ambivalent sexism to disordered eating is limited and the potential psychological mechanisms are also unknown.

Objectification theory offers a framework for understanding how internalized cultural messages, such as benevolent sexism, contribute to body-related distress. Women’s habitual exposure to objectifying environments may lead them to self-objectify—i.e., to view themselves from an observer’s perspective, focusing on how their body appears rather than how it feels [15]. Self-objectification increases body surveillance and appearance anxiety, which in turn have been linked to disordered eating [16]. A recent meta-analysis confirms that self-objectification is significantly associated with disordered eating [17]. An objectifying self-concept was found to be associated with eating disorder symptomatology and body image dissatisfaction [18]. Objectification theory proposes that the negative consequences of self-objectification may result from women continually comparing their appearance to an unattainable cultural thin ideal and coming up short [15]. Correlational studies have found an association between self-objectification and appearance comparisons [16, 19].

In recent years, women in China experience a complex interplay between traditional cultural values and modern beauty ideals promoted through social media. Social media may intensify the associations between objectification, self-objectification, and appearance concerns by providing a space where idealized images are pervasive and social comparison is encouraged [20]. As social media becomes increasingly influential in shaping perceptions of beauty and identity, addressing its role in psychological distress is more important than ever. Researchers found that visual-focused platforms like Instagram and TikTok amplify exposure to idealized beauty standards, reinforcing appearance comparisons among women [21, 22]. Adolescents who use social networking sites (SNSs) have been found to exhibit higher levels of body image concern and more frequent dieting behaviors than their non-using counterparts [23, 24]. Comparing one’s appearance with others on SNSs has been associated with increased body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [25]. Therefore, self-objectification and appearance comparison on social network sites may work in tandem to influence disordered eating, creating a psychological cascade from internalized sexism to pathology.

Additionally, little is known about how relational contexts shape these processes. Benevolent sexism is especially likely to manifest in romantic contexts, as it idealizes women’s dependence on male partners. Researchers found that men often provide tangible support in intimate relationships, while women tend to be more receptive to such assistance [26]. As intimacy increases, both partners may more readily enact gender role expectations. Men in romantic relationships often display more benevolent sexism, which women frequently interpret as care rather than discrimination [27]. Such perceptions foster internalization: when benevolent sexism is framed as protection, women may accept dependency-oriented support that subtly undermines agency and competence [28]. In contrast, hostile sexism is overt and more likely to provoke resistance than internalization [29]. Therefore, higher partner intimacy may amplify the negative effects of benevolent sexism on self-objectification and disordered eating, but not hostile sexism.

This study examines whether women’s endorsement of ambivalent sexism is associated with disordered eating and whether this relationship is mediated by self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs. Furthermore, we test whether perceived partner intimacy moderates these indirect pathways, particularly in the case of benevolent sexism. This moderated mediation model draws from both ambivalent sexism theory and objectification theory, integrating sociocultural and relational perspectives. By focusing on a sample of Chinese college women, the study adds to underrepresented literature in non-Western populations. This study proposes the following hypotheses: Both women’s benevolent sexism and hostile sexism are positively associated with disordered eating, with the correlation between disordered eating and benevolent sexism expected to be stronger than that with hostile sexism (H1). Women’s benevolent sexism and hostile sexism are expected to be associated with disordered eating through the mediation of self-objectification (H2). Women’s benevolent sexism and hostile sexism are associated with disordered eating through the serial mediation of self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs (H3). The moderating role of partner intimacy in the serial mediation model (via self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs) will differ between benevolent and hostile sexism (H4). As illustrated in Fig. 1, for benevolent sexism, the serial mediation model will be moderated by partner intimacy (H4a). For hostile sexism, the serial mediation model will not be moderated by partner intimacy (H4b).

Methods

Participants and procedure

The participants comprised exclusively female college students at Nanjing University in China. Cluster sampling was employed based on gender and colleges. The questionnaire was distributed using the university’s psychological health management system. A total of 792 questionnaires were received. After data screening, incomplete or invalid responses were removed, resulting in 761 valid questionnaires from female students that were included in the analyses. All participants provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

The demographic variables consisted of age, height, weight, sexual orientation, relationship status, and health status. In this study, Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight in kilograms by the height in metres squared (kg/m2). Relationship status was originally assessed with three categories (“single and never in a relationship,” “single but once in love,” and “in a romantic relationship or married”); as the variable was only used as a control in the main analyses, the two “single” categories were combined into one, resulting in a binary variable (“single” vs. “in a romantic relationship or married”). Health status was measured by a single self-report item: “On a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 is the lowest and 10 is the highest, how would you rate the current level of your physical health?”

All these demographic variables were included as covariates in all relevant mediation and moderation analyses to control for its potential confounding effects. This decision was informed by previous research demonstrating their potential impact on ambivalent sexism or eating disorders, and their significant correlations with the primary variables of interest in our analysis. For example, regarding age, longitudinal studies have indicated that both hostile and benevolent sexism tend to be higher during adolescence and early adulthood, lower in mid-adulthood, and rise again in later life [30, 31]. BMI is closely linked to body dissatisfaction and eating behaviors [32], also making it an important control variable in this study. Furthermore, although ambivalent sexism is present across all sexual orientations, respondents who were in, or desired to be in, heterosexual romantic relationships exhibited stronger benevolent and hostile sexist attitudes compared to sexual minority respondents [33]. Exposure to sociocultural appearance ideals and the resulting self-objectification processes vary among adolescent girls and young women, relationship status is an important contextual factor that may influence these psychological experiences [34]. In addition, self-rated health can impact psychological well-being and body image [35], so it was controlled to rule out its influence on disordered eating and related variables.

Eating disorder inventory (EDI)

Following the methodology of Slater and Tiggemann [36], this study utilized the Bulimia Subscale (EDI-B) and the Drive for Thinness Subscale (EDI-DI) from the Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI) developed by Garner et al. [37] to measure eating disorder pathology. The two parts of EDI consist of 14 items rated on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 6 (Always), with higher scores indicating greater levels of eating disorder symptoms. The Chinese version of the EDI has been validated in both clinical and non-clinical Chinese samples, showing satisfactory internal consistency across all subscales with the Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.70 [38]. These two subscales capture the core behavioral (bulimic symptoms) and attitudinal (drive for thinness) components of disordered eating, and have been widely used as stand-alone or combined indicators in both clinical and non-clinical populations (e.g [39, 40]). In the current study, the Cronbach’s α for the EDI subscales was 0.90.

Ambivalent sexism inventory (ASI)

The Ambivalent Sexism Inventory [8] was used to assess internalized sexist attitudes through 22 items, including hostile sexism (HS) and benevolent sexism (BS). Participants rated each item on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 6 (Strongly Agree), with higher scores indicating greater levels of ambivalent sexism. The HS subscale includes items such as “Women seek to gain power by getting control over men,” while the BS subscale includes items such as “Women should be cherished and protected by men.” The BS subscale further encompasses three dimensions: Protective Paternalism, Complementary Gender Differentiation, and Heterosexual Intimacy. The Chinese version of the ASI has been widely used in Chinese samples, such as Cronbach’s α = 0.76–0.81 for the HS subscale and α = 0.65–0.75 for the BS subscale [41]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the HS subscale was 0.90 and for the BS subscale was 0.70.

Self-objectification questionnaire (SOQ)

Self-objectification was measured using the Self-Objectification Questionnaire (SOQ) developed by Noll and Fredrickson [42]. The SOQ assesses the extent to which participants prioritize observable, appearance-based attributes (e.g. weight, sex appeal) over unobservable, competence-based attributes (e.g. strength, health). Participants rank 10 attributes from 9 to 0 based on their level of importance to themselves. The total self-objectification score is calculated by subtracting the sum of the competence-based attribute scores from the sum of the appearance-based attribute scores, yielding a range from − 25 to 25. Higher scores indicate greater levels of self-objectification. As the questionnaire adopts a ranking format, the external characteristics items and the internal characteristics items were found to be highly negatively correlated (r = −.996), indicating good reliability for the Chinese version [43].

Social network site appearance comparison scale (SNSACS)

The adapted Social Network Site Appearance Comparison Scale (SNSACS) was used to assess appearance comparison on social networking sites [15]. The SNSACS consists of three items (e.g., “When using social networking sites, I compare my appearance with the appearance of others”), rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (Never) to 5 (Always), with higher scores indicating greater levels of appearance comparison on SNSs. The original English version of the scale specifically referred to Facebook, whereas the Chinese version was adapted to encompass all social networking sites without reference to any particular platform. In previous research, the Chinese version of the SNSACS demonstrated good internal consistency in Chinese samples with the Cronbach’s α = 0.89 [44]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.90.

Partner intimacy

Participants currently in a romantic relationship assessed their intimacy with their partner using a single self-assessment item: “On a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 is the lowest and 10 is the highest, how would you rate the current level of intimacy with your partner?” The use of a single-item measure for assessing global relational constructs is supported by previous research showing that single-item indicators of relationship satisfaction demonstrate high correlations with multi-item scales and retain adequate validity and reliability [45]. Furthermore, single-item measures have been increasingly advocated in psychological research for constructs that are global, subjectively evaluated, and easily understood by participants, as they offer efficiency without substantially compromising psychometric quality [46].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS software version 26.0. First, descriptive statistics (including means, standard deviations, and frequencies) were calculated to summarize the sample characteristics and the distribution of key variables. Subsequently, the internal consistency of the scales was assessed through reliability analyses, such as Cronbach’s α coefficient. Pearson correlation analysis was then employed to explore the relationships among BS, HS, self-objectification, appearance comparisons on SNSs, DE, and partner intimacy.

Hayes’ Process Macro Model 6 was used to test the serial mediation model (H3) [47]. The independent variable (X) was benevolent sexism, the dependent variable (Y) was disordered eating, the serial mediators were self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs. The controlling variables were age, BMI, self-rated health, sexual orientation, relationship status and hostile sexism. We also tested the serial mediation model of hostile sexism (X) on disordered eating (Y), after controlling benevolent sexism and other covariates.

Finally, Hayes’ Process Macro Model 85 was used to test moderated serial mediation model (H4) [47]. we employed Hayes’ moderated serial mediation model (Process Macro Model 85) to test H4 []. The independent variable (X) was benevolent sexism, the dependent variable (Y) was disordered eating, the serial mediators were self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs, and the moderator (W) was partner intimacy. The controlling variables were age, BMI, self-rated health, sex orientation and hostile sexism. Similarly, we also tested the moderated serial mediation model of hostile sexism (X) on disordered eating (Y), after controlling benevolent sexism and other covariates. Statistical significance was considered when the 95% confidence interval did not include 0.

Discussion

Although prior research has demonstrated associations between ambivalent sexism and eating disorder symptoms, the underlying mechanisms remain underexplored, particularly regarding benevolent sexism. This study contributes preliminary evidence addressing this gap by testing a moderated mediation model involving self-objectification, appearance comparison on social networking sites (SNSs), and partner intimacy. The sample comprised female college students in China.

Consistent with H1, both benevolent and hostile sexism were positively associated with disordered eating tendencies, with both correlations representing small effects. Furthermore, as hypothesized in H2 and H3, these associations were partially mediated by self-objectification and the serial pathway of self-objectification followed by appearance comparison on SNSs. Importantly, after controlling for hostile sexism and other covariates, benevolent sexism remained associated with disordered eating via these mediators. Partner intimacy was found to moderate the association between benevolent sexism and self-objectification as well as between benevolent sexism and appearance comparison on SNSs; however, it did not moderate the direct association between benevolent sexism and disordered eating. These findings provide support for H4a regarding the moderated mediation for benevolent sexism. Furthermore, as expected in H4b, no moderating effect of partner intimacy was found for any of the pathways in the hostile sexism model. Thus, as hypothesized in H4, the moderating role of partner intimacy would differ between the two forms of sexism.

Our findings suggest that benevolent sexism may exert a unique influence on disordered eating beyond hostile sexism. This aligns with earlier research linking ambivalent sexism to body dissatisfaction [7]. Benevolent sexism idealizes women’s warmth and fragility in seemingly positive but patronizing ways [49]. Such attitudes may foster internalized pressure to manage appearance as a form of seeking social resources and protection. This pressure reflects cultural ideals that associate thinness with vulnerability and dependence, and fullness with fertility — a concept referring to cultural beliefs linking a fuller, curvier body type with reproductive capability and maternal femininity [12, 50, 51]. These ideals may contribute to disordered eating by reinforcing the notion that women’s bodies should conform to narrowly defined aesthetic and functional standards, thereby exacerbating body dissatisfaction and maladaptive eating behaviors [52]. Moreover, the covert and socially sanctioned nature of benevolent sexism [53] may increase its internalization among women, thereby perpetuating self-objectification and appearance comparison processes.

Supporting objectification theory [15], self-objectification was positively associated both with appearance comparisons on SNSs and disordered eating. Women endorsing benevolent sexist beliefs may internalize traditional gender roles and beauty standards [54, 55]. This internalization may lead to habitual body monitoring, increased body shame, and anxiety. These factors have been shown to contribute to eating disorder symptoms [19, 42, 56, 57]. Our data indicate that benevolent sexism influences disordered eating indirectly through self-objectification. Additionally, this influence operates via a serial pathway incorporating appearance comparison on SNSs. This finding is consistent with recent research highlighting the role of social media in body image disturbances [17, 18].

It is also important to note that hostile sexism exhibited both bivariate and indirect associations with disordered eating. Hostile sexism was also associated with disordered eating indirectly through self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs. This aligns with evidence that women endorsing hostile sexist attitudes may experience relational devaluation and objectification, which could increase disordered eating risk [28, 58]. Notably, the direct effect of hostile sexism was nonsignificant, indicating that mediating mechanisms are particularly important in explaining this association. Thus, both forms of sexism—benevolent and hostile—contribute uniquely to disordered eating, albeit through partly overlapping pathways.

Our findings further demonstrate that participants’ self-rated partner intimacy moderates the associations between benevolent sexism and two outcome variables: self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs. Benevolent sexism was positively associated with higher self-objectification only among women reporting high partner intimacy. In contrast, this association was non-significant among those with low partner intimacy. Conversely, benevolent sexism was positively associated with appearance comparison on SNSs in participants with low partner intimacy, but not in those with high intimacy.

This pattern suggests that the subjective experience of closeness and emotional connection in romantic relationships may influence how benevolent sexist attitudes relate to women’s appearance-related cognitions. Benevolent sexism emphasizes traditional gender roles and the valuation of women for their appearance and nurturing qualities [8]. Women perceiving high intimacy with their partners may be more aware of or responsive to sexualized evaluations within the relationship. Such responsiveness could lead to greater self-objectification [59]. According to Hammond et al.‘s interaction model [28], women who endorse benevolent sexism are more likely to form relationships with men who similarly endorse these attitudes. This dynamic may reinforce patterns of sexual objectification within the dyad. However, given that our study did not include data from male partners, these proposed mechanisms remain hypothetical and warrant further investigation.

In contrast, among participants with lower partner intimacy, benevolent sexism was associated with greater appearance comparison on social networking sites. This may reflect a compensatory mechanism. Specifically, insufficient emotional closeness within the romantic relationship may lead women to seek external validation through social comparison [60]. In relationships characterized by high intimacy, women may receive adequate emotional support and affirmation from their partners. As a result, they may rely less on external appearance comparisons.

No significant moderating effects of partner intimacy were found in the relationships involving hostile sexism. This finding aligns with prior research indicating that hostile sexism, characterized by antagonistic attitudes toward women, tends to involve less emotional dependency and manifests differently in intimate contexts compared to benevolent sexism [8].

Strength and limits

This study with large sample size afforded robust tests of a complex moderated mediation model, advancing understanding of how ambivalent sexism influences eating pathology in contemporary China. The integration of self-objectification and appearance comparison on SNSs as mediators extends objectification theory within the sociocultural context of Chinese young women. Highlighting partner intimacy’s moderating role offers novel insights into relational factors shaping disordered eating risk.

Clinically, findings underscore the importance of addressing subtle, internalized sexist beliefs and relational dynamics in prevention and intervention efforts targeting disordered eating. Both benevolent and hostile sexism should be considered in clinical and psychoeducational contexts, as each contributes to disordered eating via self-objectification and social comparison. Practitioners should be attentive to the covert pressures of benevolent sexism and the role of intimate relationships in reinforcing harmful appearance ideals. Finally, public health strategies aimed at reducing ambivalent sexism and promoting healthier relational intimacy may contribute to improved psychological wellbeing and eating disorder outcomes.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of the observed relationships. It is possible that the causal direction could be reversed—for instance, women experiencing disordered eating tendencies may be more inclined to endorse benevolent sexist beliefs. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics among sexism, self-objectification, and disordered eating. Second, reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases, such as social desirability and shared method variance, which could affect the accuracy of the constructs assessed. Third, the use of a non-clinical sample, while appropriate for examining early mechanisms and risk factors, limits the generalizability of findings to clinical populations with diagnosed eating disorders. Future research should replicate these models in clinical samples to enhance ecological and diagnostic validity. Fourth, the sample comprised exclusively Chinese female college students, limiting the generalizability of findings to other cultural contexts, age groups, and populations with different gender identities or sexual orientations. Future research should examine these relationships in more diverse samples. Fifth, while the study focused specifically on appearance comparisons within social networking sites—a highly relevant context for contemporary appearance pressures—this narrow focus may not capture the full range of appearance-related comparisons (e.g., in offline or other media contexts). Thus, the findings should be interpreted within the specific domain of SNSs. Future studies could broaden the scope to include multiple comparison contexts. Sixth, while we controlled for key variables (BMI, self-rated health, relationship status, and sexual orientation), unmeasured confounders may have influenced the results. Notably, sexual orientation was controlled in the analyses based on prior research. However, given the relative homogeneity in demographics (e.g., 68.1% of participants identified as heterosexual in this Chinese female sample) the influence of sexual orientation on perceptions of partner intimacy remains uncertain. Furthermore, the current study did not account for potential dyadic ambivalent sexism dynamics—specifically, how the interaction between a participant’s own sexism and their partner’s sexism might influence outcomes such as self-objectification or disordered eating. To fully elucidate dyadic influences, future studies should incorporate partner perspectives.

Conclusion

This study first examined whether women’s own ambivalent sexism predicts disordered eating. Additionally, this study integrated ambivalent sexism with objectification theory to provide a novel understanding of the mechanisms through which ambivalent sexism leads to psychological health issues such as disordered eating in contemporary China. As hypothesized, both benevolent and hostile sexism were positively associated with disordered eating in females. The impact of ambivalent sexism on disordered eating can be mediated either independently through self-objectification or serially through self-objectification and appearance comparison on social networking sites. A key finding was the differential role of partner intimacy. For benevolent sexism, partner intimacy played a moderating role. The mediation paths were only significant for women in relationships who perceived high levels of intimacy with their partners. Partner intimacy moderated the relationship between benevolent sexism and self-objectification. For women who perceived high partner intimacy, benevolent sexism was positively correlated with self-objectification. Conversely, for women who perceived low partner intimacy, benevolent sexism was positively correlated with appearance comparison on social networking sites. In contrast, the pathways linking hostile sexism to disordered eating were not moderated by partner intimacy, highlighting the difference in how these two forms of sexism operate within romantic relationships. Our findings suggest that women’s own high levels of benevolent sexism may links to their disordered eating, potentially mediated by self-objectification and appearance comparison on social networking sites, particularly within the context of perceived high partner intimacy. Meanwhile, hostile sexism appears to be associated with disordered eating through more direct pathways, irrespective of relational contexts. Intimate relationships thus serve as a crucial context for the influences of benevolent sexism, but not hostile sexism.
