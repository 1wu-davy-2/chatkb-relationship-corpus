ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare professionals have recognized medical communication (MC) skills as essential. Some common and important factors support effective MC. Physiotherapists (PTs) communicate with patients about a wide range of topics—including functioning, contextual factors, and healthy lifestyle behaviors. In movement-based settings, PTs and students may face challenges in communicating effectively with patients. However, it remains unclear how best to teach such communication techniques in physiotherapy training curricula. This study aimed to determine the effect of learning MC skills—based on an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE)—on the acquisition of communication with movement (CWM) skills in physiotherapy.MethodStudents who had learned MC techniques based on the OSCE (n = 15), students who had not learned them (n = 15), and PTs (n = 15) were recruited. MC skills and CWM skills, as defined in this study, were assessed and compared between the groups. Correspondence analysis was performed on the conversational content in movement situations, and the frequently used distinguishing phrases used by each group were compared.ResultThe learned group scored significantly higher than both the unlearned student group and the Physiotherapist (PT) group on the total score for MC skills. There was no significant difference between the learned and unlearned student groups in the total score for CWM skills. The learned group scored significantly lower than the PT group on physical contact, moving around the patient and explanation of test results. Characteristic words identified in the correspondence analysis scatterplots were “date of birth,” “name,” and “answer please” for the learned group, and “dull” for the PT group.ConclusionThe effect of learning MC techniques based on the OSCE on the acquisition of CWM skills appears limited. Through learning MC techniques, participants acquired skills for patient confirmation and polite conversation in movement-based interactions. However, essential skills related to physical contact, moving around the patient, and explaining test results—key components of physiotherapy—were difficult to acquire. This study suggests that students who have learned general MC techniques may not yet acquire CWM skills prior to clinical practice. Future studies should explore effective ways to teach CWM skills to students.Keywords: Medical Communication, Physiotherapy, Physical movement, Education

BODY
Background

Medical communication (MC) skills have been recognized as necessary for healthcare professionals recently [1–3]. For physiotherapists (PTs), communication includes medical interviews [4], pain assessment [4], palpation of body parts [5, 6], advice on exercise [5], and descriptions of exercise methods and treatment plans [6].

Previous studies have identified key MC factors such as verbal and non-verbal communication [7]. Kinesics is one of the components of non-verbal communication. It involves all communicative acts expressed through body movements, facial expressions, and gestures [8]. Studies have shown that PTs, when communicating with patients, provide information and advice on exercises [9], explain treatment plans [10], maintain physical contact related to exercise instruction [5, 6], assess movements [11], and fall risk [11]. These findings suggest that in physiotherapy settings involving physical movement as non-verbal communication. High PT communication skills have been reported to increase patients' self-reported physical activity [12] and satisfaction with exercise instruction [3]. These studies highlight that PTs communicate with patients specifically in the context of exercise and movement. Savvoulidou et al. reported that PTs felt they had not learned sufficient techniques for communicating with patients in therapeutic situations [13]. Additionally, patients believe that PTs’ knowledge of communication techniques affects the effectiveness of their care [13]. Osborn-Jenkins et al. found that students struggled to communicate exercise and lifestyle advice to patients in physiotherapy treatment settings and perceived a lack of practical knowledge. They also reported that students sought learning opportunities to prepare for clinical situations [14]. These findings suggest that both PTs and students may have difficulty communicating with patients.

Ballengee et al. examined the effectiveness of a program on psychosocial communication techniques for physiotherapy students working with patients with chronic low back pain. The program included didactic components (such as the origins and definitions of psychologically informed practice, screening methods, neuroscience, and recommended behaviors) and experiential components (including patient case studies, role-playing, and group discussions). After the intervention, students showed reduced biomedical orientation and reported improved self-confidence and communication skills [4]. Goldsmith et al. assessed the impact of communication technique training in a palliative care setting for graduate physiotherapy students. The program consisted of lectures, videos of patients and caregivers in clinical settings, and brief communication exercises. Although the intervention did not significantly affect students’ comfort, confidence, or fear of death, it did increase their willingness to communicate [15]. While there is evidence of the learning effects of communication techniques on physiotherapy students, the impact of learning techniques related to physical movement in therapeutic settings has not yet been analyzed.

According to the WHO Rehabilitation Competency Framework, communication is one of the core skills required for rehabilitation professions. One of the activity-specific skills is “assessing body functions, activities and participation through observation and interview” [16]. A systematic review examining the impact of the therapeutic relationship on physiotherapy outcomes states that communication skills are among the competencies of PTs that influence patient outcomes [17]. Therefore, effective communication with patients is essential for rehabilitation professionals. They use these skills to assess and treat patients' activities and participation, including physical function and movement. As previously noted, PT communication often involves movement [3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 12], and both PTs and students are seeking opportunities to learn communication techniques in such settings [13, 14]. Muddle et al. conducted a systematic review to identify educational approaches that could improve the communication skills of undergraduate students on physiotherapy and chiropractic programs. This systematic review ultimately included six papers published from the United States, Canada, Australia, Northern Ireland, and the United Kingdom. According to this, learning methods such as instruction, reflection, role play, providing feedback and problem-based learning were effective in improving basic communication skills [18]. However, no standardized program currently exists in PT training curriculum for teaching communication with movement. Furthermore, it remains uncleared how best to teach communication with movement in the curriculum.

Methods

Aims

This study aimed to determine the effect of learning MC skills—based on an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE)—on the acquisition of communication skills in physiotherapy. In this study, "communication with movement (CWM)" is defined as communication that occurs when PTs make contact with patients’ bodies and explain physical movements.

Design

A cross-sectional study was conducted to compare communication skills between students who had learned MC techniques and those who had not, within the curriculum. The cross-sectional design was appropriate, as there were no differences between the groups at the time of the study except for the experience of learning MC techniques. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. The Research Ethics Committee of the Open University of Japan approved this study (Notice No. 2023–62). The participants were given an informational document and consent form, and the researcher explained the study to them. It was considered that participants agreed to participate in the study when their signatures were obtained from the consent forms. Participants were informed that they could refuse or withdraw from the study without consequences even after giving their consent.

Participants

All third-year students (n = 34) in a third-year PT education program were informed of the opportunity to participate in this study. Those who consented (n = 30) were enrolled. These students had completed all coursework except clinical practice and had the necessary knowledge and skills to perform the experimental tasks described below. Of the 30 students, 15 had already learned MC techniques based on the OSCE (learned group), and 15 had not (unlearned group). After participating in the experiment, unlearned group was provided with an opportunity to learn MC techniques based on the OSCE. The inclusion criteria for students were good health and no completion of the third-year internship. All PTs (n = 20) working in a rehabilitation hospital who had been practicing physiotherapy for at least five years after licensure were invited to participate. Of these, 15 PTs (6–20 years after licensure) consented and were enrolled. Inclusion criteria for PTs included good health, at least five years of experience practicing physiotherapy after licensure, and experience treating patients with stroke hemiplegia. The five-year post-licensure criterion was set because it is the requirement for internship supervisors in Japan [19].

Measures

Three measures were used to evaluate the effectiveness of learning MC techniques based on the OSCE. These were: MC skills, CWM skills, and the conversational content of communication in movement situations. The following section details each measure.

MC Skills assessment

The OSCE assessment criteria for communication techniques were used as the measurement index [20] (Table 1). The OSCE is a structured clinical examination in which examiners score candidates according to predefined criteria. Previous studies have used the OSCE to assess the learning effectiveness of MC skills because it provides a standardized assessment method [21–23]. The OSCE consists of 14 items, each scored on a scale of 0 to 2 points, for a total of 28 points. MC skills were assessed by having participants perform specific tasks, which were video recorded. The scorers were not informed about the groups to which the videos belonged. The videos were assigned identification numbers for analysis and management purposes. The recordings were scored by someone other than the researcher, based on the OSCE criteria. To assess inter-rater reliability, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC 2.1) was calculated based on the scoring results of two scorers using a sample of 30 participants. The ICC (2.1) was 0.65 (95% confidence interval[CI], 0.35 to 0.82), confirming that this assessment is reliable. The scoring criteria for each item are listed in Table 1.

The following rationale supports the selection of these eight scoring items. First, physical contact is central to physiotherapy, as PTs routinely touch patients' bodies during examination and treatment [5], and such contact has been shown to facilitate communication [6]. Second, PTs must explain movement methods to patients, including providing information and advice about exercises [9]. Third, they must check patients’ understanding of the movement instructions and inquire about any complaints [5]. Fourth, PTs often demonstrate movements using their own bodies, making this another key component of communication. Fifth, assessing pain is essential, as patients may experience discomfort during physical activity [3, 9]. Sixth, paraphrasing technical terminology is crucial for ensuring that patients without medical backgrounds understand treatment plans. Paraphrasing has been identified as a core communication skill for PTs [13], and OSCE items in PT training also require students to explain tests and treatments in lay terms [10]. Seventh, PTs need to move around patients efficiently, maintaining appropriate proximity—an important aspect of communication technique in clinical contexts. Lastly, explaining test results clearly and transparently is important because patients expect understandable feedback about their condition and progress [3]. Each of these eight items was scored as 1 if performed adequately or 0 if not performed or performed inadequately. As these items represent the skills required of PTs, we decided that they should be assessed using a binary scale to determine whether they had been mastered. The scoring for each participant was determined using the nominal group technique (NGT), which is described later. The use of a binary scale facilitated discussions among the five scorers regarding their final scores. Face validity was confirmed, and the internal consistency of the CWM assessment scale was indicated by Cronbach's alpha coefficient, which was 0.56. The CWM scale was a preliminary operationalization of the construct, not a validated instrument.

Final scores were determined using NGT, a structured consensus method commonly used for decision-making and aggregating group judgments [24]. NGT is highly adaptable and applicable to contexts beyond idea generation [25]. Based on recommendations that NGT groups consist of five to nine members [24], five experienced PTs—each licensed for at least five years—served as scorers in this study.

The scoring process involved the following steps. In Step 1, each scorer independently watched a video of a participant performing the task and assigned scores according to the defined criteria. The scorers were not informed about the groups to which the videos belonged. The videos were assigned identification numbers for analysis and management purposes. The results were then emailed to the researchers. In Step 2, the researcher compiled and tabulated all scorers’ responses and shared the table with them via email. The initial agreement rate was 54.4%. This is the percentage of items where the scores of all five scorers matched. In Step 3, items where scoring disagreements arose were discussed in online meetings, during which scorers explained the reasons for their decisions. In Step 4, each scorer reassessed the disputed items based on the discussion and scored them again. In Step 5, the updated results were compiled and redistributed to all scorers. Finally, in Step 6, following previous studies [24], any item that received a score of 1 from at least three out of five scorers (i.e., 60% or more) was accepted as a final positive evaluation [26].

Conversational content of communication with movement

To analyze conversations during CWM task performance, each conversation between the participant and the standardized patient (SP) during the task was transcribed from the recorded voice. Correspondence analysis was conducted to examine the tendencies in word usage among the participants in each group during task performance to evaluate CWM skills. Therefore, the utterances of SP were excluded from the analysis. In order to protect the personal information of both participants and SP, all personal names were replaced with the placeholder 〈jinmei〉. The correspondence analysis results showed minimal differences between participants. To maintain consistency and minimize irrelevant variation, filler words—such as “eeto,” “ehto,” “ano,” “maa,” “nanka,” and “a”—were replaced with the placeholder 〈filler〉. These fillers appeared to function as verbal pauses, giving participants time to consider appropriate expressions during the conversation. Because they serve the same communicative purpose, they were treated uniformly in the analysis.

Study procedures

The training in MC techniques was based on the OSCE format. Learners took five training sessions. Each training session lasted 20 min, and a total of five sessions were conducted on separate days. The content of the sessions followed the OSCE materials for communication techniques [20].

The learned MC techniques comprised two domains. First, Attitude, which included (1) appropriate demeanor and greeting, (2) confirmation of the patient’s name and date of birth, and (3) obtaining the patient’s consent to speak with them. Second, Skills, which encompassed (1) appropriate positioning and distance from patients, (2) aligning the participant’s gaze with the patient’s eye level and direction, (3) appropriate posture and gestures during conversation, (4) choosing the right topic, (5) appropriate language and speaking speed, (6) clear volume and voice, (7) fluent speech, (8) natural facial expression, (9) active listening, (10) sensitivity to others’ feelings, and (11) responding appropriately to questions about prognosis.

In the first session, learners received printed training materials on MC techniques, and trainer explained the content. Learners were then asked to watch the model video which was five minutes. Training materials included the OSCE assessment criteria for MC techniques, and a QR code was provided to give learners access to a model video. The model video showed a patient with knee osteoarthritis talking to a healthcare provider and demonstrating said MC techniques content. From the second to the fourth session, learners paired up and alternated roles as a patient and a healthcare provider, engaging in role-play exercises under trainer supervision. Trainer instructed learners to perform role-play based on patient scenarios and dialogue content from the training materials. The patient role sat in a wheelchair, while the healthcare provider role practiced conversing with them while crouching beside them without touching the patient role's body. Learners could confirm the training materials and the model video whenever they needed to review the communication techniques they were learning. After each role-play, learners provided feedback to one another, discussing any problems observed and suggesting solutions. In the fifth session, trainer reviewed the role-plays and offered individual feedback to each learner on any identified deficiencies based on the OSCE assessment criteria.

Experiment

To assess MC skills, the participants were asked to perform a task that involved conversing with a trained SP who played the role of a patient with right knee osteoarthritis. The scenario simulated a visit to the rehabilitation room before the start of physiotherapy, during the absence of the PT in charge. A treatment bed, wheelchair, and four-point cane were used.

The task performance was recorded using four video cameras (Apple iPhone 11, Sony HDR-CX170, Apple iPad Air 2, and Canon iVIS HF20). The conversation between the participant and the SP during the task was recorded with sound recorders (Wireless PRO, RØDE). Before the task began, sound recorders were attached to the chests of the participant and the SP. Participants received an instruction sheet describing the MC task, which was read aloud by the researcher to ensure clarity. If participants asked questions about the task, the researcher answered them—unless the question pertained to evaluation criteria. Participants were instructed to begin the task upon hearing a buzzer.

After completing the MC task, participants were instructed to perform the CWM task. This included the left thumb sense of passive movement test [27], the right knee extension manual muscle test [28], and the left lower limb motor paralysis test [29], performed on a SP acting as a left hemiplegic stroke patient. These three tests were selected because their methods of performance are relatively consistent and suitable for evaluating CWM tasks. The SP had normal comprehension and language abilities.

The procedure for the left thumb sense of passive movement test was as follows: the SP was placed in an end-sitting or supine position with their eyes closed. The participant moved the SP’s left thumb into flexion or extension, and the SP was asked to indicate the direction of movement [27].

For the right knee extensor manual muscle test, the SP was placed in an end-sitting position. The SP was instructed to extend the right knee, and the participant confirmed whether the SP could maintain the position. The participant then placed their hand on the anterior aspect of the SP’s right ankle and applied resistance in the direction of knee flexion to test muscular strength [28].

The left lower limb motor paralysis test was conducted as follows: The SP was seated in the end-sitting position. First, the participant asked the SP to lift their left lower limb and assessed whether this could be done independently. Next, the SP was asked to dorsiflex the ankle with the heel on the ground and flex the knee more than 90° with the plantar foot on the ground, and the participant checked for proper performance. Finally, the SP stood with the support of a four-point cane and was instructed to dorsiflex the ankle with the plantar foot on the ground and flex the knee to approximately 90° without flexing the hip, which was again assessed by the participant [29].

After completing the left thumb sense of passive movement test, the right knee extension manual muscle test, and the left lower limb motor paralysis test, the participant verbally explained the test results to the SP.

Analysis

Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Steel post-hoc tests. The Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted to determine whether there were differences in the total scores among the learned, unlearned, and PT groups. Steel tests were used to compare the learned group with the unlearned and PT groups, with the learned group defined as the reference group. A post-hoc power analysis was performed. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Data normality was confirmed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

A chi-squared test was conducted to examine the association between the scores of each CWM skill item and the group. A post-hoc power analysis was also performed. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.66 and R version 4.3.3. Post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7.

Correspondence analysis was conducted to examine the tendencies in word usage among the participants in each group during task performance to evaluate CWM skills. This method was used for text mining, with the position of each word and attribute in the scatterplot determined by calculating the χ2 distance between words from the cross-table of word frequencies for each attribute [30].

A two-dimensional scatterplot was generated from the correspondence analysis to visualize the words used in conversations and the positions of the three groups. To identify characteristic words for each group, words positioned away from the origin in the direction of a particular group were noted. Words that occurred at least 10 times were selected for extraction, ensuring that they were used by more than two-thirds of the 15 participants in each group. The minimum number of documents was also set at 10 for the same reason. A total of 100 words showing the most significant differences were included in the analysis, representing approximately 10% of the 1,145 distinct words extracted.

KH Coder version 3. Beta.08e was used as the text mining software for this analysis.

Discussion

In recent years, communication skills have been identified as a necessary competency for health professionals [1, 31]. Many studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of learning communication skills specific to each profession, including doctors [32–34], pharmacists [23], and nurses [33]. This study investigated the effects of learning MC techniques based on the OSCE on CWM skills in examination performance situations. The learned group had significantly higher total scores for MC skills than the unlearned group. Previous studies have shown that learning communication skills through OSCE-based techniques enhances communication ability, and these included explanations of patient scenarios and role-playing exercises, which were similar to those in the present study [22, 23, 35]. Our study produced similar findings. The acquisition of patient identification and polite speech by the learned group was supported by correspondence analysis of conversational content in the CWM scenarios. The learned group tended to use “date of birth” and “name” to identify patients, reducing the risk of misidentification. Verifying the “date of birth” and “name” with the patient is an effective way of preventing patient mix-ups in clinical settings. OSCE-based MC training emphasizes identifying the patient’s name and date of birth. Additionally, the learned group more frequently used the polite phrase “okotae (answer please),” whereas the unlearned group used the less polite “kotaeru (answer).” These terms were used when participants explained how to respond to the direction of motion in the left big toe before performing the left thumb sense of passive movement test. We conclude that the learned group acquired skills in patient identification and polite speech through the OSCE-based MC training.

However, the learned group scored significantly lower than the PT group in the total CWM skill score. Specifically, the learned group performed worse on “Physical contact” and “Moving around the patient,” both of which assess participants’ physical movements during CWM. In the OSCE-based MC training, participants practiced speaking to SP seated in wheelchairs while crouching diagonally in front of them, without making physical contact. Therefore, during the OSCE-based MC skills training, the learned group did not practice physical contact and movements around patients. These results are similar to those of previous studies examining the effectiveness of simulation training before clinical practice. Rossiter et al. compared two groups: one that underwent simulation-based learning using simulated patients based on patient scenarios, and one that completed an online learning program. This verified the advantages of each approach. The simulation program replicated clinical practice, incorporating communication with simulated patients, practice and feedback on appropriate actions in clinical settings, and peer interaction among students. The online program covered communication skills and content related to acute care, outpatient settings and home care, as well as responses to case study questions. However, it did not include practical sessions, feedback or opportunities for peer interaction. Focus group interviews with participants from both groups revealed that, upon completing the program, the group that underwent simulation training reported improvements in adapting communication and implementing intervention. The group that underwent online training reported improvements in implementing interventions, but not in adapting communication [36]. These findings suggest that a practical approach to education is necessary to improve communication skills in physiotherapy, including physical contact with patients and moving around them. Norris et al. conducted a qualitative study to clarify the process by which physiotherapy students learn professional physical contact prior to clinical practice. The themes derived from their research echoed with our own themes. Uncertainty, self-awareness and anxiety were identified as themes, with students expressing unease about touching others and acknowledging that their lack of skill could run a risk. They noted that physiotherapy students received clinical instruction in physical contact and movement around patients, and emphasized the need for instructional methods to teach these techniques [37]. In this study, the learned group had not been taught techniques involving physical contact during the OSCE-based MC training. This may have caused them to feel anxious and unable to practice appropriate physical contact.

For the CWM assessment item “Explanation of test results,” the learned group also scored significantly lower than the PT group. Scoring criteria required explanations of the degree and gradation of disorders and symptoms in language understandable to patients. This result was also supported by correspondence analysis. The PT group tended to use the word “dull,” while explaining the results of the left thumb sense of passive movement test, whereas they also used the technical term “motor sense hypoesthesia.” Salgaonkar et al. examined anesthesia residents before and after a workshop on informed consent communication skills. Although the workshop included content on clear verbal communication and effective language use, it did not improve the use of nonmedical language [38]. Banerjee et al. studied patient satisfaction before and after an empathic communication skills intervention for oncologists treating lung cancer patients. The training included role-plays with standard patients to practice empathic responses and improve understanding. They reported increased patient satisfaction and better understandable explanations post-intervention [34]. This supports our finding that OSCE-based MC training did not enhance the ability to explain test results in general terms. These findings suggest that the ability to provide clear explanations to patients will not improve without practice-oriented training.

Although PTs use communication through movement, no formal training programs currently exist to develop these skills. Based on our results, we conclude that OSCE-based MC training had a limited effect on the acquisition of defined CWM skills. In addition to MC techniques, the inclusion of CWM skill development should be considered in PT training programs.

Limitations

This study is the first to examine whether learning general MC techniques is effective in acquiring CWM skills among PTs. Despite this strength, the study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small. Participants were recruited from a single training school and medical institution, which may have introduced demographic bias. The gender ratio is imbalanced, with a small proportion of women in each group. This creates a bias in the attributes of the participants. Empathy, which is related to communication, has been shown to be higher in women than in men [39, 40]. The high proportion of males in all groups may have influenced the content of the conversations. Moreover, the results obtained in this study may not be applicable to other situations due to this limitation. Additionally, due to the time required to complete the experimental tasks, only 45 participants were included. Second, we developed a scoring rubric for CWM skills because no standardized scale currently exists for assessing this type of communication. The rubric was defined based on previous studies. To ensure objectivity, five scorers independently recorded scores using NGT. Nonetheless, the lack of a validated, widely accepted assessment tool may limit the generalizability of the results. The future challenge lies in developing and validating a standardized CWM assessment tool.

Conclusion

The effect of learning MC techniques based on the OSCE on the acquisition of CWM skills is limited. By learning MC techniques, participants acquired skills to confirm patients’ identities and use polite expressions in CWM conversations. However, the skills required for physical contact, moving around patients, and explaining test results—essential for PT—might be difficult to acquire. In this Japanese sample, no evidence of improvement in CWM skills was observed among students who received OSCE-based MC training, although the study was underpowered to detect small or moderate effects. Thus, the impact of such training on CWM remains inconclusive. This study defined CWM skills preliminarily and verified the effectiveness of the OSCE-based MC training in acquiring these skills. Future research should establish the concept of CWM skills and confirm their role in physiotherapy education.
