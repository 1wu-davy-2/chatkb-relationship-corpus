ABSTRACT
During social interactions, individuals often share their emotions with friends to enhance mutual intimacy. According to the interpersonal process model of intimacy, emotional disclosure predicts the development of intimacy, and a partner’ s responsive behavior can further facilitate this process. However, how emotional self-disclosure and a partner’ s response jointly influence intimacy and the underlying neural mechanisms in naturalistic conversations remain unclear. This study employed functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) hyperscanning to investigate the brain mechanisms underlying the enhancement of intimacy in female dyads during emotional conversations, which included conditions of positive and negative emotional self-disclosure as well as non-self-disclosure. Behavioral results revealed that dyads in the self-disclosure condition reported higher levels of intimacy, and both state-level cognitive and state-level affective empathy were positively correlated with intimacy. At the neural level, the responder’s left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) showed significant activation differences. Specifically, responder’s brains exhibited greater activation during positive (vs. negative) emotional sharing and during self-disclosure (vs. non-self-disclosure) conditions. Furthermore, in positive emotional contexts, self-disclosure elicited stronger neural activation in responder’s compared to non-self-disclosure; however, no significant difference was observed in negative emotional contexts. Importantly, this neural activation mediated the path through which state-level empathy influenced intimacy. These findings highlight the responder’s neural reactivity as a crucial factor in transforming empathic resonance into intimacy during emotional communication, underscoring the importance of partner feedback in forging deeper emotional bonds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04257-3.

BODY
Introduction

Conversation, as the core medium of human social interaction, plays a pivotal role in emotional communication and social bonding. Among various forms of dialogue, emotional conversation is particularly critical for fostering social connectedness and interpersonal intimacy. In daily life, individuals establish and maintain relationships by sharing personal experiences and emotions, a process commonly referred to as self-disclosure. This process entails not only the exchange of information but also the elicitation of deep emotional resonance [16]. Self-disclosure, defined as the act of revealing personal thoughts, feelings, and experiences to others, lays the foundation for intimacy by promoting mutual understanding and trust [2]. A large body of research demonstrates that self-disclosure significantly enhances the quality of interpersonal relationships. Across romantic partnerships, friendships, and family interactions, higher levels of self-disclosure are positively associated with greater relationship satisfaction and prosocial behavior [38, 64, 65]. Notably, this effect extends beyond the sharing of positive emotions to include negative emotional disclosure. For instance, sharing feelings of anxiety or sadness can strengthen interpersonal bonds by evoking empathy and supportive responses [8, 51].

The Social Penetration Theory [2] offers a foundational framework for understanding the link between self-disclosure and intimacy. According to this theory, relationship development depends on both the depth and breadth of self-disclosure. Emotional disclosure, as a deeper form of sharing, accelerates the social penetration process, thereby enhancing intimacy. However, the effects of self-disclosure do not occur in isolation,they are critically moderated by responsiveness during interpersonal interaction. The Interpersonal Process Model of Intimacy proposed by Reis and Shaver [50] posits that intimacy emerges most effectively when self-disclosure is met with a partner’s responsive reaction. This model provides the foundational “self-disclosure-perceived responsiveness-intimacy” framework for the current study. Specifically, it suggests that for intimacy to develop: (a) an individual must engage in self-disclosure; (b) the interaction partner must respond in a way that is perceived as understanding, validating, and caring (i.e., perceived responsiveness); which in turn (c) enhances intimacy. In the context of our hyperscanning paradigm, the responder’s brain activity can be seen as a neural correlate of generating this crucial responsive behavior, which is hypothesized to facilitate the enhancement of intimacy. Responsive behaviors, such as active listening, empathic feedback, and emotional support, enhance the discloser’s sense of acceptance and belonging, thus improving relationship quality [49].

Empirical evidence further supports this notion, showing that a partner’s responsiveness predicts higher levels of daily intimacy and relationship satisfaction [39]. Consequently, the disclosure-responsiveness dynamic underscores the bidirectional nature of emotional conversation: responsiveness acts as a catalyst that transforms emotional self-disclosure into intimacy [67]. Within this process, state-level empathy serves as the central psychological mechanism underlying responsive behavior. State-level empathy refers to the capacity to perceive, understand, and share another person’s emotional state, encompassing two dimensions: affective empathy (emotional resonance) and cognitive empathy (perspective-taking) [12]. During emotional communication, empathic responses involve not only sharing the emotional state of others but also understanding their mental perspective, validating their emotions, and providing supportive feedback [14]. Such responses enhance the discloser’ s feelings of being understood and relational security, thereby fostering emotional bonding and intimacy.

From a neural perspective, empathic listening engages a network involved in social cognition and emotional resonance, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), insula, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [55, 71]. The co-activation of these regions enables listeners to internally simulate the emotional state of others, facilitating accurate emotional understanding and contextually appropriate responses [57]. State-level empathy, therefore, functions not only as an emotional experience but also as a social motivational mechanism that promotes prosocial tendencies during interaction [5]. Highly empathic individuals are more likely to experience and respond to others’ distress with supportive actions aimed at alleviation [4]. Such empathic engagement strengthens intimacy and trust, reinforcing relationship stability [8, 64, 65]. Moreover, Zaki [70] proposed that empathy operates through emotional synchrony: the alignment of affective states, expressions, or physiological responses between interacting individuals. Emotional synchrony deepens mutual understanding, enhances emotional resonance, and improves the fluency and satisfaction of interaction [43].

Neuroscientific evidence has further elucidated the functional mechanisms of key brain regions involved in emotional conversation. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is a central hub for empathy and social cognition [13, 56]. Specifically, the medial PFC is primarily engaged in mentalizing and perspective-taking, whereas the dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) supports cognitive regulation and inhibitory control of emotional responses [71]. Functional neuroimaging studies (fNIRS and fMRI) have consistently shown that emotional communication or listening tasks evoke increased oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and BOLD signals in the PFC, indicating its sustained engagement in emotional and social processing [66, 68]. Particularly, listening to narratives of emotional events, whether positive or negative, is accompanied by heightened PFC activation, which correlates with stronger empathic responses and subjective intimacy [8, 60]. Positive self-disclosure, in particular, induces greater PFC activation that is positively correlated with perceived social connectedness and relationship satisfaction [72]. Building on this evidence, the present study employs a dyadic fNIRS hyperscanning approach to investigate the neural mechanisms through which emotional conversations enhance interpersonal intimacy. Specifically, we test the hypothesis that neural activation in the responder’s PFC, as a key region for social-emotional processing, serves as a critical neural correlate and potential mediator that helps explain how empathic responses during conversation translate into feelings of intimacy. The present study thus aims to examine whether inter-brain synchrony within the PFC serves as the neural substrate of intimacy enhancement.

Recent research also suggests that emotion-induced increases in intimacy may be closely linked to PFC activation and interpersonal neural synchronization (INS) [66, 68]. Using fNIRS, Wei et al. [66] found that participants exhibited significantly higher PFC HbO concentrations when listening to positive emotional stories than to neutral ones, indicating that sharing positive emotions enhances emotional resonance and social connectedness. Similarly, Xie et al. [68] demonstrated via EEG hyperscanning that positive emotion sharing between dyads increased prefrontal INS, and the strength of this synchronization was positively associated with subjective intimacy. These findings suggest that dynamic PFC coupling may constitute the neural foundation for social connectedness during emotional interaction. INS, as a neural marker of social interaction quality, reflects shared representation and coordination mechanisms at both emotional and cognitive levels [48, 60]. Studies have shown that empathic understanding and mutual attention during real-time interaction significantly elevate PFC INS, which predicts cooperative behavior [31] and correlates positively with emotional alignment and relationship satisfaction [47]. Thus, emotion-induced INS may represent a key neural mechanism underpinning prosocial behavior and interpersonal intimacy.

Nevertheless, most prior research has relied on highly controlled laboratory paradigms with limited ecological validity [58, 66, 68]. Consequently, the neural mechanisms of emotional self-disclosure in more ecologically valid, interactive contexts remain underexplored. As a fundamental process for cultivating trust and intimacy [38, 59] emotional self-disclosure warrants systematic investigation in real-life interaction settings. The present study aimed to address this gap by employing a structured dyadic interaction paradigmthat incorporates face-to-face, free-form conversation. This approach seeks to balance experimental control with ecological validity, allowing for the examination of neural processes within a controlled yet socially meaningful context. Considering that the establishment and enhancement of intimacy typically require a longer time between strangers, whereas friendships inherently possess a pre-existing foundation of intimacy, selecting participants in friend relationships makes it easier to observe changes induced by emotional disclosure. Investigating the neural mechanisms of enhancing intimacy within existing close relationships offers higher ecological validity, as most meaningful emotional sharing and the development of intimate bonds occur within ongoing relationships [50]. Moreover, women typically place greater emphasis on emotional connection and support in close friendships and are more likely to provide emotional responsiveness when friends share affective experiences [17].

Based on these considerations, the present study employed a within-subjects design and fNIRS hyperscanning to examine the behavioral and neural dynamics of female friend dyads under conditions of positive and negative emotional self-disclosure versus non-disclosure. We hypothesized that: (1) Emotional self-disclosure would significantly enhance state-level cognitive empathy, state-level affective empathy, and interpersonal intimacy, with both state-level empathy components positively correlated with intimacy; (2) Emotional self-disclosure, together with the responder’s responsiveness, would predict intimacy enhancement, with responder PFC activation mediating or moderating this process; and (3) Emotional self-disclosure would further strengthen dyadic intimacy by facilitating empathic processing and increasing INS.

Methods

Participants

Before the formal experiment, a statistical power analysis was conducted to estimate the sample size by using the G*POWER 3.1.9.7 software [18]. This analysis was based on a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) within the framework of our 2 (Emotion: positive, negative) × 2 (Disclosure Type: self-disclosure, non-self-disclosure) within-subjects design. The power calculation specifically targeted the detection of an interaction effect, which was central to our primary hypothesis. The analysis was performed with a conventional alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%. Based on an expected effect size of Cohen’s f = 0.25, which represents a medium-to-large effect for social neuroscience studies employing similar paradigms, the analysis indicated that a total sample size of 24 dyads would be sufficient. We recruited 31 female friend dyads from Liaoning Normal University (mean age = 21.68 ± 2.03 years). All friend dyads shared a stable close relationship. Specifically, they had known each other for more than 9 months, were within a two-year age difference, and had similar educational backgrounds. All participants within each dyad were recruited from the same university major and academic year. All participants were native Chinese speakers, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, self-reported normal hearing, and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Prior to the experiment, all participants provided written informed consent. Upon completion of the study, each dyad received CNY 60 as compensation. The experiment was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Liaoning Normal University; code LL2025277.

Materials and measurements

The experimental materials for both the disclosers and the responders were provided by the experimenter. The standardized materials ensured that the core emotional valence (positive/negative) and intensity of the discussed events were comparable and consistent across different trials and participants. This effectively controlled for confounding variables arising from the vast individual differences in the detail, vividness, and emotional intensity of autobiographical memories.

In the self-disclosure condition, disclosers were instructed to narrate a personal positive or negative experience to their partner, following specific guidelines that included concrete emotional expression (e.g., “I felt very excited/upset”) and interactive questioning (e.g., “You’ ll never guess what happened next”). In addition, their themes are clearly required to be related to the daily lives of college students. In the non-self-disclosure condition, To correspond to the theme of the self-disclosure materials, the disclosers were provided with six emotionally valenced passages written from a third-person perspective, covering themes relevant to university students’ daily lives, such as obtaining a driver’s license, group assignments, and final examination. The responder was instructed to respond according to principles that included emotional validation (e.g., “That sounds really exciting/distressing”), nonverbal feedback (e.g., affirming eye contact, nodding), and open-ended guidance (e.g., “What would you do if you could do it all over again?”). Furthermore, to ensure the naturalism of the conversation and the believability of the non-self-disclosure materials, we implemented the following key design features: 1) Internalized retelling: Narrators were asked to read the provided material and then, after the text was removed, retell it to their partners in their own words, beginning with the phrase “I have a friend/classmate who…”. This design aimed to encourage the narrator to “internalize” the story, making their expression more personal and spontaneous rather than a mechanical recitation. 2) Single-blind design for responders: Responders were completely unaware beforehand of the specific content of the material received by the narrator. This simulated a real-world social scenario where a listener is unaware of what will be shared, thereby ensuring the authenticity of their emotional and verbal responses. 3) Free-form conversation: Following the narrator’s retelling, the dyad immediately entered a free-form conversation without a script, designed to mimic the natural flow of interaction in real interpersonal communication.

Following established procedures [8], after each task, the partner rated the emotional valence and arousal of the disclosed event on a 7-point scale. Statistical analysis revealed a significant main effect of emotional valence, with positive valence rated significantly higher than negative valence (F(1, 30) = 15.436, p < 0.001). Importantly, no significant difference was found in emotional arousal (F(1, 30) = 0.954, p = 0.344), confirming the effectiveness of the emotional materials used in this study.

To assess changes in state-level cognitive and state-level affective empathy, this study employed four subjective measures [8, 35]. These items were designed to capture state-level empathy: that is, the participants’ immediate empathic response (both cognitive and affective) following each specific conversation trial, rather than their general trait empathy [10]. A total of four items were used to evaluate participants’ empathic responses following emotional self-disclosure and non-self-disclosure conditions. Based on previous studies [8, 35], two questions were designed to assess state-level cognitive empathy, namely: “To what extent can you understand how your partner felt in the story?” and “When listening to your partner’s narrative, can you imagine yourself as the protagonist in the story to understand their feelings and thoughts?” and the remaining two items assessed state-level affective empathy, namely: “Did seeing your partner (or the protagonist) happy make you feel happy?” and “Did seeing your partner (or the protagonist) sad make you feel sad?”. Participants responded to each item using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all, 7 = Very much).

Regarding the measurement of changes in intimacy, this study similarly employed a 7-point Likert scale (1 = minimal increase, 7 = maximal increase), following the precedent of recent dyadic hyperscanning studies [3, 68]. Prior to the task, participants were provided with a clear operational definition of intimacy as “a subjective feeling of closeness, connectedness, and bondedness with one’s partner.” This measure was selected for its brevity and suitability for repeated administrations within the experimental paradigm, aiming to minimize participant burden and maintain engagement across multiple trials. It was chosen after consideration of alternative scales (e.g., the Inclusion of Other in the Self scale, IOS) for its directness and clarity from the participant’s perspective. The single-item measure of “perceived increase in intimacy” was adopted directly from established hyperscanning studies [3, 68] to ensure comparability with prior literature. Its brevity was also deemed suitable for our repeated-measures design, minimizing participant fatigue after each conversation trial.

Tasks and procedures

The experiment adopted a 2 (Emotion: positive, negative) × 2 (Disclosure Type: self-disclosure, non-self-disclosure) within-subjects design. Drawing on established dyadic hyperscanning paradigms [29, 33], we implemented a structured yet interactive face-to-face conversation task. While participants were provided with core topics and response guidelines to ensure experimental control, the conversational flow itself was unscripted and spontaneous. Simultaneous fNIRS hyperscanning data were collected throughout these interactions (Fig. 1A). In each dyad, one participant was randomly assigned the role of discloser and the other the responder. Participants in each dyad were randomly assigned to the roles of discloser or responder, and these roles remained fixed throughout the experiment. This fixed-role design was employed to maximize experimental control by preventing carry-over effects (e.g., learning, fatigue) that could confound the neural and behavioral measures if participants were to switch roles. Random assignment helped to balance any pre-existing individual differences across the two role conditions, thereby strengthening the validity of comparisons between the neural mechanisms of disclosure and response.Fig. 1Experimental design. The experimental procedure comprised two primary phases: a resting-state acquisition phase and an emotional disclosure phase. During the resting-state phase, a three-minute dyadic resting-state scan was collected both before and after the task block, during which participants were instructed to remain silent and refrain from interaction. The emotional disclosure phase consisted of four task conditions: positive self-disclosure, negative self-disclosure, positive non-self-disclosure, and negative non-self-disclosure. After each task, participants completed subjective rating scales assessing emotional valence and arousal. Each disclosure task lasted three minutes, followed by a one-minute inter-task interval to allow participants’ neural activity to return to baseline prior to the onset of the next task (Fig. 1C).

A pseudo-randomized design was employed to counterbalance the order of the four conditions, which were derived from a 2 (Emotion: positive, negative) × 2 (Disclosure Type: self-disclosure, non-self-disclosure) factorial design. For instance, if the order for the first dyad was positive self-disclosure, positive non-self-disclosure, negative self-disclosure, negative non-self-disclosure, then the order for the second dyad might be negative non-self-disclosure, negative self-disclosure, positive non-self-disclosure, positive self-disclosure. This pseudo-randomized condition arrangement was implemented to control for linear order and carryover effects.

fNIRS data acquisition

This study utilized the NirSmart portable functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) system (NirSmart, HuiChuang, China) to simultaneously record brain activity from both participants at a sampling rate of 11 Hz. Near-infrared light at two different wavelengths (785 and 850 nm) was used to detect concentration changes in oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR). Based on our hypotheses, the region of interest (ROI) for this study was the prefrontal cortex (PFC) [31, 32, 60]. The PFC of both participants in each dyad was covered by a probe set consisting of 2 × 7 optode arrays, forming a total of 19 channels (CHs). These channels were composed of 7 light sources (emitters) and 7 detectors arranged in a specific configuration (Fig. 1B). The placement of the two probe sets conformed to the international 10–20 system. The distance between adjacent emitters and detectors was approximately 3 cm. Prior to the experiment, signal quality was adjusted and calibrated using the NirSmart system.

Data analysis

First, this study conducted a repeated-measures ANOVA on the self-reported intimacy, cognitive empathy, and affective empathy of the dyads (N = 31). Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to explore the relationships between intimacy and both cognitive and affective empathy.

The study utilized NirSpark 1.8.8 for preprocessing and subsequent analysis of the acquired fNIRS raw data. Based on the Modified Beer-Lambert Law, the original optical density was converted into hemoglobin concentration changes [46]. Subsequently, principal component analysis (PCA, [30]) was applied for motion artifact correction, eliminating transient spikes and baseline drift caused by head movements. To further suppress systemic physiological noise from superficial layers, short-channel regression was implemented computationally [et al.,73]. The first principal component extracted from the preprocessed signals across all channels, which captures dominant global physiological fluctuations, was used as a regressor of no interest in the subsequent general linear model. The data were band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.5 Hz to suppress high-frequency physiological noise and slow instrumental drift. In line with common practice in fNIRS studies, we focused our analysis on HbO concentrations due to their higher sensitivity to cerebral blood flow changes and superior signal-to-noise ratio compared to HbR.

After preprocessing, the general linear model (GLM) was employed to localize task-related brain activations. The pre-task resting-state run was used as the baseline for this analysis, as it provides a reference for neural activity in the absence of experimental manipulation. The GLM fitted the convolution of the hemodynamic response function (HRF) with the task design, and β values were estimated via maximum likelihood estimation and tested for significance [21]. The β values for all channels under each task condition were extracted for repeated-measures ANOVA to examine brain activations separately for disclosers and responders. Significant differences were only found at the single-brain activation level for responders. Therefore, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships between responders’ brain activation and interpersonal intimacy, cognitive empathy, and affective empathy.

Similarly, after data preprocessing, the wavelet transform coherence (WTC) method [23] was applied to estimate INS between disclosers and responders for each valence condition and each dyad. The INS for each valence (positive, negative) was time-averaged and converted to Fisher-z statistics. Based on existing research, the analysis primarily focused on the frequency band of 0.02–0.5 Hz, excluding INS caused by physiological signals such as heartbeat and respiration (~ 1 Hz; [24, 74]).

Finally, mediation analysis (Model 4 from the PROCESS macro for SPSS; [28]) was performed using the PROCESS macro in SPSS, with cognitive and affective empathy as the independent variables (X), intimacy as the dependent variable (Y), and either INS or single-channel brain activation as the mediator (M). This model specifies a simple mediation pathway.

Results

Behavioral results

For state-level cognitive empathy, the main effect of Disclosure Type was significant (F(1, 30) = 4.317, p = 0.042, η2 = 0.067). Participants reported higher levels of cognitive empathy in the self-disclosure condition compared to the non-self-disclosure condition.

For state-level affective empathy, the main effect of Emotion was significant (F(1, 30) = 6.517, p = 0.013, η2 = 0.098), with participants reporting higher affective empathy in the positive emotion condition than in the negative emotion condition. The main effect of Disclosure Type was also significant (F(1, 30) = 4.577, p = 0.036, η2 = 0.071), indicating higher affective empathy in the self-disclosure condition compared to the non-self-disclosure condition. For interpersonal intimacy, the main effect of Disclosure Type was significant (F(1, 30) = 5.070, p = 0.028, η2 = 0.078). Discussion

Using fNIRS hyperscanning, the present study examined the behavioral and neural mechanisms underlying emotional sharing in simulated social interactions. The findings revealed that in positive contexts, emotional sharing through self-disclosure significantly enhanced both state-level empathy and interpersonal intimacy. State-level cognitive empathy, state-level affective empathy, and intimacy were positively correlated, underscoring their interdependent roles in emotional communication. Although no significant effects of emotional valence or disclosure type were observed at the level of INS, single-brain analyses revealed that the l-IFG of responders exhibited significantly greater activation, which was positively associated with behavioral indices of state-level empathy and intimacy. Our exploratory mediation analysis identified a plausible associative pathway, whereby the link between increased state-level affective empathy and enhanced intimacy was significantly accounted for by heightened activation in the responder’s l-IFG.

Intimacy, state-level cognitive and state-level affective empathy

Behavioral results demonstrated that state-level affective empathy was significantly higher in the positive emotion condition than in the negative emotion condition. This pattern may reflect the prosocial and affiliative nature of positive emotions (eg., joy and gratitude), which facilitate emotional sharing and foster the motivation to establish and maintain social bonds [1]. In addition, state-level cognitive empathy levels were greater under the self-disclosure condition relative to the non-self-disclosure condition. Self-disclosure provides the listener with first-hand psychological information rich in emotional and cognitive cues, thereby offering a more substantial foundation for perspective-taking and mentalizing [40]. Moreover, self-disclosure serves as a social signal of trust and relational closeness, motivating listeners to adhere to reciprocity norms and allocate greater cognitive resources toward generating appropriate and authentic responses [38].

The present findings also revealed that self-disclosure significantly enhanced both state-level affective empathy and intimacy compared to non-self-disclosure, consistent with the classical “self-disclosure-perceived responsiveness-intimacy” framework [38]. By facilitating mutual understanding and emotional resonance, self-disclosure effectively reduces psychological distance between individuals. The authenticity and emotional contagion embedded in self-disclosure narratives may activate the listener’s emotional simulation system [44, 71], eliciting stronger empathic resonance and relational engagement.

Furthermore, the positive correlation between state-level empathy and intimacy emphasizes the pivotal role of state-level empathy in sustaining close interpersonal relationships. Individuals with heightened empathic capacity are more adept at recognizing others’ emotional cues, demonstrating affective responsiveness, and strengthening relational bonds through trust and care-based behaviors [4, 11, 27, 36].

Neural mechanisms: the responder’s perspective

At the neural level, the responder’s l-IFG showed significantly greater activation when processing positive compared to negative emotional narratives. This activation pattern suggests that this region may play a crucial role in decoding positive socio-emotional information during interpersonal interaction. The l-IFG, as a core hub of the mirror neuron and language networks, not only supports speech production but is also critically involved in understanding others’ emotional intentions and social cues [7]. Thus, the heightened activation in response to positive stimuli could reflect the internal simulation of approach-oriented social signals (e.g., smiling, elevated tone), a process that would facilitate the decoding of the speaker’s emotional state and the generation of contextually appropriate feedback [25].

Additionally, the l-IFG is functionally connected to reward-related structures such as the ventral striatum [62]. Therefore, its enhanced activation during positive self-disclosure is consistent with the view that affiliative social experiences are intrinsically rewarding. Listening to discloser’s positive story may evoke vicarious pleasure, potentially amplifying l-IFG activity through shared positive affect [20, 41]. The observed stronger l-IFG activation under self-disclosure than non-self-disclosure conditions further indicates that its activity is modulated not only by emotional valence but also by the authenticity and intimacy of the interaction. Genuine self-disclosure provides richer semantic and emotional information, requiring more intensive semantic integration and intentional inference, which may in turn elicit heightened activation in the l-IFG [19, 45].

A significant interaction between emotion and disclosure type was also observed. Specifically, under positive emotion conditions, self-disclosure induced stronger l-IFG activation than non-self-disclosure, whereas this difference was not significant under negative emotion conditions. This interaction effect points to a synergy between positivity and intimacy, suggesting that l-IFG activation peaks when positive valence co-occurs with high-intimacy exchanges [61]. This pattern may be explained by the combined influence of the rewarding qualities of positive emotion and the trust-signaling value of self-disclosure, which jointly could enhance social approach motivation [22] and fostering emotional engagement.

The marked asymmetry in neural responses to positive versus negative disclosure warrants further consideration. While our study cannot definitively discriminate between accounts, the null effects in the negative condition invite a nuanced discussion. First, this may reflect a true functional dissociation; negative social interactions likely recruit distinct affective systems (e.g., for threat detection, aversion, or complex regulation) that are not centered on the PFC [42, 54]. Crucially, our fNIRS setup was limited to the PFC and may have missed activity in regions critical for processing negative social interactions, such as the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) or insula [42, 52, 69]. Alternatively, methodological factors like task constraints on supportive responding [53, 71], or psychological factors like arousal ceiling effects for negative stimuli [6, 34, 37], may have attenuated measurable responses. This pattern highlights the potential uniqueness of positive interactions in engaging PFC-centric mechanisms of intimacy. In contrast, negative emotional contexts may heighten arousal and direct attention toward emotion regulation and supportive responding, which might account for the attenuation of neural differences across Disclosure Types under these conditions [63].

Additionally, our exploratory mediation analysis identified a plausible associative pathway, whereby the link between increased state-level affective empathy and enhanced intimacy was significantly accounted for by heightened activation in the responder’s l-IFG. This finding suggests that this neural response may be a key correlate in the process of translating perceived empathy into intimacy within the context of a dyadic conversation. Although the explicit rating of empathy was provided after the dialogue, the psychological process of state-level affective empathy: the spontaneous sharing of the discloser’s emotion, is understood to be an online, relatively automatic response elicited during the act of listening to emotional personal narratives [66]. The fNIRS-measured hemodynamic response in the lIFG, which unfolds over seconds, is hypothesized to be a neural correlate of this ongoing empathic engagement. Therefore, we posit that the experiential onset of empathy precedes or is concurrent with the measured neural activity. The rating of intimacy, in contrast, is a conscious, global evaluation that logically constitutes the final outcome of the entire social interaction. Thus, our model posits that the intensity of the online empathic experience drives the degree of neural engagement, which in turn shapes the resulting intimacy.

However, the cross-sectional nature of our measurements, the modest sample size, and the reliance on a single fNIRS channel preclude strong causal or mechanistic inferences. The association between the brain signal and intimacy was also marginal. Therefore, we frame this result as revealing a statistically significant associative pathway, consistent with our theoretical model, but not conclusive evidence of a causal mechanism. The cross-sectional nature of our data, the modest sample size, and the focus on a single channel necessitate that these findings be interpreted as preliminary and generative for future hypothesis-driven research.

Brain-to-brain synchronization perspective

At the inter-brain level, no significant differences in INS were detected across conditions after correction, implying that INS may not be a ubiquitous feature of social interaction but rather a dynamic neural marker specific to certain interactive configurations. The disclosure-response paradigm entails distinct cognitive roles: the speaker focuses on narrative construction, whereas the listener engages in emotional comprehension, response planning, and verbal articulation [26]. This functional asymmetry may produce temporal and functional decoupling between partners’ neural activities, reducing the likelihood of sustained synchronization. Moreover, responsive communication involves turn-taking and moment-to-moment adjustment, contributing to transient fluctuations rather than continuous coupling.

Such dynamic and multi-layered interaction processes (e.g., feedback generation, social monitoring) may obscure subtle modulations induced by emotion or Disclosure Type [33]. Consequently, in naturalistic social conversations, INS may manifest in brief, episodic patterns rather than as stable, time-locked resonance. This interpretation provides novel insight into the temporal characteristics of neural coupling during real-world social interaction.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered. First, we did not assess the friendship characteristics between dyad members, which may limit our ability to ensure consistency in friendship quality across all dyads. Besides, the sample consisted exclusively of female friend dyads, which enhances ecological validity for female friendships but limits the generalizability of the findings to male-male or mixed-gender interactions. Given well-documented gender differences in empathy and friendship dynamics [17], future research should include diverse gender compositions to examine whether the observed effects extend to other social contexts.

Second, a specific consideration regarding ecological validity arises from our choice to use standardized fictional narratives with dyads who were close friends. While this design allowed for experimental control over emotional content, it creates a potential tension: friends familiar with each other’s lives might have been able to infer that a story was not a genuine personal experience of their partner. Although we implemented procedures to enhance believability, we did not directly measure participants’ perceptions of the stories’ plausibility. Therefore, we cannot definitively rule out that some responders may have doubted the authenticity of the disclosures, which could have influenced their empathic engagement and the resulting intimacy ratings. Future studies could strengthen ecological validity by employing genuine autobiographical disclosures, perhaps within dyads of varying closeness, or by including a post-experiment measure of perceived believability to statistically account for its potential effects.

Third, the measurement strategies have constraints. The use of a single-item scale for intimacy, though practical for repeated administrations, may lack the sensitivity and dimensionality of multi-item instruments. While we provided a clear definition of intimacy to participants, the measure could be influenced by demand characteristics or social desirability. Moreover, we did not quantitatively assess pre-existing friendship quality (e.g., using standardized scales) or baseline states (e.g., mood, arousal, fatigue), which might confound the outcomes [9]. Future studies should incorporate validated multi-dimensional measures of intimacy and relationship quality, as well as control for baseline variables to isolate experimental effects more precisely.

Fourth, the fNIRS data acquisition was limited to the PFC with 19 channels, potentially missing activity in other brain regions critical for social-emotional processing, such as the TPJ, insula, or amygdala, especially for negative emotions. This spatial constraint may explain the null findings in negative emotional contexts and underscores the need for broader brain coverage. Combining fNIRS-EEG could provide complementary temporal resolution and enable more nuanced analysis of transient neural dynamics, such as event-related interpersonal neural synchronization during key interaction moments.

Finally, we did not measure trait empathy. Although our all-female sample aimed to homogenize gender-based variability, individual differences in dispositional empathy might still influence outcomes [15]. Future research should include trait empathy measures (e.g., the Interpersonal Reactivity Index) to explore their moderating role. Additionally, the experimental paradigm, while structured to balance control and ecological validity, may have constrained naturalistic responsiveness variability. Participants were screened for adequate engagement, potentially limiting the range of response quality. Thus, investigating how high versus low responsiveness levels affect neural and behavioral outcomes represents a promising direction.

For future research, we recommend exploring the cognitive neural mechanisms underlying emotional versus non-emotional self-disclosure to disentangle the effects of self-relevance from affective content. Studies could also examine dyadic interactions in more diverse populations (e.g., strangers or clinical groups) and employ longitudinal designs to track intimacy development over time. By addressing these limitations, future work will advance our understanding of the dynamic interplay between brain, behavior, and social bonding in real-world conversations. Notwithstanding these limitations, the current study provides a valuable methodological and theoretical framework for investigating the neural underpinnings of intimacy in ecologically oriented dyadic interactions. Future research building upon this work could employ experimental manipulations (e.g., of empathy), continuous behavioral ratings, or experience-sampling methods during interaction to definitively test the temporal dynamics and causal precedence among these variables, thereby further elucidating the causal roles of these neural correlates.

Conclusion

By employing fNIRS hyperscanning within a naturalistic conversational framework, this study elucidated the behavioral and neural mechanisms through which emotional self-disclosure and partner responsiveness foster intimacy among female friends. The findings highlight that self-disclosure and responsive behaviors are not merely communicative acts but constitute core socio-emotional processes that deepen interpersonal bonds. Enhanced intimacy arises not solely from emotional resonance but from the responder’ s active neural simulation and socio-cognitive processing of shared emotions. Collectively, these results advance our understanding of the neurocognitive foundations of interpersonal intimacy and provide a methodological and theoretical framework for future research on the neural dynamics of complex social interactions.
