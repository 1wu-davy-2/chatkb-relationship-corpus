ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough extensive literature exists on supporting couples after an affair, there is a notable lack of clinical focus on working with the other woman—a figure often viewed as a moral transgressor and subjected to social stigma. Despite this marginalization, her experience is frequently marked by secrecy, emotional ambivalence, and a mix of profound pleasure, guilt, and shame. This paper presents a case study of individual therapy with a Japanese woman in her late 30 s, struggling with the unresolved emotional aftermath of a 5‐year affair that had ended more than 7 years earlier. Early sessions revealed significant unfinished emotional business with her ex‐boyfriend, who left her shortly after his wife gave birth, a relationship she had entered unaware of his marital status. The therapy focused on helping the client process the complex emotional residue of this relationship, which had affected nearly every aspect of her life. Drawing on an affect‐focused integrative approach that combined Emotion‐Focused Therapy (EFT) and Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy (AEDP), the therapist supported the client in processing both the pleasurable and painful aspects of the affair. These had remained unspoken due to the absence of a safe space for expression. Within the safety of the therapeutic relationship, she was able to articulate long‐suppressed needs, reclaim her sense of self‐worth, and regain agency. The case also explores the cultural and gendered dimensions of infidelity within the Japanese context and reflects on implications for affect‐focused clinical practice with clients experiencing stigma or struggling with morally complex personal issues.Keywords: affairs, culture, emotion‐focused therapy, infidelity, Japan, romantic breakup

BODY
The Other Woman in an Affair

In couples therapy, working through a partner's affair is a common and clinically important concern. There is ample literature on how to help couples move from the initial phase of confusion and emotional chaos to later stages of rebuilding trust or parting ways with a sense of closure (Spring 2020). Several therapeutic models have been developed to address infidelity (Atkins et al. 2005), and studies have demonstrated their effectiveness (e.g., Kroger et al. 2012). The literature often assumes a moral framework, aiming to support the couple in overcoming the rupture caused by infidelity.

By contrast, therapeutic work with the other person who has an affair with someone in the committed relationship is notably absent from clinical literature. Although some popular books explore this experience and offer guidance on leaving such harmful relationships (e.g., Culver 2012; DePompo and Butsuhara 2016), there remains little clinical attention or empathic understanding for the person who is engaged in sexual relationship with someone in the relationship. In this case study, the word, the other woman, is used to describe the experience of a Japanese female client. The term may implicitly shift blame away from the man in the relationship, imposing moral judgment on the woman outside the relationship, representing dominant social values and gender bias. Since these values and gender norms strongly shaped her experience, the term was used in this paper to reflect and contextualize her lived reality. The other woman is often perceived as a violator of a sacred relational boundary, unworthy of support or healing. There are notable commonalities in the experience of being the other woman. Affairs often generate intense emotional and sexual intimacy, a sense of aliveness, and emotional authenticity (Perel 2017). Occurring within social, moral, and temporal constraints, affairs can paradoxically heighten erotic intensity. The relationship is often idealized, functioning as a romantic bubble suspended from everyday responsibilities and stress (Allen 2005). The risk of being discovered, combined with longing and uncertainty, adds emotional charge. Parker (2011) noted that many women feel “more alive” and “more like themselves” during the affair. Such experiences can foster a sense of agency, particularly when they challenge traditional expectations of female passivity. Idealization by the partner, the thrill of secrecy, and the urgency of stolen time may temporarily boost confidence (Allen 2005; Perel 2006). Emotional investment is often sustained by the fantasy that “he will leave his wife” (Allen 2005). Affairs can offer affirmation of desirability and emotional worth, especially for women whose needs were unmet in previous relationships.

Yet these moments of aliveness are often shadowed by guilt, shame, and social condemnation. The other woman is commonly labeled a homewrecker, seductress, or shameful secret (Parker 2011). Even when the affair is mutual or initiated by the married partner, the woman's role is socially discredited. Cultural expectations around monogamy and female purity intensify internalized shame, especially in gender‐normative contexts.

Ambivalence is another defining feature. Though desired by the man, the woman must remain hidden—her relationship possible only through secrecy. She may hope for a shared future but simultaneously anticipate abandonment. Joy and intimacy are nearly always accompanied by grief. Allen (2005) described this state as psychological splitting: moments of idealization followed by collapse into betrayal and diminishment. Many women long to be fully seen and acknowledged, yet their role requires invisibility. Their emotional reality cannot be easily shared with friends or family for fear of moral judgment and ostracization. Often, they are cast as threats to marital and social stability—as seductresses who undermine the institution of family.

This complex interplay of desire, shame, secrecy, and relational invisibility has profound emotional consequences. A nuanced, compassionate clinical lens is needed to understand and support those navigating the aftermath of such experiences.

Affairs in Japan

In Japan, affairs (furin) have a long, complex history shaped by cultural, religious, and social transformations. Unlike the moral absolutism often found in religious traditions elsewhere, Japanese attitudes toward adultery have been comparatively varied and lenient, influenced by Confucianism, Buddhism, gender roles, social class, and legal frameworks. In certain historical periods, affairs were not only common but also romanticized.

In the Heian period, for example, “The Tale of Genji” by Murasaki Shikibu depicted the romantic life of “Shining Genji,” an aristocratic man admired for his beauty and love affairs with multiple women, including court ladies and geishas. For aristocratic men, extramarital affairs were not considered immoral but a matter of discretion and social prestige. In contrast, women had far less sexual freedom. Diaries and literary accounts frequently expressed the anguish women felt over their partners' affairs, highlighting how love was seen as fleeting and often outside the bounds of marriage. As Buddhism and Confucianism gained influence—especially during the rise of samurai ethics in the 12th century—adultery became morally condemned. Adultery by women, in particular, was seen as a betrayal of familial loyalty and could result in severe punishment. Nevertheless, even monks and laypeople continued to engage in sexual relationships, despite Buddhist ideals emphasizing detachment from desire.

In the Edo period (1600–1868), a long era of peace allowed town culture to flourish. Extramarital sexual activity, particularly among men, was widely accepted and often associated with wealth and status. Men were permitted to have mistresses and children with them. Ihara Saikaku's 1682 novel, “The Life of an Amorous Man,” depicts the protagonist Yonosuke's thousands of romantic encounters, including with women and young men, reflecting accepted bisexual practices among merchants and samurai. In contrast, women's infidelity was harshly punished, often through public humiliation or execution. Kabuki and popular literature of the time frequently portrayed women's romantic tragedies, reinforcing gendered double standards.

Following the Meiji Restoration in 1868, adultery was reframed less as an individual moral failing and more as a disruption of the family system. Only women were criminally prosecuted for adultery until 1947. Meanwhile, literature continued to idealize affairs as a search for personal freedom and authentic emotional connection for both men and women.

In the 1980s and 1990s, with Japan's economic boom and consumerist culture, extramarital affairs became commodified. Novels and TV dramas such as Jun‐ichi Watanabe's Lost Paradise, which ends in a double suicide, popularized a formulaic narrative of passion, secrecy, and tragic resolution. In these portrayals, affairs offered a romantic escape from emotionally stagnant marriages and restrictive societal roles. As Perel (2006) has noted, affairs are sometimes sought as a form of self‐reinvention or personal transformation. Today, mass media in Japan fixates on celebrity affairs, which generate public scandal and commercial profit. Tabloid coverage of infidelity often provokes public outrage, with celebrities and public figures forced to resign or lose sponsorships. Despite this strong social sanction, media attention to affairs remains constant (Iwakabe 2024).

A 2024 survey (Mynavi 2024) on 2000 married men and women found that over 30 percent of married women and 59.3% of married men in Japan reported having had sexual relationships with someone other than their spouses. The ratio of women reporting sexual relationships outside their marriage is higher in those in 20's and 30's than over 40's. Therefore, there is less moral inhibition in younger generations of Japanese women. For these married women who had affairs, the most common reasons for affairs were marital dissatisfaction and sexless relationships. For single women, affairs often began with workplace interactions—frequently during social drinking events—where conversations about shared work‐related concerns led to emotional intimacy. Many women described these relationships as fulfilling precisely because they were secret. Others felt validated by being desired by men in higher‐status positions (Baila 2024).

Societal expectations and gender role identity may contribute to Japanese women's involvement with married men. The traditional model of marriage in Japan centers on parenting and economic stability, often sidelining romance and sexual fulfillment. The enduring ideal of the “Good Wife and Wise Mother” (ryosai kenbo) encourages women to sacrifice personal desires for the good of the family. As a result, many single women are reluctant to marry, fearing a loss of autonomy and emotional expression. Some commentators argue that women's affairs reflect an assertion of agency and resistance against rigid gender norms (Seto 2015). Because religious prohibitions against adultery are relatively weak in Japan, many women rationalize affairs with the belief that “it's okay as long as no one finds out.” In some cases, married women report that affairs added a sense of vitality and self‐worth to their lives—and even contributed positively to their marriages (Preat 2024).

Affect‐Focused Integrative Therapy

The therapeutic approach in this case integrates two affect‐focused modalities: Emotion‐Focused Therapy (EFT) and Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy (AEDP). Though developed independently, both emphasize emotional experience as central to psychological healing and transformation. EFT is an evidence‐based psychotherapy model rooted in humanistic traditions and supported by research in psychotherapy, developmental psychology, emotion theory, and cognitive psychology (Greenberg 2015). It has demonstrated efficacy in treating depression, complex trauma, generalized anxiety, and marital distress (Timulak et al. 2019). EFT promotes therapeutic change by transforming maladaptive emotion schemes, which develop from unresolved emotional injuries and often lead to persistent patterns of fear, shame, and emotional avoidance. These patterns are associated with cognitive distortions and unsatisfied core needs for attachment security and identity development. EFT therapists create a secure and empathic therapeutic relationship that enables clients to access these emotion schemes and transform them by experiencing adaptive emotions—such as assertive anger to protect boundaries or grief that fosters compassion and connection (Greenberg 2015).

AEDP is informed by short‐term dynamic psychotherapy, experiential methods, attachment theory, affective neuroscience, and emotion psychology (Fosha 2000). AEDP therapists take an affirming, emotionally engaged stance to dyadically regulate intense emotions (Fosha 2021). To support integration, AEDP introduces “metaprocessing,” a technique that invites clients to reflect on moments of emotional transformation, helping them savor and consolidate positive affect (Fosha and Thoma 2020). This upward spiral of positive emotion broadens cognition and behavior and has been linked to psychological and physiological well‐being (Fredrickson and Joiner 2018). Research supports AEDP's long‐term effectiveness for issues such as depression, emotion regulation difficulties, interpersonal problems, and positive functioning including self‐esteem and self‐compassion (Iwakabe et al. 2022).

Both EFT and AEDP offer structured pathways to process unresolved emotional injuries and restore self‐worth. EFT facilitates resolution of unfinished relational issues through interventions like empty‐chair work, which is particularly useful in cases involving secret or socially stigmatized relationships. AEDP emphasizes the client‐therapist relationship as a vehicle for healing and encourages the cultivation of positive emotional states as resources for confronting difficult experiences.

This integrative approach was essential for the present case. The focus on emotion allowed the client to revisit the intensity of the affair and the shame that followed, while also affirming her emotional needs. AEDP's relational stance helped her rebuild trust and examine longstanding fears in the therapeutic relationship. The therapist also needed to help the client process her challenging emotions differently at times: this involved guiding her emotional processing through experiential tasks such as EFT chair work expressing previously disavowed anger toward her ex‐boyfriend at times. At other times, using dyadic regulation, the therapist supported the client in experiencing painful emotions, helping her develop the capacity to deal with emotions with another's support instead of remaining in defensive isolation. Furthermore, AEDP's emphasis on positive emotions and flourishing helped building her strengths early in therapy as a foundation for confronting painful emotions. The integration of these two affect‐focused therapies is particularly helpful with working with clients like the one described in this case study, who presented with intense and unprocessed emotional experiences. This integrative approach allowed the therapist to work flexibly with a wide range of her emotions. AEDP's emphasis on interdependence and attachment security also provided a culturally congruent foundation for working with Japanese clients, as emotional safety in Japanese culture is closely tied to relational harmony. Research has demonstrated the effectiveness of EFT with Japanese clients in resolving emotional injuries (Noda et al. 2023), and AEDP has shown promise with Asian populations (Hanakawa 2011). Together, these modalities offered a culturally adaptable framework to help the client process deeply painful yet meaningful experiences, rebuild self‐worth, and restore a sense of emotional integrity.

This case study aims to deepen the understanding of romantic breakup involving extramarital affairs, especially from the perspective of “the other woman.” While such relationships must be kept secret, their forbitten nature often intensifies the emotional experience, amplifying pleasure, infatuation, feeling of connection, as well as shame, guilt, and jealousy. The case study will illustrate how emotion‐focused interventions grounded in an empathic and validating therapeutic relationship can help allow clients reveal and process complex and intricate experiences in a culturally responsive way.

Case Illustration

Presenting Problems and Client Description

Yuko, a 38‐year‐old single woman, was living with her parents and working part‐time in a small legal office. She earned just enough to support her minimal needs and showed little interest in advancing her career. Her parents provided housing and meals, alleviating financial pressure. Referred by a concerned friend who felt she was not reaching her potential, Yuko shared that her goal was to settle down, have children, and stop working. This desire—once central to her identity—grew increasingly strained as her dating experiences failed to yield meaningful relationships. Frequently using dating apps, she found each match disappointing. As she approached 40, which she considered the absolute deadline for childbirth, she felt increasing emotional instability, marked by tearful episodes, depressive mood, and hopelessness.

She had recently broken up with her boyfriend of 1 year. They had lived together and discussed having a family. Yuko was confident he would propose soon. Instead, he abruptly told her to move out within a week. She was shocked, having believed he was a safe choice—plain‐looking, conservative, and reliable. She admitted to never being romantically attracted to him but believed his sincerity made the relationship stable. His rejection was therefore baffling and destabilizing.

Yuko had grown up in a conventional Japanese household. Her father worked long hours in a reputable firm. Her mother, despite being intelligent and capable, became a homemaker after marriage. Though her mother often claimed satisfaction with her domestic role, Yuko sensed a quiet dissatisfaction and emotional emptiness. After graduating from a top university, Yuko entered the advertising industry. She was charismatic, witty, and had a disarming sense of humor—traits that made her popular among colleagues and clients. She was often asked to join business negotiations and social events because her presence eased interactions. While she enjoyed the attention, it was not always safe or respectful. On several occasions, intoxicated male colleagues made advances. For example, a junior boss once invited her to his apartment for a nightcap, then tried to initiate physical contact while his wife slept in the other room. Though she fended him off and left, she internalized the encounter as something to be excused due to alcohol.

Yuko met Kazu a few years later on a joint project. He was gentle, shy, and, importantly, single—unlike the older, married men who had previously pursued her. They started dating casually. Their relationship developed slowly—coffee, dinner, walks—and felt safe and pressure‐free. They met about once a week, which seemed normal given their workloads. Company policy prohibited romantic relationships on the same project, so they kept their relationship hidden. Over time, Yuko noticed that he never stayed over on Sundays, would only meet in her neighborhood, and avoided outings. Their meetings eventually dropped to once a month. Despite these signs, Yuko, afraid of the truth, avoided questioning him or seeking advice from friends.

Eventually, Kazu confessed that he had married a few months earlier and could no longer see her. Though devastated, Yuko told him she still wanted to see him. For another year, they continued their relationship. She convinced herself that he would eventually choose her. Avoiding questions about his wife, she fixated on their emotional connection. A year later, Kazu told her his wife was pregnant and that he would stop visiting. Reflexively, Yuko congratulated him, then broke into tears. She told him she still wanted to see him, but his visits stopped.

Afterward, Yuko's emotional and professional life unraveled. She became distracted at work, made mistakes, and lost her energetic persona. No longer asked to handle negotiations, she felt her confidence erode. Feeling incompetent, she resigned. Remaining in her apartment, filled with memories of Kazu, became unbearable. She experienced what she later recognized as her first depressive episode—marked by cognitive fog, persistent sadness, and helplessness.

She moved to a small town, seeking respite. She accepted a low‐pressure job well below her capabilities, but soon quit due to emotional exhaustion. She then stayed at a Zen temple, seeking spiritual refuge and fantasizing that Kazu might come to rescue her. When her savings ran out, she returned to Tokyo and moved in with her parents.

Gradually, she regained some stability. However, a deep sense of loss lingered. At 35, her urgency to find a partner resurfaced. She began using dating apps daily. Although she met many matches, they were often married men posing as single, or men who quickly became attached despite her lack of interest. One relationship lasted over a year. She described the man as nerdy and not her type but moved in with him because he felt like a safe option. After a year, he unexpectedly broke up with her. She was stunned. Once again, she turned to dating apps, feeling disillusioned, desperate, and hopeless.

The Therapist

The therapist is a Japanese male clinical psychologist in his early 50 s with over two decades of clinical experience. His research focuses on emotional change processes, particularly the resolution of past emotional injuries. This interest informs his clinical work, which includes extensive experience with both couples and individuals navigating the aftermath of infidelity. His therapeutic approach integrates theoretical frameworks and interventions from Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy (AEDP) and Emotion‐Focused Therapy (EFT). His involvement in this case was shaped by two factors. First, the client's emotional difficulties provided an opportunity to apply and refine AEDP and EFT interventions. Second, the cultural and gendered dynamics embedded in the client's experience offered a rich context for exploring how societal norms influence emotional processing, particularly in cases of romantic betrayal.

In this case, the therapist integrated AEDP and EFT within a Japanese cultural framework. This created a unique therapeutic environment that prioritized emotional safety while remaining attuned to culturally specific dynamics such as shame, social belonging, and relational ethics. He recognized that the client's struggles reflected not only universal psychological wounds but also culturally mediated narratives about femininity, morality, and romantic legitimacy.

The therapist approached the work with cultural humility and clinical curiosity. By honoring both individual and societal influences on the client's suffering, he was able to create a therapeutic space where emotional healing could occur without moral judgment. The case contributed to the therapist's ongoing research by illustrating how affect‐focused models can be adapted for use in culturally specific contexts. Through the careful application of AEDP and EFT principles, the therapist extended existing theoretical models to better accommodate clients whose emotional pain is shaped by sociocultural narratives. This case also highlights the adaptability of experiential approaches in culturally responsive psychotherapy, contributing to the growing literature on cross‐cultural applications of emotion‐focused therapies.

Case Formulation

The case formulation in Emotion‐Focused Therapy (EFT) includes an analysis of core emotional conflicts, unmet needs, emotional processing styles, and attachment dynamics (Goldman and Greenberg 2015). For Yuko, the central issue was the unprocessed loss of her relationship with Kazu—an experience filled with both intense pleasure and profound pain. At the start of therapy, she was unaware of the lingering impact of this breakup, which had occurred 7 years earlier. Kazu had been her first serious boyfriend. Their relationship began as an innocent and affectionate connection, one that aligned with her dreams of marriage and future security. However, once it became an affair, it shattered her idealized love and placed her in a role that was socially and morally condemned. Despite his betrayal, she could neither acknowledge the pain of that violation nor express her emotional needs. She held on to hope that he would return, which made it difficult to grieve the relationship or assert her sense of self. Unable to speak to anyone about her experience for fear of judgment, she carried the emotional burden in isolation. Therapy provided the safe relational space needed to explore and process this unresolved grief.

A second critical area of work involved exploring Yuko's identity development in the context of Japanese gender norms. Throughout her early professional life, senior male colleagues who were ostensibly invested in her growth often made inappropriate advances. She was valued more for her youth and appearance than for her competence or potential. This dynamic cultivated a fear that her worth would diminish with age and that her value was conditional. Consequently, her identity as an independent, capable adult was underdeveloped. In addition, her mother's ambivalence about her role as a stay‐at‐home wife appeared to influence Yuko's own internal conflict around conventional femininity. These experiences underscored the importance of separating her sense of self from gendered expectations and external validation.

The therapeutic relationship also became a meaningful context for examining these dynamics. The therapist—an older man in a position of authority as both a university professor and her clinician—represented a relational figure whose presence could easily replicate past patterns. For therapy to be effective, it was essential to address the gendered and hierarchical dynamics between therapist and client. By naming and exploring these patterns, the therapeutic alliance became a reparative space that supported emotional risk‐taking and identity consolidation.

Course of Treatment

This therapy was conducted in a private psychotherapy setting. The client agreed to have her sessions videotaped and used for research purposes. For the first few months, Yuko and the therapist met weekly. As Yuko found a new job, the session frequency was reduced to accommodate her new work schedule. As she started to do well in everyday life, the session frequency was further reduced to monthly monitoring. At the pretreatment assessment, Yuko initially showed high depression on the Center for Epidemic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D). She also showed emotional dysregulation, low self‐compassion, and experiential avoidance. Her flourishing scale score showed that she was languishing. At the end of a 10‐month treatment, her depression was within the normal range. Other areas of functioning also made clinically significant improvement. There were three important areas of therapeutic work: (a) creating relational safety and accessing her emotional experiences, (b) relational processing: working through gender‐biased experiences and rebuilding her confidence, and (c) working through the complex interplay of emotion, culture, and gender.

Creating Relational Safety and Accessing Her Painful Emotional Experiences

Initially, Yuko talked about frustration with her job that paid so little. She felt she was wasting her time. As the therapist empathized with her frustration and disappointment and focused on creating safety, Yuko tearfully talked about her desperation to find a partner as time was running out for her to have a baby. She then talked about the recent breakup with the boyfriend with whom she was considering to have a baby. To build a strong working alliance, the therapist consistently maintained an empathic and validating stance. In addition, the client's reactions were actively sought to collaboratively construct a comprehensive overview of her life situations and to guide the course of therapeutic exploration. After several sessions, she finally disclosed her secret relationship with Kazu. As soon as she started to talk about him, her tone and mannerism changed. Her voice trembled as she talked about finding out about his marriage. Her vulnerability was eminent. The therapist validated her, saying that whether he was married or not, this was a very important relationship for her and that the therapy was not a place to judge her or him. He also affirmed her courage and willingness to share such an important private experience. Yuko looked relieved with a smile.

The therapist initially had Yuko freely revisit and recount her relationship with Kazu without introducing any structured task. This was important as she could be in control of getting in and out of sensitive materials with which she felt highly vulnerable: this also helped to cultivate her sense of agency and emotional safety. When her distress became less volatile, the therapist invited Yuko to try out chair work with Kazu. As the therapist described what empty chair work was, she burst into tears. But after 1 min of heavy tears, she nodded yes to initiating the work. The therapist asked her to look at him in the chair, but she covered her face with a handkerchief. When asked to see him, her crying became more intense. When the therapist asked if she could give words to her tears, she was not able to speak. Therefore, the therapist expressed her emotions on her behalf.

Th: So much feeling. You have cherished him as if no time has passed since he stopped coming to see you.

Cl: (crying; nods).

Th: It is so hard but so important that you are allowing yourself to sit with him and allowing your feeling toward him this much. You are doing so well. Can you tell him what comes to your mind?

Cl: (Gently shook her head no).

Th: May I try to say in words what you may be thinking but find not so easy to say?

Cl: (Nodded)

Th: (To the chair) I loved you so much that I wanted to stay with you no matter what.

Cl: (Cries harder) Th: (To the chair) But it really hurt me when you left, and I am not over it yet.

Cl: (Sobs; quietly to the chair) I am broken. I am lost. A part of me is still not…(whispering)

Th: Yes. You are doing so well. It will be great for you to voice that feeling.

Cl: (Crying) A part of me (sighs) had died. That is what it felt like. I couldn't say this to you or anyone. I couldn't think this thought because I was worried that you would go farther away from me than you already are. I don't want to lose you. I am scared of losing you.

Th: Say it again to him.

Cl: (Takes a deep breath and sits up straight) I lost you. I lost a part of myself. I have never recovered. It has been so hard.

Th: Can you look at him as you say it? Look him in the eye and tell him.

Cl: (Biting her lips tight and holding her breath as if gathering courage and momentum, yet looked down, sighs).

Th: I am right here with you. You are doing so fine.

Cl: (Takes a deep breath and looks at the chair, in an assertive voice) I don't want to let you go. I always believed that you would come back. I still want to believe that. And now…. I don't want to say good‐bye, but I am not clinging to you. I am not hanging on to you and my memory of you like my life depends on it (A sigh of relief, then started looking angry). I felt so guilty and ashamed of myself because I was going out with a married man. I suffered so much. You made me go through this. I really trusted you. I really believed you. (A sigh, followed by quiet crying)

Th: Can you tell him that again?

Cl: I felt so guilty and shameful, and I endured them because I believed in you. (after crying for 1 min, she takes a sigh of relief and raises her head).

Th: It feels like something is shifting in you. What's happening? What do you notice is happening inside you?

Cl: Don't know. I just feel lighter. Lighter in my body, my chest, my head. I can breathe better. A knot in my throat is gone.

Th: Good. Would you feel that more in your body? You may close your eyes and put your hands where you feel it.

Cl: (closes her eyes and nods) I feel a bit more space in here (with her hand on chest). A bit freer.

Th: Mmh. Yes, feel that. Freeness in your chest.

Cl: (Takes a deep breath with her hand on chest) I am feeling a bit of relief and at the same time some energy.

Th: Would you focus on the energy? What's that like? (Cl: giggles). What happened? Something funny?

Cl: No. It is strange but it feels like some energy is being released.

Th: Let's just feel what is happening in your body.

Cl: It was the first time that I was able to talk about how I felt toward him. It was the first time that I was able to face my feelings… him. That gave me a sense of power. I am sad but I am glad. Do you know what I mean?

Th: Absolutely. Absolutely.

Cl: I never thought I could face it or get over it. But I am (moved to tears).

In the above segment, an empty‐chair work was introduced so that Yuko could express emotions that she had withheld. In the beginning, due to the intensity of fear, she needed to just sit with him, as talking to him seemed overwhelming. The therapist explicitly offered his emotional support and encouragement. She then gradually expressed her feelings. She expressed how much she still cherished their relationship. By expressing these positive feelings that she still held to its full intensity, she was finally able to move into her anger, disappointment, and sadness. Instead of being afraid of losing him, she was able to assert her unmet needs. Expressing these emotions resulted in a sense of relief, which was followed by the rise of energy and vitality. By further focusing on the experience of these emerging positive emotions, her sense of accomplishment was deeply felt.

Relational Processing: Working Through Gender‐Biased Experiences and Rebuilding Her Confidence

Yuko returned to the following session reporting that she had cried extensively throughout the week. Vivid memories of her time with Kazu resurfaced, leaving her emotionally overwhelmed. On one occasion, she had to take a day off from work because she feared breaking down in her office. She expressed not only sadness but also resentment toward the therapist for initiating a process that had stirred such intense emotions. During the session, Yuko and the therapist reflected on her reaction and processed the emotional and relational dynamics that had emerged.

Cl: It is hard for me to say this to you. I resented you for making me go through all these things. I was not sure if I wanted to change anything. I wanted to keep them the way it was. It felt like you pushed me to say good‐bye, which I didn't want to do.

Th: Thank you for letting me know. It was certainly not my intention to make you feel bad or push you. It sounds like you had a very tough week.

Cl: (Perplexed) Okay. It is strange for me to hear you say thank you. Never expected that response.

Th: I am thanking you because you are sharing with me something that is not easy to bring up. That takes courage and effort. I appreciated that you let me know how you really feel. It is important for me to know how you feel, both positive and negative.

Cl: (Tearful) I am touched for some reason. (pauses and sighs). I feel like you just welcomed me, even though I am just complaining. I feel like I could speak my mind.

Th: I am glad that you feel that way. Many men in your life might have not wanted to hear how you felt. I hope that this is a different experience for you.

Cl: Please explain to me more.

Th: When your bosses valued you but also made advances, you were not able to say how you really felt because of societal rules that demand you to be respectful of your seniors, even when they were so inappropriate and hurtful. So it is so important that you can say to me whatever you want because I am also an older man who is in a position of authority.

Cl: It makes sense. (cries). I was not able to say anything back to them. I was shocked when they made advances. I was scared and could not say No. I was afraid of how they would treat me at work if I came across too strong. I was so naïve and helpless.

Th: I think you were so strong to have gone through that by yourself. I do admire such a young person trying to fight off these powerful older men the best that she could. I admire that she went back to work after these things happened. It is scary to have these older men in respectable positions coming onto you. It is no excuse that they were drunk. That was not right.

Cl: (Smiles and cries). It feels like you are speaking my feeling that I felt but could not verbalize clearly. I felt so disillusioned about the society through their acts. I remember often thinking that: “I don't care if I am successful or not because this is the world where these men who do such things are respected and admired.”

Th: I am glad to hear how you felt then. How do you feel about us talking about it this way now?

Cl: A bit weird (laughs). I feel hopeful. I cannot help thinking that if I could have said a half of things that I said to you now when I was younger, things would have been very different. But I did well all by myself. I would like to congratulate her on that (laughs).

Following this emotional exchange, the therapist introduced chair work involving her younger self. He asked Yuko to imagine her 25‐year‐old self seated across from her. In this visualization, she saw a young woman doing her best—masking exhaustion with makeup, sitting up straight, and listening intently despite emotional fatigue. She expressed admiration for the younger version of herself, acknowledging her effort and resilience. She envisioned giving her younger self a warm hug, telling her she was doing well, and reassuring her that she had done her best in the relationship with Kazu. She reminded her younger self of her capacity to love.

This experiential intervention resulted in a sense of internal support and emotional integration. Yuko described feeling peaceful in the days that followed and reported being able to access this feeling again when needed. This study played an important role in rebuilding her confidence and cultivating a more compassionate view of her past and present self.

Working Through the Complex Interplay of Emotion, Culture, and Gender

As Yuko became more emotionally regulated through earlier experiential work, she was able to begin exploring deeper layers of her emotional experiences, particularly those shaped by cultural and gendered expectations. She revisited the history of her relationship with Kazu and traced the evolving complexity of her feelings. What began as an innocent romantic connection eventually turned into an affair that deeply conflicted with her values and self‐image. Upon learning of Kazu's marriage, Yuko experienced shock, followed by intense guilt toward his wife and a simultaneous sense of rivalry. She also described feeling ashamed of being the “other woman,” while secretly savoring the sense of specialness that came with their hidden relationship. At times, she felt a sense of superiority over other women, rooted in being part of a relationship no one else knew about. In the same breath, she expressed longing for the freedom she associated with the older married men who had pursued her, as opposed to the constricted life of her mother, whom she perceived as trapped in the role of a housewife.

These reflections led to several critical insights. Yuko realized she had internalized binary notions of womanhood within Japanese society: either becoming a housewife who chooses security at the cost of independence and passion, or pursuing a career and romance at the risk of being single and socially marginalized. Her pattern of choosing emotionally “safe” but uninspiring partners emerged as a protective strategy—she believed that if she didn't love them deeply, their eventual rejection wouldn't hurt her as much. She also recognized that her prolonged attachment to Kazu reflected a kind of illusion—that she could maintain the benefits of both singleness and connection, when in reality, she had neither. This illusion, she came to understand, was emotionally and existentially unsustainable as their relationship could not be recognized by others and could only exist in secret.

Through this phase of therapy, Yuko gained emotional clarity and a broader perspective on her life narrative. She began to see her experiences not simply as personal failures, but as shaped by cultural narratives around gender, desire, and worth. These insights empowered her to construct a more coherent autobiographical narrative—one in which she could hold nuance, contradiction, and self‐compassion. She developed a deeper understanding of how her affair with Kazu had functioned as both an escape and a mirror, reflecting back the social and emotional constraints she faced as a woman in contemporary Japanese society.

This marked a turning point in therapy, as Yuko shifted from internalized shame to empowered meaning‐making. Her expression of resentment toward the therapist later in therapy was not interpreted as her transference or displacement of her anger but rather a newly developed capacity to express her emotions instead of suppressing them to maintain superficial harmony. Her ability to contextualize her pain within cultural and gendered frameworks helped her feel less isolated and more capable of creating relationships and a future aligned with her evolving values and self‐concept.

Prognosis and Outcome

In the later stages of therapy, Yuko found a part‐time job aligned with her interests. Within a few months, she was offered a full‐time position in recognition of her interpersonal skills and professional competence. Though she initially felt overwhelmed by her new responsibilities, she gradually developed the capacity to manage daily stress. She maintained positive working relationships, including with senior male colleagues, and took on a mentoring role with younger staff members, which gave her a renewed sense of purpose.

In her romantic life, she continued to experience frustrating encounters through dating apps, but she felt more able to assert her needs and set boundaries. Rather than avoiding connection for fear of rejection, she allowed herself to be seen, even when it felt risky. While she still desired to marry and start a family, she no longer felt defined by this goal. She was increasingly curious about how she might live a fulfilling life with or without marriage.

Yuko also joined a local jogging group. Although she initially felt disappointed that no one expressed romantic interest, she later appreciated the group's respectful and relaxed atmosphere. She began to enjoy non‐romantic friendships with men, which helped her feel more confident navigating interpersonal relationships and broadened her sense of relational agency.

Clinical Practice and Summary

The experience of being the other woman varies depending on cultural norms, relational dynamics, and life stage, yet it often carries intense emotional consequences that require therapeutic attention. Yuko's case illustrates how the compounded effects of secrecy, cultural stigma, and emotional ambiguity can hinder natural grieving processes and obstruct emotional integration. Since she could not disclose her relationship to others, the intensity of both positive and negative emotions accumulated to a point where she was no longer able to reflect and process them alone.

To work with such complex emotional experiences, establishing a safe, affirming therapeutic relationship is essential. EFT and AEDP are particularly suited for addressing unresolved emotional injuries. Both models emphasize relational safety, empathic attunement, and the validation of inner experience. In this case, the therapist created a space where Yuko could gradually approach and explore her secret without fear of condemnation. This validation enabled her to revisit and process the emotional weight of the relationship that had remained frozen for years. With the support of experiential interventions, Yuko reconnected with both the pain and meaning of her relationship with Kazu. These techniques allowed her to assert unmet needs, access suppressed grief, and rediscover self‐worth through embodied emotional expression.

Yuko's experience also resonates with broader psychological literature on romantic loss and ambiguous grief. Studies on breakups highlight the disruption to self‐concept and emotional stability that can follow relational dissolution, especially when attachment remains unresolved (Lewandowski 2009; Sbarra 2015). Her mourning process closely aligns with Boss (2007) concept of ambiguous loss: Kazu was neither entirely present nor entirely absent, which made emotional closure elusive.

Furthermore, her experience underscores the cultural shaping of emotional life. Markus and Kitayama's (1991) theory of interdependent self‐construal suggests that individuals in collectivistic cultures may experience relational rupture not only as emotional injury but as identity fragmentation. Yuko's shame and isolation can be better understood in this context—as the loss of a relationship also signified a disruption of her socially embedded sense of self.

This case raises significant implications for working with clients whose identities and relationships fall outside culturally sanctioned norms. Therapists must remain vigilant about their own moral stance and avoid reinforcing the stigmatization that clients already carry. By affirming the full range of affect—including grief, longing, love, and shame—clinicians can support clients in reclaiming narrative dignity amid moral complexity. In particular, this case demonstrates the efficacy of integrating AEDP and EFT for addressing gendered trauma. These models facilitate relational healing, repair ruptures in moral and affective meaning, and rebuild coherent self narratives. They offer pathways for transforming shame and fostering self‐compassion.

In conclusion, Yuko's transformation affirms the capacity of culturally responsive, emotionally attuned psychotherapy to address the wounds of relational trauma and social marginalization. Her case reminds us that those often perceived as morally transgressive may in fact be emotionally silenced—and that it is through empathetic connection, not judgment, that true repair becomes possible.
