ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe PLISSIT model has a significant impact on alleviating sexual dysfunction and enhancing sex life.AimThis study aimed to investigate the 1-year outcomes of the effect of sexual training–counseling based on the PLISSIT model on the resolution of sexual dysfunction among women using contraceptive methods.MethodsThe study was designed prospective, randomized controlled, and experimental, and carried out in 2 stages. In the first stage, 352 women were included in the study. The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) was administered to these women, and their sexual dysfunction status was assessed according to the FSFI cut-off point (≤26.55). And then, 100 women who detected sexual dysfunction were randomized and divided into 2 groups (study group—SG; n = 49 and control group—CG; n = 51). Sexual education-counseling based on the PLISSIT model was implemented only for the SG. At 1-year follow-up, the sexual functions of women in both groups were evaluated with FSFI.OutcomesThe rate of sexual dysfunction in women using contraceptive methods is important, and PLISSIT-based sexual counseling is effective in reducing this problem.ResultsThe sexual dysfunction rate was found to be 28.4% (n = 100). After PLISSIT-based sexual counseling, the sexual functions of the women in the SG improved starting from the third month and the FSFI levels were higher than the CG women at the third month, sixth month, and first year (P < .001). The effect size of PLISSIT-based counseling on the total FSFI score was 37.6%. While in the first assessment, sexual dysfunction rates were 100% in both groups; after 6 months, this rate decreased to 38.8% and 42.9% after 1 year in the SG (in CG, respectively; 94.1% and 80.4%) and the difference was significant (P < .001).Clinical ImplicationsThe results show the importance of sexual function assessment and sexual counseling in women using contraceptive methods, the FSFI can be used in the assessment, and PLISSIT-based sexual counseling was effective in reducing sexual dysfunction.Strengths and LimitationsThe limitations of this research are its cross-sectional nature and the fact that it was conducted only with women who use contraceptive methods.ConclusionStudy results showed that the rate of sexual dysfunction was high in women using contraceptive methods and PLISSIT-based sexual counseling was effective in reducing or resolving sexual dysfunction experienced during contraceptive use.Keywords: contraception user, women, sexual function, sexual dysfunction, PLISSIT model, counseling

BODY
Introduction

Sexuality is an essential part of human life that includes emotional, physical, and psychological closeness shared between individuals looking for mutual satisfaction.1,2 While sexuality is a natural part of existence, it can be shaped by many different elements.3

While the primary purpose of using contraceptive methods is to prevent unwanted pregnancies, they also allow couples to engage comfortably in sexual intercourse without the fear or anxiety of pregnancy. However, contraceptive methods can also have potential disadvantages that negatively affect sexuality or cause sexual dysfunction (SD).4–13

SD in women refers to dysfunction in one or more stages of the sexual response cycle—desire, arousal, and orgasm.14,15 The International Classification of Diseases, 11th edition (ICD-11) describes SD as a condition that comprises syndromes of impaired experiencing of enjoyable consensual sexual interactions due to various reasons.16 The prevalence of SD among women ranges from 20% to 43%,17 and it can significantly affect their quality of life, emotional health, self-esteem, and social relationships.17,18

There are many studies in the literature examining the effect of changes in contraceptive methods on sexual functions.5–13,19–28 Some5–13 show that contraceptive method use has negative effects on sexual function (decreased frequency of sexual intercourse, difficulty in arousal, orgasm, or lubrication), while others20–22 suggest that it may improve sexual behaviors. Some research has reported that the contraceptive method employed does not have an impact on sexual function.23–26 Despite this significant number of studies, there is only limited research that includes recommendations on what to do when sexual dysfunction occurs in contraception users and which methods and models can be used.

In contraceptive counseling, it is important to evaluate the sexual functions of the applicant and provide sexual counseling when necessary. It is recommended that health professionals use a model to effectively evaluate sexual functions and provide the most appropriate care in the presence of SD.27 The education and counseling provided with the PLISSIT model, which is frequently used in this direction, can form a bridge between the health professional and the individual in eliminating SD.28,29 The PLISSIT model was developed by Annon in 197630 and revised by Taylor and Davis in 2006.28 The model aims to enable the individual to share information about sexuality and meet care needs related to sexual health. The efficacy of the PLISSIT (Permission, Limited Information, Specific Suggestions, Intensive Therapy) model28 in providing sexual education and counseling has been demonstrated in several studies.31–34 Nevertheless, the number of studies employing the PLISSIT model in addressing the challenges faced by women experiencing SD during contraceptive method use remains limited. Moreover, there is a paucity of research that has evaluated the effectiveness of training and counseling provided using this model over a 1-year period. To address this knowledge gap, the present study aimed to investigate the 1-year outcomes of the effect of sexual training and counseling based on the PLISSIT model on the resolution of sexual problems among women using contraceptive methods. The results will contribute to the literature and to the health care services and clinical practices of professional health care providers in this field.

The study sought to answer the following question: In cases of sexual dysfunction in women using contraceptive methods, what is the effect of counseling based on the PLISSIT model on resolving the problem and improving sexual function?

Methods

The CONSORT checklist was used in reporting the study.

Study design

This prospective, randomized controlled, and experimental study was conducted in the Family Planning outpatient clinic of a public hospital in southeastern Türkiye.

Participants

The study was conducted in 2 stages. In the first stage, a total of 352 women who were ≤45 years old, had a sexual partner, had an active sexual life, used one of the effective contraceptive methods (IUD, COC, condom, injection method, Implanon) for at least ≥3 months and volunteered to participate in the study were included in the sample. The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) was applied to participants, and sexual dysfunction (SD) status was identified according to the FSFI cut-off value (≤26.55). The second stage of the study was then initiated.

Randomization

In the second stage of the study, 100 women—with sexual dysfunction according to the FSFI cut-off value in the first stage assessment—were divided into 2 groups by simple randomization.

There was used women’s hospital admission protocol number for the randomization. Those whose protocol numbers ended with an even number were included in the study group (SG; n = 49), and ended with an odd number were included in the control group (CG; n = 51).

Instruments

Data were collected through the participant identification form and the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI). The participant identification form consists of 13 questions related to participants’ socio-demographic, obstetric, and contraceptive method usage characteristics.

The Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) was developed by Rosen et.al (2000). FSFI is a 19-item Likert-type scale to evaluate sexual problems and sexual function over the past 4 weeks.

The FSFI comprises 6 subdomains: desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain.

The total FSFI score ranges from 2 to 36, with higher scores indicating better female sexual function.35 The FSFI cut-off value is ≤26.55, and the score of <26.55 indicates sexual dysfunction.36 In this study, the Turkish version of the FSFI that was validated and adapted by Aygin and Eti Aslan was used.37 In this study, Cronbach’s alpha value for the FSFI total was found to be .86, and the subdomains’ Cronbach’s alpha values were between .91 and .93.

Ethics

Written institutional study permission was obtained from the Health Sciences University Gazi Yaşargil Training and Research Hospital and approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the same institution (Approval date:27.04.2018; Decision no:2018/71) for both permissions). Written consent was obtained from the participants who agreed to the study.

Implementation of the study

Stage 1 of the study and initial FSFI assessment

Written consent was obtained from women who met the inclusion criteria of the study and volunteered to participate (N = 352) after brief information about the study. The participant identification form and FSFI were administered to the 352 women (first FSFI assessment). The face-to-face interview technique was used in applying these forms. In this stage, women with sexual dysfunction were determined according to the FSFI cut-off score (≤26.55).

Stage 2 of the study

Women who were diagnosed with sexual dysfunction (n = 100) at the initial FSFI assessment were randomized and divided into 2 groups.

Other FSFI assessments and consulting practice

First interview: After randomization, study group (SG) participants were contacted by telephone and invited to the hospital. In order to provide one-on-one training–counseling, a maximum of 10 women were invited in 1 day and all invited women attended their scheduled appointment. The counseling was conducted individually. While the duration varied depending on each woman’s needs, each session lasted approximately 30-40 minutes on average.

A total of 5 interviews were conducted with the women included in the study group, with the first counseling being conducted face-to-face and subsequent sessions delivered via telephone. There was no negative feedback or resistance from SG women to the PLISSIT model and counseling sessions. The second FSFI assessment was conducted 1 month after the counseling provided to the SG women. In the second and subsequent FSFI assessments, the first administration of the FSFI was conducted, followed by brief and concise one-on-one counseling based on the conditions identified in the first step of the PLISSIT. FSFI assessments were performed 5 times in SG women (initial, first, third, sixth months, and 1 year later), and 3 times in CG women (synchronously with SG, initial, sixth month, and 1 year later). The FSFI assessments in both groups were synchronized to ensure comparable time points. Researchers systematically recorded all assessment data. No training–counseling was provided to women in the control group (CG) during the study period.

Implementation of sexual training–counseling according to the PLISSIT model

Permission (P) stage: Women were asked questions such as: “Women using contraceptive methods may experience concerns or problems in their sexual lives during use. Would you like to share anything about this?” Participants were prompted to articulate their emotions, cognitions, and personal experiences related to contraceptive use and were explicitly permitted to disclose any associated concerns, anxieties, or apprehensions.

Limited Information (LI) stage: In response to the questions posed by the women or by the answers received from the women, limited information was provided regarding the potential impact of the contraceptive methods they use on their sexual functions.

Specific Suggestion (SS) stage: Specific suggestions were made by taking into account the sexual problems identified during the interview or expressed by the woman and her individual needs on the subject.

Intensive Therapy (IT) stage: In this context, no woman was sent to therapy.

Second interview and second FSFI assessment

This interview was conducted by telephone with the SG women 1 month after the initial FSFI assessment and the counseling provided in the first interview. The FSFI was first applied to the SG women (second FSFI assessment), and then, they received brief sexual counseling according to their needs. There was no interview or assessment with the CG women.

Third interview and third FSFI assessment

This interview was conducted by telephone 3 months after the initial FSFI assessment and the counseling provided in the first interview. FSFI was first applied to the SG women (third FSFI assessment) and then received brief sexual counseling according to their needs. No intervention or assessment was given to the CG women.

Fourth interview and fourth FSFI assessment

This interview was conducted by telephone 6 months after the initial FSFI assessment. FSFI was first applied (fourth FSFI assessment for SG women) and then received brief sexual counseling SG women according to their needs. CG women were also contacted by phone, and only the FSFI assessment was performed (second FSFI assessment for CG women).

Fifth interview and fifth FSFI assessment

This interview was conducted by telephone 1 year after the initial FSFI assessment. During the 6 months after the fourth interview, no counseling was provided to the women in the SG. The final telephone interview was conducted at the end of the first year, during which the FSFI was administered to the women in the SG group (fifth FSFI assessment for SG women) and then asked about their sexuality-related needs and brief counseling was given according to their needs. CG women were also contacted by phone, and only FSFI assessment was performed (third FSFI assessment for CG women). In the final interviews conducted at the end of one year (fifth interview for SG and third interview for CG), interviews could not be conducted with 6 women from the SG group and 5 women from the CG group as they could not be reached. The study was completed with 89 women (SG: n = 43; CG: n = 46). All stages of the study are given in the CONSORT Flow Diagram for the Study (Figure 1).

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 23. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied, and it was determined that the data showed normal (P > .05) distribution. Independent t-test, chi-square, and 2-way (2-factor) repeated-measures ANOVA test were used in the analysis. The chi-square test was used to test whether there was a difference between the 2 groups in terms of descriptive characteristics and sexual dysfunction. In each sexual function assessment, the t-test was used to analyze the difference between the 2 groups in the FSFI subdomains and total score averages. A repeated-measure ANOVA test (general linear model) was used to test whether there was a statistical difference between the initial FSFI assessment and the 4 subsequent FSFI assessments made at different times (a total of 5 assessments) in the SG. The Bonferroni test was used as a post hoc test. In repeated measurements during sexual function follow-ups, the 2-way (2-factor) repeated-measures ANOVA was used to test whether there was a difference between the 2 groups in terms of FSFI subdomains and total values at the first month, 6 months, and 1 year later assessments. In the last assessment made (1 year later), 6 women from the study group and 5 women from the control group could not be reached. Therefore, intention-to-treat analysis (ITT) was performed and the results were analyzed accordingly. All results were evaluated at a 95% confidence interval and 0.05 significance level.

The mean age and duration of marriage were higher in the CG (P .05) (Table 1). Discussion

In the present study, sexual education- counseling based on the PLISSIT model was provided to women using contraceptive methods who were identified as having SD according to the FSFI, and the effect of this counseling on reducing SD and improving sexual functions was examined by following the cases for one year. The study was carried out in 2 stages. First, 352 women using contraceptive methods were identified as having SD according to the FSFI. This evaluation revealed that 28.4% of the women experienced SD, meaning that 1 in every 4 women was affected. The prevalence of SD in women is reported to vary between 20% and 43% in the literature.17 This result obtained from the study supports the literature data. While many studies show that contraceptive use negatively affects sexual function and that this effect may vary depending on the method,5–13,20–22 other studies argue the opposite.23–26 In this study, no correlation was found between the contraceptive method used and the SD experienced. The contraceptive methods used differed between the SG and the CG women in the study. However, the lack of correlation between the contraceptive method used and the level of SD experienced by women was considered important in strengthening the data regarding sexual counseling results based on the PLISSIT model.

Although studies have applied the PLISSIT model to women with various medical conditions, most have used the FSFI to measure sexual function before and after the intervention. These studies have reported that education and counseling based on the PLISSIT model significantly improved sexual function.29,31–34,38–45 In the second phase of the study, the effects of PLISSIT-based sexual counseling given to women with SD who received the SG were monitored for 1 year. The FSFI subgroup and total mean scores for the women who received the counseling began to increase by the third month, peaked by the sixth month, and remained close to the sixth-month level 1 year later (Table 2 and Figure 2). These results are valuable because they demonstrate the importance of sexual counseling for women using contraceptive methods and the effectiveness of PLISSIT-based sexual counseling in reducing SD. In the CG, there were also significant improvements in the FSFI desire and pain subscales, as well as the FSFI total score, in assessments conducted 6 months and 1 year after the initial assessment (Table 2). However, the increase in this score was minimal in the FSFI total and below the cut-off value. Many factors may have contributed to these results, but it is thought that they may be related to women’s or couples’ adaptation to the contraceptive method over time or to the awareness raised about sexuality by the FSFI evaluations.

In SG and CG women with SD detected in initial assessment, when evaluating whether the improvement in sexual function differed between the SG and the CG at 6 months and after 1 year, the results showed highly significant differences in favor of the SG at 6 months and after 1 year in FSFI total and all sub-dimensions. The effect of PLISSIT model–based education on the improvement in FSFI total, which indicates sexual function status in general, was 37.6% (Table 3 and Figure 3). All these results showed that sexual education–counseling based on the PLISSIT model had a long-term and significant positive effect on the improvement of sexual function, and the results were consistent with the findings of other sexual education and counseling studies using the PLISSIT model in the literature. Improvements were observed in the third and sixth months of the study by Tütüncü and Yıldız,39 in the second and third months of the study by Khakbazan et al.,40 and after the third month of the study by Almeida et al.32 Zangeneh et al.46 also reported an improvement in women’s sexual function, and Khoei et al.47 reported positive changes in sexual capacity, motivation, and performance in the 6th and 12th weeks. Like these studies in the literature, our study result underlines the potential of the PLISSIT model as an effective intervention to improve sexual function in various clinical populations.

Providing structured sexual education and counseling tailored to women can significantly reduce sexual dysfunction and enhance overall quality of life.31–34 According to the literature, application of the first 3 stages of the widely utilized PLISSIT model is effective in resolving approximately 80%-90% of sexual problems.31–34,38 Although the number of studies using the PLISSIT model in sexual counseling for contraceptive method users is limited, a study by Ahmed et al.48 indicated that the PLISSIT model had a positive effect on sexual dysfunction in women using oral contraceptives. In our study, the continuation rates of SD were examined among women in both groups, all of whom initially presented with 100% SD. Six months after the initial sexual training–counseling based on the PLISSIT model, the rate of SD decreased to 38.8% in the SG and to 42.9% after 1 year. In contrast, SD persisted in 94.1% of the CG at 6 months and 80.4% at 1 year (Table 4). These findings highlight the effectiveness of sexual training–counseling based on the PLISSIT model in addressing SD. The slight increase in SD rates in the SG after 1 year, compared to the more substantial improvement observed at 6 months, may be explained by the structure of follow-up: during the first 6 months, participants received additional online counseling every 3 months, whereas no additional counseling was provided during the subsequent 6 months. This suggests that the continuity of counseling, even at 3-month intervals, is critical and highlights the importance of ongoing support in maintaining improvements in sexual function.

The results obtained were considered to be of significance in demonstrating the importance of utilizing the PLISSIT model in addressing sexual dysfunction. Furthermore, it was considered important to reveal that counselling based on the PLISSIT model, which is utilized in the literature to address sexual functions/dysfunctions due to various medical conditions, would be beneficial when employed within the context of family planning services, thereby contributing to the existing literature in this domain.

Limitations

The limitations of this research are its cross-sectional nature and the fact that it was conducted only with women who are using the contraceptive method.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the impact of sexual education and counseling according to the PLISSIT model on the resolution of sexual problems in contracepting women over a 1-year time frame. The women who received counseling experienced a statistically significant enhancement in sexual function starting 3 months after the educational counseling program. Longer-term follow-ups at 6 months and 1 year showed even greater improvement in SG sexual function compared with CG. In addition, a significant drop in the sexual dysfunction rate was recorded.

The results indicated that sexual education and counseling, according to the PLISSIT model, had a significant impact on alleviating sexual dysfunction and enhancing sex life. Healthcare professionals working in family-planning services must address sexual problems, assess women for sexual functioning, and provide sexual counseling alongside family-planning counseling. There exists a multitude of potentially useful models for sexual counseling in a general sense or where sexual dysfunction has been detected. The PLISSIT model is one of the most effective and practical models for sexual counseling.
