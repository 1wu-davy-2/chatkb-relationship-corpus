ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe co-occurrence of physical diseases, psychological disorders, and cognitive impairment—here termed physical–psychological–cognitive multimorbidity (PPC-M)—poses growing challenges to healthy aging but its upstream, modifiable determinants remain underexplored. Sensory impairments are common in later life and may be associated with the emergence of PPC-M, partly through psychosocial factors such as loneliness. This study aimed to examine the associations of vision, hearing, and dual sensory impairments with incident PPC-M and to assess whether loneliness partly explains these associations in three national ageing cohorts.MethodsWe analyzed 15,022 adults aged ≥ 45 years from three nationally representative aging cohorts—CHARLS (China), HRS (United States), and ELSA (England)—followed for 6–8 years. Vision impairment (VI), hearing impairment (HI), and dual sensory impairment (DSI) were self-reported at baseline. Incident PPC-M patterns included: physical–psychological (P1P2), physical–cognitive (P1C), psychological–cognitive (P2C), and triple comorbidity (P1P2C). We fitted Cox proportional hazards models and multi-state survival models, adjusting for demographic factors, educational level, marital status, and health behaviours. Loneliness was assessed using harmonized measures and tested as a mediator within a counterfactual mediation framework. Sensitivity analyses included propensity score matching, competing risk of death, and alternative definitions of exposures and outcomes.FindingsAt baseline, 52.8% reported no sensory impairment, 10.9% HI only, 17.2% VI only, and 19.1% DSI. A graded association emerged across PPC-M outcomes. Compared with no impairment, DSI showed the strongest associations with P1P2 (HR = 2.18), P2C (HR = 2.36), and P1P2C (HR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.19–2.03), with VI generally showing stronger associations than HI. Results were directionally consistent across countries and robust in sensitivity analyses. Loneliness statistically accounted for approximately 10–12% of the observed associations across impairment types, suggesting a potential psychosocial explanatory pathway. Multi-state models suggested accelerated transitions from single-domain conditions to combined multimorbidity among those with DSI.InterpretationSensory impairments—especially dual impairment—were independently associated with the onset and progression of PPC-M. Loneliness accounted for a meaningful share of these associations, suggesting a potential target for risk stratification and intervention. Integrating accessible and affordable vision/hearing services with loneliness-reduction strategies may help identify older adults at elevated risk of complex multimorbidity and inform equitable healthy-aging strategies across diverse settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02958-0.

BODY
Introduction

Multimorbidity, the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions, is a major public health challenge in the aging global population [1, 2]. Research has traditionally focused on the accumulation of physical diseases. However, multimorbidity frameworks increasingly distinguish physical, psychological, and cognitive domains and their co-occurrence [3–5]. In recent multicountry and longitudinal ageing studies, these domains have been operationalized as distinct combinations, including physical–psychological, physical–cognitive, psychological–cognitive, and physical–psychological–cognitive multimorbidity [4, 5]. PPC-M, characterized by the co-occurrence of physical diseases, mental disorders, and cognitive impairment, places a substantial burden on older adults. This complex comorbidity pattern reduces quality of life and functional independence, while increasing caregiver and economic burdens on families and healthcare systems [6, 7]. Therefore, identifying early and modifiable factors associated with PPC-M is important for developing targeted strategies to promote healthy aging.

Sensory impairments, particularly vision impairment (VI) and hearing impairment (HI), are highly prevalent among older adults [8]. Prior research has linked these impairments to various individual health problems. For instance, HI is a key risk factor for dementia and is associated with an increased risk of other diseases [9, 10]. Similarly, VI is strongly associated with cognitive decline, depressive symptoms, and higher mortality [11, 12]. However, these studies have primarily focused on single health outcomes. Whether sensory impairments serve as upstream markers associated with the simultaneous development of physical, psychological, and cognitive problems remains unclear. Specifically, it is unknown whether different types of sensory impairment (VI alone, HI alone, or dual sensory impairment) accelerate the progression to PPC-M.

Complex biopsychosocial pathways may link sensory loss to multimorbidity, and loneliness is a plausible mediator [13]. Hearing impairment can hinder communication, while vision impairment limits social participation and environmental interaction. Both conditions may increase social isolation and feelings of loneliness [14]. Loneliness is a potent stressor and is associated with depression as well as cardiovascular, immune, and cognitive dysfunction through neuroendocrine and inflammatory pathways [15, 16]. Recent evidence has further linked functional limitations, including vision, hearing, cognitive, and mobility limitations, with adverse psychological outcomes, while longitudinal data suggest that persistent loneliness and social isolation are associated with subsequent functional impairment and mortality in older adults [17, 18]. Therefore, we hypothesized that sensory impairments may be associated with PPC-M partly through loneliness, a psychosocial factor linking sensory health with later physical, psychological, and cognitive outcomes.

This study uses data from three large, nationally representative aging cohorts: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS), and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). We aim to prospectively evaluate the association between individual and dual sensory impairments and the risk of incident PPC-M. We will also quantify the mediating effect of loneliness in this pathway. This research seeks to provide novel evidence on how sensory impairments affect the overall health of older adults and to identify loneliness as a potential target for interventions aimed at preventing complex multimorbidity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, multi-cohort study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. We analyzed individual-level data from three publicly available longitudinal aging studies: the HRS, the ELSA, and the CHARLS. These are established population-based aging cohorts in the United States, England, and China, respectively, designed to provide nationally representative samples in their original study settings. All three cohorts used stratified, multistage probability sampling designs and followed similar standardized protocols, conducting follow-up surveys every 2–4 years to collect data on sociodemographics, health status, psychological state, and biomarkers. These surveys have been widely used in ageing research and apply standardized questionnaires, trained interviewers, and quality-control procedures to support data reliability and cross-wave comparability. To ensure cross-cohort comparability and minimize heterogeneity, we harmonized the data waves, selecting HRS waves 10–13 (2010–2016), ELSA waves 6–9 (2012–2018), and CHARLS waves 1–4 (2011–2018).

Participants were included if they were: (1) aged 45 years or older at baseline; (2) had complete baseline data on vision and hearing impairments; (3) were free of PPC-M at baseline and had complete data on comorbidity patterns at follow-up; and (4) had no missing data on any covariates. The participant selection process is detailed in Fig. 1. Ethical considerations were addressed in the original cohort studies: HRS, ELSA, and CHARLS each obtained approval from their respective institutional review boards or ethics committees, and all participants provided written informed consent. The present secondary analysis used de-identified, publicly available data; therefore, no additional ethical approval was required.

Assessment of vision, hearing, and dual sensory impairments

Baseline sensory impairments were assessed using self-reported, harmonized data from the HRS, ELSA, and CHARLS cohorts. Across cohorts, hearing, distance vision, and near vision were assessed using conceptually comparable self-rated items with the same five response categories (“excellent”, “very good”, “good”, “fair”, or “poor”), allowing a common binary definition to be applied. Consistent with previous research, participants who rated their hearing or vision as “fair” or “poor” were classified as having an impairment. Specifically, HI was determined by a single question on overall hearing ability, while VI was defined as an impairment in either distant or near vision. Responses of “don’t know” or “refused to answer” were treated as missing data. Dual Sensory Impairment (DSI) was subsequently defined as the presence of both HI and VI. This resulted in four mutually exclusive baseline categories: no sensory impairment, HI only, VI only, and DSI (Supplementary Table S1).

Assessment of loneliness

Loneliness, the potential mediating variable, was assessed at baseline using cohort-specific instruments. In the HRS and ELSA cohorts, the three-item UCLA Loneliness Scale measured how often participants felt a lack of companionship, felt left out, or felt isolated. Based on the total score (range: 3–9), a score of 6 or higher was used to classify participants as lonely. In the CHARLS cohort, loneliness was assessed with a single question; participants were classified as lonely if they reported feeling lonely on “three to four days a week” or more. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Physical, psychological and cognitive multimorbidities

Physical, psychological, and cognitive conditions were assessed in all cohorts at baseline and follow-up using standardized instruments that have been widely used in ageing cohort studies. Physical morbidity was defined by seven chronic diseases: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke, and arthritis. These were identified via self-reported physician diagnoses, a method previously validated against clinical records. Psychological morbidity was evaluated using validated and widely used CES-D scales adapted within each cohort. In the HRS and ELSA, it was defined by a score of ≥ 4 on the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). In the CHARLS, the threshold was a score of ≥ 10 on the 10-item CES-D. Cognitive status was assessed with standardized cognitive batteries covering episodic memory (immediate and delayed word recall), orientation (time and space), and executive function. The latter was measured using the Serial 7s test in HRS and CHARLS, and the animal fluency test in ELSA. Cognitive impairment was defined as a score more than 1.5 standard deviations below the age-specific mean in at least one domain, stratified across eight age groups (45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, and ≥ 80 years). The outcome taxonomy was specified a priori based on all cross-domain combinations of the three prespecified health domains, rather than derived from the study data. Consistent with prior studies of physical, psychological, and cognitive multimorbidity, the primary outcomes were four incident multimorbidity patterns: (1) physical-psychological comorbidity (P1P2), (2) physical-cognitive comorbidity (P1C), (3) psychological-cognitive comorbidity (P2C), and (4) physical-psychological-cognitive trimorbidity (P1P2C) [4, 5]. Each pattern was defined by the co-occurrence of the specified conditions.

Covariate assessment

Covariates were selected a priori based on prior epidemiological evidence and a conceptual confounding framework, prioritizing variables that were plausibly related to both sensory impairment and PPC-M and could be harmonized across the three cohorts. These included demographic factors (age and sex), socioeconomic position (educational level), social context (marital status), and health behaviours (smoking status and alcohol consumption). To ensure comparability across cohorts, education was categorized as “less than secondary education” versus “secondary education and above.” Marital status was dichotomized into “married” and “unmarried.” Smoking and alcohol consumption were treated as binary variables (ever vs. never).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by sensory impairment status and study cohort. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs) and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Between-group differences were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or its non-parametric equivalent for continuous data, and the chi-squared (χ²) test for categorical data. Transition patterns of multimorbidity states from baseline to follow-up were visualized using Sankey diagrams, stratified by sensory impairment status.

The association between sensory impairment and incident multimorbidity was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensory impairment was modeled in two ways: (1) as a four-category variable (no impairment [reference], hearing impairment only, vision impairment only, and dual impairment), and (2) as two separate binary variables (hearing and vision impairment) to assess their independent effects. To evaluate the influence of confounder adjustment, we developed three sequential models for each outcome: Model 1 (crude), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, marital status, and education), and Model 3 (additionally adjusted for smoking and alcohol consumption).

Multi-state survival models were used to analyze state transitions from a healthy state to various comorbidity patterns. These models, adjusted for all covariates, estimated HRs for the independent effects of hearing and vision impairment and their synergistic effect (i.e., dual vs. single impairment) on specific transitions.

The potential mediating role of loneliness in the pathway from sensory impairment to multimorbidity was examined using two methods: (1) the Baron-Kenny method adapted for survival data to estimate the proportion mediated, and (2) VanderWeele’s counterfactual mediation approach to estimate the natural direct (NDE) and indirect effects (NIE).

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our findings, including: (1) a Fine-Gray competing risk model to account for the competing risk of other comorbidities; (2) propensity score matching (PSM) to balance baseline covariates; and (3) calculation of the E-value to assess potential unmeasured confounding.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study population comprised 15,022 participants. Of these, 52.8% had no sensory impairment, while 10.9% had hearing impairment only, 17.2% had vision impairment only, and 19.1% had dual sensory impairment. Compared to the non-impaired group, participants with sensory impairments were generally older, more likely to be male, and had lower educational attainment, with most baseline differences being statistically significant (P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S3). These distribution patterns remained consistent across the three national cohorts (Supplementary Tables S4-S6). State transitions in multimorbidity status from baseline to follow-up, stratified by sensory impairment status, are visualized in Sankey diagrams (Fig. 2).

The association between sensory impairment and incident multimorbidity patterns (P1C, P2C, P1P2, and P1P2C) was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models under three sequential adjustment levels (Table 1). Both the categorical effects (hearing only, vision only, dual impairment vs. none) and the independent effects of hearing and vision impairment were examined. A significant dose-response relationship was observed for P1P2, P2C, and P1P2C. Compared with participants with no impairment, single sensory impairment (either hearing or vision) was associated with higher hazards of these outcomes, while dual impairment consistently showed the strongest associations. This graded association remained robust after full multivariable adjustment (Model 3). Furthermore, when modeled as separate variables, both hearing and vision impairment remained significantly associated with these outcomes after adjustment, suggesting distinct associations. In contrast, the findings for P1C were distinct. In the fully adjusted model, vision impairment only and dual impairment were associated with a reduced risk of incident P1C. Cohort-stratified analyses revealed specific variations in these associations across the different population contexts (Supplementary Tables S7-S9).

Multi-state model analysis revealed complex transition dynamics among comorbidity states. The overall transition patterns were first visualized using Sankey diagrams, transition matrices, and network plots, which depicted changes in state distribution, frequency, and structure between baseline and follow-up (Supplementary Figures S1-S3). To quantify factors associated with these state transitions, multi-state models were employed (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figure S4). Analysis of independent effects VI showed stronger associations than HI with initial transitions from a healthy state (None) to P1P2 (HR = 1.59) and P2C (HR = 1.71). Furthermore, a clear synergistic effect was observed: dual sensory impairment markedly amplified the risk of transitioning to P1P2 (HR = 2.18) and P2C (HR = 2.36) compared to single impairments.

To explore whether loneliness may partly explain the association between sensory impairments and multimorbidity, mediation analysis was conducted (Table 2). The analysis revealed that loneliness was a significant partial mediator in the association between sensory impairment and the development of several multimorbidity states. Specifically, loneliness accounted for a substantial proportion of the total effect on the risk of P1P2C, mediating 11.2%, 10.7%, and 12.2% of the effect of hearing impairment only, vision impairment only, and dual impairment, respectively. The proportion mediated was also significant for P1P2, accounting for 11.2% (hearing impairment only), 7.4% (vision impairment only), and 5.8% (dual impairment). A similar mediating role was observed in the pathway toward P2C. The specific strengths and pathways of these mediating effects are visualized in Fig. 4 through a heatmap and network diagram.

The robustness of the primary findings was validated through a series of sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S10). First, in a competing risk model that accounted for other comorbidity patterns (P1P2, P1C, and P2C), the associations of both hearing impairment (HR = 1.26) and vision impairment (HR = 1.27) with triple comorbidity remained significant. Second, after controlling for baseline confounders via PSM, the risk associations persisted for both hearing impairment (HR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.11–1.76) and vision impairment (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.03–1.65). Finally, the calculated E-value of 1.84 for the hearing impairment association indicated that a substantial unmeasured confounder would be required to nullify the observed effect, further corroborating the finding’s robustness.

Discussion

This study, leveraging data from three large-scale prospective cohorts across China, the US, and the UK, provides longitudinal evidence that sensory impairments are independently associated with incident PPC-M in middle-aged and older adults. A clear dose-response relationship was observed: the risk of multimorbidity increased from single sensory impairment (vision or hearing) to DSI when compared to no impairment. For instance, DSI was associated with a substantially higher risk of developing P1P2C (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.19–2.03). Notably, our findings suggest that loneliness may partly account for this association, explaining approximately 10–12% of the observed association between sensory impairments and multimorbidity. The consistency of these findings across three culturally and economically diverse populations suggests that the observed associations may be relevant across different social and health-system contexts.

A primary contribution of this study is that it positions sensory impairment as a potentially important upstream marker of complex health trajectories, extending prior work that examined single endpoints such as dementia or depression [9, 19]. This perspective is consistent with models proposing that age-related sensory decline may reflect accelerated biological ageing or systemic vulnerability [20]. Potential explanations include increased cognitive load, altered brain function, reduced environmental engagement, and impaired detection or self-management of physical health problems [21–23].

The stronger associations observed for DSI may reflect the compounded burden of simultaneous vision and hearing limitations. DSI can restrict environmental engagement and social communication through two sensory channels, reduce compensatory capacity, and may also indicate greater systemic vulnerability or shared underlying pathology [24–26].

Our finding that loneliness statistically accounted for part of the association between sensory impairment and multimorbidity provides insight into a potential psychosocial explanatory pathway. This finding is consistent with social isolation theories of sensory loss and with models that describe loneliness as a stress-related psychosocial state [13, 27]. Several biological and behavioral processes may help explain this association. Loneliness has been linked to hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activation, sympathetic nervous system activity, inflammation, immune dysregulation, and cellular ageing [28]. It is also associated with health-related behaviours such as physical inactivity, poor sleep quality, and lower healthcare engagement [29]. These pathways may help explain why loneliness could accompany the development of more complex physical, psychological, and cognitive health states among individuals with sensory impairment.

The counterintuitive finding that vision impairment and DSI were associated with a reduced risk of P1C warrants cautious interpretation, and we propose several potential, non-mutually exclusive explanations. First, survival bias is the most plausible explanation. Individuals with severe sensory impairments, particularly when coupled with pre-existing somatic conditions, may experience higher mortality rates. This selective mortality means a subset of the at-risk population may not survive long enough to develop a subsequent cognitive impairment, creating a statistical artifact that appears as a protective effect [30]. Second, detection bias is another likely contributor. Standard cognitive assessments are heavily reliant on visual and auditory function. Consequently, these instruments may systematically underestimate the cognitive abilities of individuals with sensory impairments, leading to their misclassification [31]. While we attempted to mitigate this with age-stratified normative data, residual bias may persist.

Finally, a more speculative possibility involves compensatory mechanisms. It is conceivable that individuals adapting to sensory loss develop alternative cognitive strategies that confer a degree of resilience against cognitive decline. Alternatively, their manifest impairments might lead to more intensive medical surveillance and care, which could, in turn, delay the onset of cognitive decline relative to their physical health trajectory [32].

This study has several strengths. First, the use of three large, nationally representative cohorts from China, the US, and England enhances the cross-national relevance of the findings. Second, the prospective design with 6–8 years of follow-up supports temporal ordering between baseline sensory impairments and subsequent PPC-M outcomes. Third, the combination of Cox models, multi-state models, mediation analyses, and sensitivity analyses allowed us to examine both incident PPC-M and state transitions from complementary perspectives.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our reliance on self-reported sensory function, as opposed to objective, gold-standard clinical assessments, may have introduced measurement error. This non-differential misclassification would likely bias our estimates towards the null, potentially underestimating the true magnitude of the associations. Similarly, the use of self-reports or screening scales for assessing chronic conditions, depression, and loneliness could be susceptible to reporting bias. Second, although covariates were selected to capture key demographic, socioeconomic, social, and behavioural confounders that were harmonized across cohorts, residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors, such as healthcare access, wealth, frailty, physical activity, environmental exposures, or other socioeconomic resources, cannot be eliminated. These limitations preclude causal interpretation, and the mediation findings for loneliness should be interpreted as supporting a potential explanatory pathway rather than confirming a biological or behavioral mechanism, given their sensitivity to unmeasured confounding and modeling assumptions. Finally, loss to follow-up is an inherent challenge in longitudinal studies and introduces a potential for attrition bias. If participants who dropped out were systematically different from those who remained (e.g., having a higher burden of frailty), the generalizability of our findings to the most vulnerable populations might be constrained.

Our findings have significant implications for public health and clinical practice. The association between sensory impairment and complex multimorbidity suggests that early detection and intervention may help identify older adults at elevated risk and inform strategies to reduce downstream health burden. Comprehensive screening programs should be implemented in primary care settings, integrating routine vision and hearing evaluations into standard geriatric assessments rather than treating sensory health in isolation. Intervention strategies should concurrently address both the direct effects of sensory loss and the psychosocial pathways involved. Given the observed mediating role of loneliness, combining sensory rehabilitation with social support initiatives may be particularly relevant. At the policy level, improving access to sensory assistive devices should be a priority. Despite robust evidence of their benefit, the uptake of hearing aids remains below 30% in most countries, largely due to cost [33].

These findings open several avenues for future research. First, prospective studies using objective sensory assessments are needed to confirm our results and to establish clinical thresholds for intervention. Second, mechanistic research should investigate other potential mediators beyond loneliness. These could include behavioral pathways, such as physical inactivity and reduced cognitive engagement, or biological markers like chronic inflammation, cortisol dysregulation, and altered brain connectivity. Third, randomized controlled trials are required to determine whether correcting sensory impairments can prevent or delay the onset of multimorbidity. The ongoing ACHIEVE trial, which tests the effect of a hearing intervention on cognitive decline, serves as a valuable model for such research [34].

Conclusions

In this large, cross-national cohort study, dual vision–hearing impairment was robustly associated with incident physical–psychological–cognitive multimorbidity. Loneliness consistently accounted for a meaningful proportion of these associations, supporting a plausible psychosocial pathway. These findings suggest that integrating early sensory screening and rehabilitation with loneliness-reduction initiatives may help identify older adults at elevated risk of complex multimorbidity and inform strategies to support healthy aging across diverse settings.
