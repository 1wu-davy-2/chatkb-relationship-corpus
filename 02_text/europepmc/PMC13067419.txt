ABSTRACT
Satisfaction experienced in romantic relationships is a significant variable that can influence individuals’ physical and psychological well-being. Most studies examining relationship satisfaction have employed cross-sectional designs. The lack of longitudinal studies addressing this variable, which can have long-term effects on various aspects of individuals’ lives, represents a notable gap in the literature. This study aims to examine the longitudinal relationship between relationship satisfaction, psychological inflexibility, and subjective vitality. Conducted with 268 Turkish participants, the study investigated the longitudinal mediating role of psychological inflexibility in the link between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality. A two-wave cross-lagged panel design was utilized for this analysis. Gender was included as a covariance variable in the model. The findings revealed that psychological inflexibility longitudinally mediated the association between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality. The direct cross-lagged path from relationship satisfaction at T1 to subjective vitality at T2 was not statistically significant (β = 0.004, p > .05; 95% CI [-0.065, 0.014]). Specifically, relationship satisfaction at T1 negatively predicted psychological inflexibility at T2 (β = −0.15, p < .01), and psychological inflexibility at T1 negatively predicted subjective vitality at T2 (β = −0.12, p < .05), supporting the proposed longitudinal mediation. The autoregressive paths indicated substantial stability over time for relationship satisfaction (β = 0.86, p < .01), psychological inflexibility (β = 0.65, p < .01), and subjective vitality (β = 0.63, p < .01). These results contribute to the literature at a longitudinal level by expanding our understanding of the psychological processes involved in romantic relationships. Nonetheless, the findings should be interpreted within the boundaries of the study’s cultural context and convenience sampling strategy, given that the data were obtained from a Turkish sample using a two-wave longitudinal design.

BODY
Introduction

Vitality, which originates from Self-Determination Theory [1], is defined as energy that emanates from the self [2]. While Nix et al. explain this energy as a positive emotion [3], Greenglass describes it as a process related to self-actualization [4]. The common point in the concept of vitality, which is discussed in different cultures such as China and Japan, is the emphasis on the individual’s internal energy [2]. Although the concept of vitality expresses a general life energy, how an individual experiences and perceives this energy can be explained by the concept of subjective vitality. In the literature, subjective vitality is explained as the dynamic reflection of well-being [5] and is defined as a state in which individuals feel alive and energetic [6]. Fini et al. also emphasize that individuals’ being alert, energetic, lively, and vigorous in life is related to subjective vitality [7]. Many different studies support this emphasis [8, 9]. In all these studies, it has been reported that people with high levels of subjective vitality are more lively and energetic in life, both emotionally and physically. On the other hand, subjective vitality is not limited to individuals’ internal resources; it is also influenced by environmental factors such as interpersonal relationships [2]. In this context, the quality of relationships established with the social environment and the satisfaction derived from these relationships may be among the determinants of subjective vitality. In particular, satisfaction from romantic relationships can increase the level of subjective vitality, as it makes individuals feel safe, valued, and emotionally supported. There are findings in the literature that satisfying romantic relationships support well-being and help individuals feel more energetic and lively [10–12]. In the studies in question, it is stated that the relationship satisfaction experienced by individuals strengthens mental health and makes the person more vital. In this regard, it can be said that to understand subjective vitality in a more holistic way, the experiences of individuals in the context of romantic relationships should also be taken into account.

Relationship satisfaction is defined as the general level of satisfaction of individuals in their close relationships [13]. Orenstein and Lewis explain relationship satisfaction in terms of individuals’ ability to establish healthy boundaries and privacy [14]. According to Bodenmann et al. relationship satisfaction increases as the difference between individuals’ expectations of the relationship and the reality they experience decreases [15]. Additionally, individuals who can easily cope with negative emotions in their romantic relationships can get more satisfaction from the relationship [16]. On the other hand, relationship satisfaction also has important effects on the general mental health of individuals. Research demonstrates that high relationship satisfaction reduces levels of depression, anxiety, and stress and increases positive mental health indicators such as life satisfaction and psychological well-being [17, 18]. According to Mónaco et al. romantic relationship satisfaction has a direct influence on the well-being of individuals [12]. In this context, satisfaction experienced in romantic relationships supports not only the individual’s relational satisfaction but also their overall quality of life and mental health. Based on this, relationship satisfaction may also have an impact on subjective vitality. Different variables may play a role in this relationship. High relationship satisfaction may support psychological flexibility by making it easier for individuals to be more adaptable to emotional fluctuations and develop constructive responses. Such flexibility may enable individuals to experience less emotional exhaustion in the face of stressful life events, maintain positive emotions, and increase their overall satisfaction with life. Accordingly, psychological flexibility may function as a key underlying mechanism that enhances the influence of relationship satisfaction on individuals’ levels of subjective vitality. In contrast, psychological inflexibility can lead individuals to remain rigid and reactive to emotional fluctuations, weakening their ability to cope with stressful life events. This can lead to the maintenance of negative emotions, especially those that occur in romantic relationships, and thus reduce individuals’ overall subjective vitality.

Psychological inflexibility as a longitudinal mediator

Psychological flexibility, which has different definitions in the literature, is explained as the capacity of individuals to recognize the present time and exhibit behaviors compatible with their values ​​ [19]. Psychological flexibility is related to individuals’ ability to recognize their emotions, thoughts and bodily sensations, to remain open to these internal experiences and to continue their behaviors in accordance with their own values ​​ [20]. Kashdan and Rottenberg emphasize the importance of managing emotions, thoughts, and values ​​in exhibiting adaptive behavior [21]. Gloster et al. state that staying in the “now” and taking actions consistent with values ​​are closely related to psychological flexibility [22]. Psychologically flexible individuals can respond functionally to difficulties encountered in relationships despite negative emotions, which can support both relationship satisfaction and subjective well-being [23–25]. As a matter of fact, many studies have shown that the emotional, behavioral, and cognitive components of psychological flexibility have strong relationships with mental health [26–28]. In particular, the effects of individuals’ psychological flexibility levels on variables such as subjective vitality, happiness and psychological well-being are attracting increasing attention both at theoretical and empirical levels [29–32]. Although some of this literature has examined vitality-related constructs in academic or organizational contexts, these studies converge on a common theoretical assumption that psychological flexibility facilitates sustained psychological energy, engagement, and adaptive functioning by reducing internal struggle and promoting value-consistent action. From an Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)-based perspective, subjective vitality can be understood as an experiential indicator of psychological energy that emerges when individuals act in alignment with their values rather than expending energy on experiential avoidance or cognitive control. Within the ACT framework, psychological flexibility is conceptualized as a core process that enables individuals to remain engaged in value-consistent actions even under emotionally challenging interpersonal conditions [19, 33]. Empirical ACT-oriented studies have shown that higher psychological flexibility is associated with greater vitality, energy, and life engagement, whereas psychological inflexibility is linked to emotional exhaustion, reduced vitality, and diminished psychological energy due to persistent avoidance and internal conflict [26, 28, 31]. From this perspective, romantic relationships constitute a salient contextual domain in which ongoing emotional demands, unmet relational needs, or chronic dissatisfaction may progressively challenge individuals’ capacity for openness, present-moment awareness, and value-based action. All these studies reveal that psychological inflexibility may have a mediating role in the relationship between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality.

Psychological inflexibility, defined as the opposite of psychological flexibility [34], has been shown to be holistically related to both relational satisfaction and individual well-being indicators. Psychological inflexibility is defined as individuals’ tendency to respond to internal experiences in an avoidant, controlling, and value-incompatible manner [35]. This situation can make it difficult for individuals to adapt to stressful life events by limiting their flexibility at both emotional and behavioral levels. In fact, having psychological inflexibility may be an indicator of the inability to establish empathic and open communication in relationships and low relationship satisfaction [33]. Additionally, individuals with a rigid psychological structure are expected to have difficulty developing positive emotions and become more vulnerable to the negativities they experience [28]. This can negatively influence the subjective vitality of individuals. ACT theory suggests that persistently unsatisfying relational experiences may foster maladaptive regulation strategies such as experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion, which are central components of psychological inflexibility [19, 33]. Over time, reliance on these inflexible strategies may drain individuals’ psychological energy by increasing internal struggle and reducing engagement in meaningful, value-driven behaviors, thereby undermining subjective vitality. Therefore, psychological inflexibility can pave the way for the emergence of unhealthy patterns not only at the individual level but also at the level of interpersonal relationships.

The present study

This study aims to examine the mediating role of psychological inflexibility in the longitudinal relationship between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality. The research makes an original contribution to the literature by examining the effects of individuals’ rigid and maladaptive responses to emotional and cognitive challenges encountered in romantic relationships, especially on subjective vitality over time. Most previous studies have examined the negative effects of psychological inflexibility on subjective well-being or relationship satisfaction using cross-sectional designs [27, 36, 37]. What sets the current study apart is its use of a longitudinal model in which psychological inflexibility is positioned as a mediating mechanism. Thus, it is empirically tested whether psychological inflexibility functions as a mediating mechanism in explaining why subjective vitality levels may remain low in some individuals despite high relationship satisfaction. In other words, this research seeks to answer the question of whether a high level of psychological inflexibility may weaken the positive effect of relationship satisfaction on subjective vitality. Importantly, this investigation is conducted within the Turkish cultural context, which is characterized by relatively strong relational interdependence, family-centered values, and a greater emphasis on harmony and relational obligations [38, 39]. Within such cultural settings, relationship satisfaction may be more closely tied to social expectations and role-based responsibilities, potentially increasing the emotional and cognitive demands placed on individuals in intimate relationships. The results of the study may reveal the long-term effects of the psychological inflexibility construct, theoretically defined within the framework of ACT, on individual well-being. The ACT provides a process-based framework that explains how individuals’ responses to internal experiences—rather than the experiences themselves—shape long-term well-being outcomes. Within this framework, psychological inflexibility represents a core mechanism through which relational stressors may undermine subjective vitality by restricting value-consistent action and increasing experiential avoidance over time. From an ACT perspective, culturally reinforced expectations regarding emotional restraint, relational sacrifice, and maintaining harmony may increase reliance on inflexible coping strategies, such as experiential avoidance or cognitive fusion, thereby intensifying the impact of psychological inflexibility on subjective vitality over time. Furthermore, the findings may shed light on internal barriers that mental health practitioners should be aware of in relationship-based psychological interventions. Additionally, this study may contribute to the literature by providing a clearer understanding of this process through a longitudinal model of how psychological inflexibility may limit the subjective vitality gains that individuals can derive from relationship satisfaction. Accordingly, ACT serves as the guiding theoretical framework for the present research by offering a coherent explanation of the hypothesized mediating pathways examined in the research questions. In this context, in line with the main purpose of the study, the following research questions (RQ) are sought to be answered:RQ1. Does relationship satisfaction predict psychological inflexibility longitudinally?RQ2. Does psychological inflexibility predict subjective vitality longitudinally?RQ3. Is there a longitudinal mediating effect of psychological inflexibility on the association between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality?

Method

Participant and procedure

The present study used a two-wave cross-lagged panel design, consisting of two waves of data collection, with assessments completed at three-month intervals. The research used convenience sampling, a method that involves choosing individuals who can be reached easily in order to include them in the study. This sampling approach was deemed appropriate given the longitudinal nature of the study, which required repeated participation over time from individuals currently involved in ongoing romantic relationships, a condition that can limit accessibility and participant retention in probability-based sampling methods. The research data was collected via an online form. This form was also used to get the consent of the study participants. The participants in the study granted informed permission with the choice to quit at any time throughout the survey. To enhance data quality in the online data collection process, responses were screened for completeness and consistency, and questionnaires with substantial missing data or implausibly short completion times were excluded from the analyses. Participants have heterosexual romantic relationships. A total of 300 participants were reached at Time 1 and 270 at Time 2. In the data obtained at Time 1 and Time 2, there were 268 participants who were matched to each other. Out of these, 75.7% were female and 24.3% were male. The average age of the participants was 36 years, with a standard deviation of 7.6. The average length of their relationship was 11 years, with a standard deviation of 7.59. In terms of the participants, 56 participants (20.9%) completed high school, 175 participants (65.3%) had a bachelor’s degree, and 37 participants (13.8%) had a graduate degree.

Measures

The Subjective Vitality Scale

The scale is used to measure the subjective vitality of the participants. This measure applies a seven-point Likert scale to evaluate each of the seven items [2]. Akın et al. conducted the adaptation of the scale for a Turkish sample [40]. The construct validity of the Turkish form model was well fit, and its reliability coefficient was satisfactory (α = 0.84).

The acceptance and action questionnaire-II (AAQ-II)

The scale is a self-assessment tool created by Bond et al. to measure degrees of psychological inflexibility [20]. Greater scores obtained from the scale indicate elevated levels of psychological inflexibility, thereby leading to an increase in experience avoidance. Yavuz et al. conducted the adaptation of the scale for a Turkish clinical and non-clinical sample that included 474 individuals [41]. The findings of our study showed that the Turkish version of the AAQ-II had good validity. The Turkish AAQ-II had strong internal consistency, as shown by a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.84.

Relationship Assessment Scale

The scale is used to measure the relationship satisfaction of the participants. This measure applies a seven-point Likert scale to evaluate each of the seven items [42]. Curun conducted the adaptation of the scale for a Turkish sample [43]. The factor analysis revealed a single component with an eigenvalue greater than 1, which accounted for 52% of the variance, consistent with the original version, and the internal consistency coefficient was found to be 0.86.

Data analysis

The objective of the present research was to investigate how psychological inflexibility predicts the long-term relationship between relationship satisfactions and subjective vitality. During the initial analyses, correlations, and descriptive statistics among all study variables were calculated. Subsequently, AMOS 24 software was used to perform autoregressive analysis of a cross-lagged panel model with a two-wave cross-lagged panel design, as described by Cole and Maxwell [44] and Preacher [45]. Given the 10% attrition rate between Time 1 and Time 2, missing data were handled using maximum likelihood estimation in AMOS. This approach uses all available data under the assumption of missing at random and is recommended for longitudinal structural equation modeling to reduce potential bias associated with attrition. This approach examines if changes in the independent variable at time 1 may predict changes in the mediator at time 2, and if changes in the mediator variable at time 1 can predict changes in the dependent variable at time 2. Gender was included in the model as a covariate to control for potential individual differences that may systematically influence relationship satisfaction, psychological inflexibility, and subjective vitality. Controlling for gender allowed the model to more accurately estimate the longitudinal associations among the focal variables by reducing unexplained variance attributable to demographic factors. Age and relationship length were examined as potential covariates; however, as they were not significantly associated with the primary study variables, they were not included in the final structural model in order to preserve model parsimony. The adequacy of the model was assessed by evaluating several fit indices, including χ2/degrees of freedom, the goodness of fit (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), normed fit index (NFI), incremental fit index (IFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). The model is deemed satisfactory if the GFI, CFI, NFI, and IFI exceed 0.90; RMSEA and SRMR are below 0.08 [46]. In addition, the significance of the indirect effects was tested using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated.

Two-wave cross-lagged panel mediation model

The research used a two-wave cross-lagged panel design with autoregressive analysis in a cross-lagged panel model to investigate the mediating role of psychological inflexibility in the association between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality. Initial findings indicated that the path from relationship satisfaction T1 to subjective vitality T2 was not statistically significant (β = 0.004, p > .05; 95% CI [-0.065, 0.014]). Additionally, although many of the fit index values ​​were within acceptable limits, the RMSEA value was determined to be 0.09. Therefore, the analysis was repeated by removing the path from relationship satisfaction T1 to subjective vitality T2. The analytical findings suggested that the fit indices were within adequate fit indices, χ2/df = 3.01; GFI = 0.912, CFI = 0.956, NFI = 0.936, IFI = 0.956, RMSEA = 0.08, and SRMR = 0.056. In the depicted model (Fig. 1), the autoregressive paths revealed substantial temporal stability for all study variables. Relationship satisfaction at Time 1 significantly predicted relationship satisfaction at Time 2 (β = 0.86, p < .01), psychological inflexibility at Time 1 significantly predicted psychological inflexibility at Time 2 (β = 0.65, p < .01), and subjective vitality at Time 1 significantly predicted subjective vitality at Time 2 (β = 0.63, p < .01). Regarding the cross-lagged effects, relationship satisfaction at Time 1 significantly and negatively predicted psychological inflexibility at Time 2, even after controlling for psychological inflexibility at Time 1 (β = −0.15, p < .01). In addition, psychological inflexibility at Time 1 significantly and negatively predicted subjective vitality at Time 2, after controlling for subjective vitality at Time 1 (β = −0.12, p < .05). Taken together, these findings indicate that psychological inflexibility functions as an intervening process linking earlier relationship satisfaction to later subjective vitality within a two-wave cross-lagged panel mediation framework. However, given the two-wave design, the results support longitudinal mediation in a temporal and process-oriented sense rather than establishing full causal mediation. Discussion

Romantic relationships can have a considerable influence on individuals’ overall life satisfaction, as well as their physiological and psychological well-being. High levels of relationship satisfaction may enhance individuals’ subjective vitality by increasing their enjoyment of life and their perceived energy levels. In this context, findings from previous studies have documented the link between relationship satisfaction and subjective well-being [12, 47]. However, the majority of these studies have employed cross-sectional designs, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the relationships among these variables. In particular, psychological inflexibility—the opposite of psychological flexibility—is a cognitive and emotional process that may influence both relationship satisfaction and individuals’ feelings of energy and vitality. Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the longitudinal mediating role of psychological inflexibility in the relationship between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality. The results of the mediation analysis, based on two-wave longitudinal data, are presented in detail, and the temporal role of psychological inflexibility within this relationship has been evaluated. In this regard, the study offers a more nuanced contribution to the understanding of psychological processes in romantic relationships and provides insights into the mechanisms that influence individual well-being. Although relationship length is often considered an important variable in romantic relationship research, it was not associated with the focal constructs in the present sample. The findings related to the study’s research questions are discussed and interpreted in detail below.

The first finding of the study indicates that relationship satisfaction negatively predicts psychological inflexibility over time. This finding provides an answer to the first research question (RQ1). It demonstrates that individuals who are satisfied with their romantic relationships tend to experience lower levels of psychological inflexibility over time. Similar results have also been reported in previous studies that examined these constructs. For instance, Ben-Itzhak et al. discussed the link between psychological flexibility and the functionality of relationships characterized by negative emotions [23]. Likewise, Ercan and Çelik reported a significant negative association between these two variables [36]. In a more recent study by Klein et al. [24], comparable findings were observed, indicating that relationship satisfaction can influence individuals’ levels of psychological inflexibility. From the perspective of the ACT, this finding suggests that satisfying romantic relationships may function as a supportive interpersonal context that facilitates openness to internal experiences and engagement in value-consistent behaviors. ACT posits that psychologically flexible functioning is fostered in environments where individuals experience emotional safety and reduced need for experiential avoidance. Accordingly, higher relationship satisfaction may buffer against the development of inflexible coping strategies, such as avoidance or cognitive fusion, by reducing chronic relational stress over time. Based on these findings, the level of satisfaction derived from romantic relationships may be considered an important factor that can shape individuals’ psychological flexibility capacity.

The finding obtained in relation to the second research question indicates that psychological inflexibility negatively predicts subjective vitality longitudinally (RQ2). This result suggests that as individuals’ levels of psychological inflexibility increase, their physical and psychological energy and vitality tend to decrease over time. Supporting evidence for this finding has been reported in various studies in the literature [29, 30, 32]. All of these studies have identified a significant relationship between psychological flexibility and subjective vitality. A similar result was also emphasized in the recent longitudinal study by Kütük et al. [31], which demonstrated that individuals’ psychological flexibility has a significant influence on their level of subjective vitality. From an ACT perspective, this finding can be interpreted as evidence that persistent experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion—core components of psychological inflexibility—may gradually deplete individuals’ psychological energy by increasing internal struggle and reducing engagement in value-consistent activities. ACT posits that subjective vitality emerges when individuals allocate their psychological resources toward meaningful action rather than toward controlling or avoiding internal experiences. Accordingly, these findings indicate that psychological inflexibility can adversely impact individuals’ physical and mental energy over time. Therefore, cognitive flexibility and emotional adaptability appear to play a crucial role in maintaining subjective vitality. Within the ACT framework, this underscores the importance of flexibility-based processes as central mechanisms linking psychological functioning to sustained vitality and well-being. This supports the view that psychological inflexibility impacts not only mental but also physiological well-being.

The third and final finding of the study indicates that psychological inflexibility plays a longitudinal mediating role in the relationship between relationship satisfaction and subjective vitality. This finding responds to the third research question (RQ3). It reveals that the effect of relationship satisfaction on individuals’ level of subjective vitality occurs indirectly over time through psychological inflexibility, rather than directly. This pattern suggests that relationship satisfaction does not function as a direct source of psychological energy; rather, it operates by shaping underlying self-regulatory and experiential processes that either preserve or deplete vitality. This result underscores the importance of addressing relational and individual well-being from a holistic perspective. To date, no studies in the literature have examined these three variables together. In this respect, the current study fills a gap in the literature and offers a unique perspective on the long-term psychological effects of individuals’ romantic relationships. Furthermore, these findings provide valuable insights into how subjective vitality, as a component of life satisfaction, can be shaped by relational factors and individual psychological processes.

According to Diener’s model of life satisfaction, interpersonal relationships are among the key determinants of individuals’ overall life satisfaction [48]. Accordingly, it is expected that relationship satisfaction may influence individuals’ sense of energy and enjoyment in life through indirect pathways. However, the absence of a direct effect in the present study suggests that feeling satisfied in a relationship alone may not be sufficient to enhance vitality unless it alters how individuals manage internal experiences, stressors, and emotional demands associated with close relationships. In addition, from the perspective of Ryan and Deci’s Self-Determination Theory (SDT) [1], relationship satisfaction meets the basic psychological need for relatedness, and when this need is fulfilled, individuals tend to feel more flexible, more vital, and psychologically well. However, SDT also emphasizes that when basic psychological needs—particularly autonomy and relatedness—are frustrated, individuals are more likely to experience controlled forms of self-regulation and diminished vitality. From this standpoint, vitality emerges not simply from positive relational evaluations, but from the absence of need frustration processes that drain psychological energy.

From the perspective of the ACT, the present mediation finding suggests that relationship satisfaction shapes subjective vitality by influencing how individuals relate to their internal experiences over time. ACT conceptualizes psychological inflexibility as a process through which contextual stressors—such as chronic relational dissatisfaction—promote experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion, thereby weakening self-regulation and limiting engagement in value-consistent action. In this sense, psychological inflexibility represents an energy-depleting process rather than a neutral psychological state, as ongoing avoidance and rigid self-control require sustained cognitive and emotional effort. Integrating ACT with SDT, psychological inflexibility can be understood as a process-level manifestation of basic psychological need frustration, whereby unmet needs for autonomy and relatedness foster rigid coping patterns that undermine vitality. Within this framework, satisfying romantic relationships may reduce reliance on inflexible coping strategies, allowing psychological energy to be directed toward meaningful activities, which in turn enhances subjective vitality. Thus, the absence of a direct effect suggests that relationship satisfaction does not “energize” individuals per se; instead, it contributes to vitality by preventing the depletion of psychological energy through reduced inflexibility and improved self-regulation. In this context, the effect of relationship satisfaction on psychological inflexibility is closely related to the individual’s self-regulation capacity, which in turn plays a crucial role in subjective vitality. Thus, psychological inflexibility represents a critical bridge between relational need satisfaction and sustained psychological vitality, aligning with ACT’s emphasis on mediating psychological processes rather than surface-level symptoms. This integrative perspective holds important implications for both clinical practice and future research.

Implications

The findings of this study offer significant implications by presenting a longitudinal model that explains the impact of relationship satisfaction in romantic relationships on individuals’ subjective vitality. The main result of the study demonstrated that the effect of relationship satisfaction on individuals’ subjective vitality occurs not directly but indirectly over time through psychological inflexibility. This finding suggests that the satisfaction individuals experience in their romantic relationships gradually shapes their subjective vitality by influencing their level of psychological flexibility. This outcome underscores the need to address both individual and relational well-being from an integrated perspective.

The findings of the research provide meaningful implications not only for academic researchers but also for mental health practitioners and policymakers. From the perspective of mental health practitioners, the results indicate that in addition to enhancing clients’ relational satisfaction, interventions aimed at increasing psychological flexibility may have sustained effects on subjective vitality. In this context, therapeutic approaches that target psychological flexibility—particularly ACT [19]—can be considered promising interventions for supporting both individual and relational well-being. For researchers, the results reveal the need for further longitudinal studies that examine the long-term psychological mechanisms influenced by relationship satisfaction. By integrating the variables of relationship satisfaction, psychological inflexibility, and subjective vitality within a longitudinal framework for the first time, the present study makes a valuable contribution to this research gap. Policymakers may also benefit from these findings by designing community-based programs aimed at enhancing subjective vitality, with a focus on both individuals’ relational lives and their levels of psychological flexibility. In this way, promoting healthy romantic relationships may contribute to improving life satisfaction and psychological well-being at the societal level.

Limitations and future research

Despite the important implications of this research, some limitations should be emphasized. First, the data were collected using self-report scales. Although data collection was based on the principle of voluntariness, self-report measures may have been influenced by participants’ social desirability bias. This indicates the need for caution when interpreting the findings. In particular, the limitations of this method should be considered when evaluating the causal relationships between variables. Additionally, since the data were collected online, certain technical limitations may have affected data quality. Therefore, adopting alternative data collection strategies in future studies would be beneficial. The second limitation concerns the data collection intervals, which were conducted at three-month intervals. While this period is consistent with the requirements of the cross-lagged panel analysis employed in a two-wave cross-lagged panel design, longer follow-up studies are recommended to more robustly examine causal relationships between the variables. Moreover, although two-wave cross-lagged models offer stronger evidence than cross-sectional designs, they do not allow for definitive causal inferences, as alternative explanations and unmeasured third variables cannot be fully ruled out. In addition, although most fit indices indicated acceptable model fit, the RMSEA value (0.08) falls within the marginal fit range, suggesting that the model may not fully capture all underlying relationships among the variables. This should be considered when interpreting the structural findings. Although missing data were handled using maximum likelihood estimation under the missing at random assumption, individual-level dropout comparisons could not be conducted due to data management procedures. Future studies may benefit from directly testing attrition bias through group comparisons. Accordingly, future research would benefit from employing three-wave or multi-wave longitudinal designs to more clearly capture the dynamic and reciprocal relationships among relationship satisfaction, psychological inflexibility, and subjective vitality over time. Third, the study was conducted solely with a Turkish sample using a convenience sampling method. The use of convenience sampling restricts the representativeness of the sample and constitutes a notable limitation in terms of the external validity of the findings. Moreover, the substantial gender imbalance in the sample, with a predominance of female participants, may introduce systematic bias, as relationship satisfaction and psychological inflexibility may be experienced and expressed differently across genders. Although gender was included as a covariate in the analytical model to statistically control for its potential confounding effects, this procedure does not fully eliminate the possibility of gender-related bias. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution regarding gender-related generalizability. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to the cultural context in which the study was conducted. Future studies are therefore encouraged to recruit more gender-balanced samples and to examine gender as a potential moderating variable in the associations among the study variables. In particular, in Türkiye, family relationships and cultural values strongly influence individual decision-making processes. Therefore, the extent to which these findings can be generalized to other cultural contexts is limited. Beyond issues of generalizability, future research is encouraged to examine measurement invariance across different cultural groups to determine whether the strength and direction of the observed pathways differ across cultural contexts. In addition, incorporating diary methods or experience sampling designs may allow future studies to investigate how daily fluctuations in relationship satisfaction influence psychological flexibility and subjective vitality at a micro-process level. From this perspective, future cross-cultural studies with highly representative samples from different cultural backgrounds could provide valuable insights into the cross-cultural validity of the findings.
