ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBeing in a romantic relationship can be associated with positive health outcomes. However, little is known about how characteristics of these relationships influence health among older adults.MethodsAdults (aged 50+ years) from seven mid-Atlantic US states completed an online survey, which included measures of emotional, intellectual and sexual intimacy from the Personal Assessment of Intimacy in Relationships Inventory. We conducted logistic regression analyses of relationship intimacy scores and health behaviours (having a recent healthcare check-up, current smoking and recent binge drinking).ResultsOverall, 1774 participants were in an intimate partner relationship. Adjusting for demographic characteristics, all three relationship intimacy scores were associated with having a recent check-up (emotional: adjusted OR (aOR) 1.04, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.06; intellectual: aOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.07; sexual: aOR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.07). Intimacy scores were not associated with current smoking but were negatively associated with binge drinking (emotional: aOR 0.96, 95% CI 0.94 to 0.99; intellectual: aOR 0.95, 95% CI 0.92 to 0.98). Associations between intimacy and binge drinking were stronger for male than female participants.ConclusionsIntimacy in romantic relationships may positively impact health behaviours among adults aged 50+ years particularly for males. Improving relationship intimacy may benefit health particularly for older male adults.

BODY
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Romantic relationships can support better health behaviours, but the specific relationship characteristics associated with health outcomes are understudied.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Emotional, intellectual and sexual intimacy in a romantic relationship are associated with increased receipt of a recent preventive healthcare check-up and reduced odds of binge drinking, especially for male participants.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE, OR POLICY

Supporting intimacy in romantic relationships may reap health benefits, particularly for male older adults.

Chronic diseases contribute to substantial population-level morbidity and mortality, but primary and secondary prevention can prevent many of these diseases.13 These protective health behaviours include limiting tobacco and alcohol consumption and receipt of routine healthcare services. For example, up to 40% of cancer deaths could be avoided through behaviour change and cancer screening.4 However, many people do not meet recommendations for protective health behaviours. For example, as of 2022, 14.6% of people in the USA smoked cigarettes and 21.7% engaged in binge drinking in the last month.5 Among older adults (aged 60+ years), these estimates were 12.4% for smoking cigarettes and 12.8% for engaging in binge drinking.6

To address these gaps in chronic disease prevention, research has increasingly examined the influence of factors outside of the individual, including interpersonal factors. Having close social relationships (for example, friendships, romantic relationships) is often associated with improved health outcomes, such as better physiological functioning, lower incidence of disease and lower risk of mortality.79 One possible mechanism explaining this association is that members of a social relationship may encourage protective health behaviours either explicitly or through modelling.1012 Another mechanism is that relationships facilitate dyadic coping, which in turn can buffer non-relationship stressors and reduce the use of risky coping behaviours.1315

Romantic relationships (including married and non-married relationships) are among the most enduring relationships in a person’s life, with potential implications for health and well-being across the lifespan.16 17 To date, much of the research on romantic relationships and health has focused on heterosexual marriages, and some of these studies have demonstrated that men accrue greater health benefits from marriage than do women.18 However, little is known about how characteristics of these relationships impact health.19 One such characteristic is relationship intimacy, or feelings of closeness and shared experiences between members of a romantic relationship.20 Conceptually, intimacy is a dynamic characteristic of a romantic (or friendship) relationship, borne out of shared experiences over time. Intimacy can be observed across several dimensions (including emotional, intellectual and sexual), which are correlated but independent from each other. Although people may differ in how much intimacy they desire in their relationships (or how many relationships to have), intimacy in at least one relationship is typical for adult development.20 Relationship intimacy could support the hypothesised mechanisms linking relationships with health (ie, encouraging healthy behaviours and dyadic coping), but data to evaluate this association are sparse. Addressing this gap in the research on relationship intimacy could identify characteristics of romantic relationships that are particularly influential for health promotion and chronic disease prevention.

This study uses a cross-sectional survey of older adults (aged 50+ years) in seven mid-Atlantic states to assess the association between relationship intimacy and health behaviours. We hypothesise that, among older adults in a romantic relationship, greater relationship intimacy will be associated with more positive health behaviour outcomes and that this association will be stronger for men than for women.

Materials and methods

Data source

Survey data were collected from February to August 2021 through the Qualtrics research panel. Individuals were eligible for participation if they were aged 50 years or older and living in Delaware, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania or West Virginia. Of the 2966 participants who completed the survey, our analytic sample included only participants who indicated they were dating someone, living with a partner or married (n=1774, or 60.9% of the overall sample). Among these participants, age ranged from 50 years to 94 years, with a mean of 65.6 years (SE=0.19).

Measures

We collected demographic and health characteristics that have established relationships with our outcomes of interest: sex, age, race/ethnicity, education level, annual household income, rurality, health insurance type and Charlson Comorbidity Index17 score. In addition, we included other demographic variables that could be related to relationship intimacy: marital status (married or non-married), sexual orientation (straight or lesbian, gay or bisexual (LGB)) and monogamy (monogamous or not monogamous).

Relationship intimacy was assessed using the Personal Assessment of Intimacy in Relationships (PAIR) Inventory created by Schaefer and Olson.20 We measured three subscales of intimacy: (1) emotional intimacy, or closeness of feelings; (2) intellectual intimacy, or sharing ideas and (3) sexual intimacy, or sharing general affection and sexual activity.20 For brevity, the survey did not assess social intimacy (sharing mutual friends and social networks) or recreational intimacy (shared interest in hobbies). All subscales are considered independent of each other and can be collected separately.20 Each subscale is comprised of six items, and response options were on a five-point Likert scale from (1) does not describe me/my relationship at all to (5) describes me/my relationship very well. Example items include ‘My partner can really understand my hurts and joys’ (emotional intimacy), ‘My partner helps me clarify my thoughts’ (intellectual intimacy) and ‘I am satisfied with our sex life’ (sexual intimacy).18 Responses were summed to create scores for each intimacy subscale, with higher scores indicating greater intimacy (possible range: 6–30). Each intimacy subscale demonstrated high reliability, as assessed with Cronbach’s alpha (emotional intimacy: α=0.88; intellectual intimacy: α=0.80 and sexual intimacy: α=0.75). These internal reliability coefficients are comparable to the original PAIR study, which demonstrated Cronbach’s alpha of ≥0.70 across subscales.20 Further, the intimacy subscale scores were correlated with each other (emotional and intellectual intimacy: r=0.87; emotional and sexual intimacy: r=0.71; intellectual and sexual intimacy: r=0.67 (all p<0.001)).

Measures of health behaviour outcomes were drawn from population-based health surveillance surveys to create dichotomous indicators of whether participants (1) had a routine healthcare check-up in the last year,21 (2) were current smokers22 or (3) reported binge drinking in the past 30 days (defined as having four+ drinks (female participants) or five+ drinks (male participants) on an occasion).22

Statistical analysis

Descriptively, we generated frequencies and percentages for demographic and health characteristics of the analytic sample, both overall and stratified by sex. Then, we used χ2 tests to assess bivariate differences in these variables by sex.

Next, we generated the mean and SE of scores for each relationship intimacy subscale. We used bivariate t-tests to examine differences in each relationship intimacy subscale score by sex. Then, we used multivariable linear regression models to assess differences in intimacy subscale scores by participant demographic and health characteristics.

Then, we generated the prevalence of each health behaviour outcome. We used χ2 to assess differences in these outcomes by participant sex, and we used logistic regression models to estimate the association between each outcome and each intimacy subscale score. The final multivariable logistic regression models estimated the association between each health outcome and each intimacy subscale score, adjusting for participant demographic and health characteristics.

Finally, we conducted an exploratory analysis of sex differences in the associations between intimacy subscale scores and health behaviours. Separately for each outcome, we created models regressing the health behaviour on each intimacy subscale score, participant sex and their multiplicative interaction. We used Wald χ2 tests to evaluate the joint contribution of the interaction terms to the models. For models with evidence of statistical interaction (p<0.10), we re-estimated the association between intimacy score and the health behaviour outcome, stratified by participant sex.

Analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 (Cary, North Carolina, USA). The Penn State College of Medicine Institutional Review Board/Human Subjects Protection Office approved data collection and analysis for this project (protocol number 16024).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the development of the research study or the current analysis.

Results

Participant demographic characteristics

The majority of participants were older adults (aged 65+ years: 58.7%) and were non-Hispanic white (81.9%) (table 1). Less than half of the participants had a bachelor’s degree or higher (43.0%) and most participants had at least one comorbidity (56.7%). Most participants were married (91.0%), straight (97.1%) and monogamous (94.6%).

Demographic, health and relationship characteristics overall and by sex for adults aged 50+ years in a romantic relationship (n=1774)

Compared with males (n=1235, 42.4% of the sample), females (n=1676, 57.6% of the sample) were more likely to be in the younger age group, had lower education and income and were more likely to be rural and have no comorbidities (all p<0.05). Further, female participants were less likely to be LGB than were male participants (p=0.03).

Relationship intimacy scores

Emotional intimacy subscale scores

The mean score for the emotional intimacy subscale was 23.44 (SE=0.14). In bivariate analysis, male participants reported higher emotional intimacy (mean=23.84, SE=0.18) than female participants (mean=23.08, SE=0.20) (p<0.01). In multivariable analysis, emotional intimacy scores were higher for participants who were older, with higher annual household incomes and urban participants, compared with their reference groups (table 2). Further, emotional intimacy scores were higher for straight versus LGB participants and for monogamous versus non-monogamous participants.

Adj, adjusted; ref, reference.

Intellectual intimacy subscale scores

The mean score for the intellectual intimacy subscale was 22.60 (SE=0.13), which did not differ between male and female participants in bivariate analysis (p=0.14). In multivariable analysis, intellectual intimacy scores were higher for participants who were older, with higher annual household incomes and urban participants, compared with their reference groups, and scores were lower for participants of ‘Other’ race/ethnicity compared with non-Hispanic white participants (table 2). Further, intellectual intimacy scores were higher for straight versus LGB participants and for monogamous versus non-monogamous participants.

Sexual intimacy subscale scores

The mean score for the sexual intimacy subscale was 21.47 (SE=0.13). In bivariate analysis, male participants reported lower sexual intimacy (mean=20.89, SE=0.19) than female participants (mean=22.02, SE=0.17) (p<0.001). In multivariable analysis, sexual intimacy scores were higher for non-Hispanic black versus non-Hispanic white participants, and scores were lower for participants with a comorbidity index of 1+ compared with to 0 comorbidities (table 2). Further, sexual intimacy scores were higher for straight versus LGB participants and for monogamous versus non-monogamous participants.

Associations between relationship intimacy and health behaviours

Check-up in the last year

Overall, 80.6% of participants reported having a routine check-up within the last year, and this outcome did not vary by participant sex (p=0.32). When adjusted for demographic and health characteristics, higher relationship intimacy scores were associated with higher odds of having a check-up in the last year (emotional intimacy: adjusted OR (aOR) 1.04 per each three-unit change in intimacy (95% CI 1.01 to 1.06); intellectual intimacy: aOR 1.04 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.07) and sexual intimacy: aOR 1.04 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.07) (table 3; for complete model results, see online supplemental table S1).

The associations between relationship intimacy scores and having a check-up in the last year did not vary by participant sex (all interaction p value >0.10).

Current smoking

Overall, 12.2% of participants were current smokers, and this outcome did not vary by sex (p=0.70). When adjusted for demographic and health characteristics, none of the relationship intimacy subscale scores were associated with current smoking (table 3; for complete model results, see online supplemental table S1).

However, the interaction analyses revealed that the association between intellectual intimacy and current smoking varied by sex (p=0.06). Specifically, for male participants, higher intellectual intimacy scores were associated with lower odds of current smoking (aOR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.93), but for female participants, there was no association (figure 1). Binge drinking

Overall, 13.2% of participants reported binge drinking, and binge drinking was more common among male participants (16.3%) than female participants (10.3%) (p<0.001). When adjusted for demographic and health characteristics, higher relationship intimacy scores were associated with lower odds of binge drinking (emotional intimacy: aOR 0.96 (95% CI 0.94 to 0.99) and intellectual intimacy: aOR 0.95 (95% CI 0.92 to 0.98)) (table 3; for complete model results, see online supplemental table S1).

Discussion

In summary, among adults aged 50+ years who were in a romantic relationship, health behaviours were associated with select indicators of relationship intimacy. Specifically, emotional, intellectual and sexual intimacy were associated with higher odds of having a last-year healthcare check-up, and emotional and intellectual intimacy were associated with lower odds of last-month binge drinking, even after adjusting for participant demographic, health and relationship characteristics. Notably, several of these associations were only observed among male participants. Thus, relationship intimacy is associated with more favourable health behaviours among older adults, especially older males. Although the association between relationship/marital status and health outcomes is well established,2325 the role of relationship intimacy is relatively understudied, and our study addresses this research gap. These findings have implications for public health research and practice on how interpersonal factors (ie, intimacy in a romantic relationship) may impact chronic disease outcomes among middle-age and older-age adults.

The emotional and intellectual intimacy subscales were associated with having a recent check-up and abstaining from binge drinking. One mechanism that could explain these associations includes modelling of protective health behaviours, such that one partner observes and adopts the other partner’s behaviours.1012 Further, the influence of one partner’s behaviours on another partner’s behaviours can be stronger in the context of more intimate, closer relationships.2628 An alternative mechanism to explain these associations is dyadic coping, that is, when partners work together to manage stressful experiences.29 To the extent that dyadic coping reduces stress, individuals may be less inclined to use alcohol or other substances as a coping mechanism.1315 Further, these associations could be related to stress reduction and relationship satisfaction; that is, intimacy may bolster a person’s psychological resources that protect against substance use.30 Dyadic coping may specifically involve substance use agreements, or shared goals about limiting frequency, intensity or type of consumption, demonstrating that these agreements meaningfully impact health behaviours.31 Notably, the effect sizes for relationships observed in the current study are relatively small (for last-year healthcare check-up: aORs were 1.04 for each subscale; for last-month binge drinking, aORs were 0.95–0.96 for subscales with statistically significant relationships). However, these aORs were expressed in terms of a three-point change in intimacy subscale scores (possible range: 6–30), and the relationships were robust to controlling for a number of demographic and relationship characteristics. Additional research, including qualitative studies, are needed to parse the mechanisms linking relationship intimacy with health behaviours and clinical outcomes.

Of particular interest for this study were potential sex differences in relationship intimacy and associations with health behaviours. We found that male participants reported higher emotional intimacy than did female participants, male participants reported lower sexual intimacy and there was no difference by sex in intellectual intimacy. Sex differences in intimacy have been demonstrated previously.30 32 Females may experience lower levels of emotional intimacy because they attend to emotional changes in their relationships that may undermine their satisfaction and feelings of intimacy with their partner.32 In contrast, males may experience lower levels of sexual intimacy because they tend to be more dissatisfied with their sexual experiences.30 Furthermore, some of the associations between intimacy and health behaviours (ie, current smoking, last-month binge drinking) were apparent for male participants but not for female participants. These findings support existing findings that males may uniquely benefit from the presence and quality of social relationships.18

In addition, we examined associations between other demographic and relationship characteristics and relationship intimacy subscale scores. We demonstrated that older participants (aged 65+ years) had higher emotional and intellectual intimacy than younger participants (aged 50–65 years). This finding resonates with a recent meta-analysis, which demonstrated that relationship satisfaction plateaus at a high level after age 65 years.33 Higher income was also associated with higher emotional and intellectual intimacy, perhaps because lower-income couples experience more economic strain and less stability in their relationships.34 To our knowledge, our study is the first to demonstrate higher emotional and intellectual intimacy in urban compared with rural participants in a US setting. Future research should continue to investigate this finding, which could be related to rural/urban differences in social capital, gender norms and intimate partner violence.3537 Certain relationship characteristics (ie, sexual orientation and monogamy) were also associated with differences in emotional, intellectual and sexual intimacy; despite their salience to intimacy and the putative ‘detraditionalization’ of intimate partner relationships in recent decades,38 these characteristics are relatively understudied, particularly among middle-age and older-age adult populations, and additional research is needed. Thus, demographic and relationship characteristics may contextualise the associations between intimacy and health behaviours.

These findings have implications for public health research and practice. To date, the bulk of research on relationship intimacy has focused on psychological or mental health outcomes, but our findings demonstrate that intimacy is also associated with health behaviours. Previous interventions to improve relationship intimacy (or related constructs, such as quality and satisfaction) have often focused on limited samples, such as heterosexual married couples or patient samples (eg, recently-diagnosed cancer survivors and their partners).39 40 Our findings demonstrate that intimacy can contribute to better health among the general population of adults aged 50+ years. Males, in particular, could benefit from interventions to improve relationship intimacy and, subsequently, health behaviour outcomes. Such interventions could target intimacy itself or the conditions (eg, higher income) that may impact intimacy. These strategies could contribute to increased engagement in protective health behaviours (ie, accessing routine healthcare services and abstaining from binge drinking), with long-term benefits for reducing the burden of chronic disease among older adults in the US.

Strengths and limitations

In terms of study strengths, our analysis leveraged a relatively large sample of adults aged 50+ years, who are generally understudied in the context of relationship intimacy. Further, the survey used measures of relationship intimacy and health behaviours that were collated from high-quality and validated surveys. Additionally, the use of the PAIR inventory,20 which is primarily used in clinical settings, in this research study is an innovative approach.

In terms of study limitations, the sample was relatively homogeneous in terms of sociodemographic characteristics (eg, 81.9% of participants were non-Hispanic white) and relationship characteristics (eg, 94.6% of participants were monogamous); future studies should seek to recruit more diverse samples of adults in order to support more robust inferences about relationship intimacy and health. Further, our sample did not include participants’ partners, which would have allowed us to examine dyadic processes that could influence health behaviour outcomes. For brevity, the survey did not include all of the relationship intimacy subscales that are part of the PAIR inventory, and participants reported their current experiences of intimacy (ie, did not report their expectations or ideals about intimacy), which is an area for future research. Lastly, the survey did not assess relationship duration, which is likely important for relationship intimacy scores.

Conclusions

In summary, we examined the associations between relationship intimacy and health behaviours among adults aged 50+ years in the mid-Atlantic region of the USA. Our findings demonstrate that greater emotional intimacy (ie, closeness of feelings) and intellectual intimacy (ie, sharing ideas) were associated with having a recent healthcare check-up and with refraining from binge drinking, particularly for males. Future research should evaluate the causal link between relationship intimacy and health behaviours among the general population, with special consideration of sex-specific differences in these associations, as well as the heterogeneity in romantic relationships among older adults.
