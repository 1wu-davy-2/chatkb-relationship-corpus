ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnforgiveness is a potential response following a relational transgression in couple relationships, manifesting in cognitive‐evaluative, emotional‐ruminative, and offender reconstrual dimensions. The type and degree of unforgiveness experienced after a relational transgression could be influenced by interpersonal variables such as perceived partner responsiveness. However, the link between perceived partner responsiveness and unforgiveness has not been examined. This study aimed to investigate this link to better understand the factors that might hinder unforgiveness after relational transgressions. It was hypothesized that perceived partner responsiveness would be negatively associated with unforgiveness. Data were collected from 275 couples in which partners reported having experienced a relational transgression, using self‐report questionnaires. Data analyses revealed a significant negative association between perceived partner responsiveness and all dimensions of unforgiveness. Conversely, a positive association was found between perceived partner insensitivity and all dimensions of unforgiveness. These findings might offer practical insights for therapeutic interventions with couples navigating relational transgressions.Keywords: couples, partner responsiveness, relational transgressions, unforgiveness

BODY
Research shows that relational transgressions are common events that can happen in many relationships (Crowley et al. 2019). A relational transgression is defined as “violations of implicit or explicit expectations, norms, or rules in relationships, and can impact the partners' perceptions of the relationship and their relationship dynamics” (Niehuis et al. 2019, p. 466). These events usually have negative consequences for the well‐being of both partners and the relationship (Ysseldyk and Wohl 2012). After relational transgressions, the offended partner can experience unforgiveness towards their partner, causing a strain in the relationship (Crowley et al. 2019). Despite the prevalence and impact of these events, the factors involved in unforgiveness have rarely been examined, and the process of unforgiveness in couples is not well understood (Stackhouse et al. 2018). One potential factor that might be involved in the unforgiveness process is perceived partner responsiveness after the relational transgression has occurred. Perceiving the offending partner as responsive to one's disclosure of feelings might help the offended partner to experience less emotional and cognitive distress due to the relational transgression and might reduce their level of unforgiveness. This study's aim was to better understand the factors involved in unforgiveness within couples and to examine the association between perceived partner responsiveness and unforgiveness in offended partners who have experienced a relational transgression. By exploring this association, the study will provide a theoretical framework for understanding how perceived partner responsiveness might help understand reactions of unforgiveness. Clinically, the findings could inform interventions designed to promote forgiveness and relational healing, ultimately enhancing the well‐being of couples navigating the aftermath of relational transgressions. Such insights have the potential to improve therapeutic strategies and relationship counseling practices, making the study highly relevant for both researchers and practitioners in the field of couple therapy.

Relational Transgressions and Unforgiveness

While romantic relationships generally serve as protective factors for the partners' well‐being (Wazid and Shahnawaz 2017), they can become detrimental in certain situations, especially when relational transgressions occur (Côté et al. 2022). Relational transgressions could entail many events such as infidelity, threat of infidelity, lying, broken promises, or inconsiderate behaviour (Cameron et al. 2002). After such events, some people are able to forgive their partner for their transgression. Forgiveness is the process wherein offended partners reduce their motivations to think, feel, and behave negatively toward a transgressor while fostering benevolent behaviors (Russell et al. 2018). Forgiveness has been extensively studied and has been associated with many positive constructs, such as empathy (Kimmes and Durtschi 2016), relationship satisfaction (Wu et al. 2022), as well as a diminution of negative consequences due to a relational transgression (Rye and Pargament 2002).

However, relational transgressions can also negatively impact a romantic relationship, potentially hindering the personal and relational well‐being of both partners. Research has highlighted the adverse effects of relational transgressions on romantic partnerships (Behrens and Kröger 2024; Ysseldyk and Wohl 2012). Following a relational transgression, the offended partner often struggles with relational and psychological difficulties and the duration of these consequences can last for various amounts of time (Micco et al. 2018). Some negative consequences to relational transgressions include lower relationship quality as reported by both partners (Paleari et al. 2010). Another possible response to such transgressions is unforgiveness (Crowley et al. 2019).

Traditionally, unforgiveness has been operationalized as the opposite of forgiveness and has been seen as a negative response (Beltrán‐Morillas et al. 2019; Crowley et al. 2019; Worthington 2001). However, some authors have challenged this binary perspective, revealing that forgiveness and unforgiveness are not strictly opposites (Younger et al. 2004; Zechmeister and Romero 2002). Factors distinguishing forgiveness and unforgiveness exist. For instance, forgiveness is associated with trait forgiveness and the degree to which an individual attempts to forgive, whereas unforgiveness is not (Stackhouse et al. 2018). Furthermore, unforgiveness can be reduced through various means beyond direct forgiveness, such as seeking revenge, suppressing negative emotions, pursuing legal justice, or justifying the offender's actions (Wade and Worthington 2003). Stackhouse et al. (2018) dive deeper into methodological issues created by trying to measure unforgiveness by reversing forgiveness measures. This creates methodical problems, by restricting our ability to predict the antecedents, correlates, and outcomes of unforgiveness. Moreover, a recent study suggests that individuals who report having forgiven an offense might still remain unforgiving to some extent (Boon et al. 2022), suggesting that both types of experiences and processes may occur independently from one another. Additionally, assuming that unforgiveness is maladaptive can oversimplify the complexity of human response to wrongdoing. Such assumptions may cause a false impression that forgiveness is the only appropriate reaction to relational transgressions and may pressure offended partners to prematurely resolve relational transgressions. In certain circumstances, relational transgressions may be deemed unforgivable, such as severe offences where forgiveness can cause the re‐victimization of the offended partner, responding to a relational transgression with unforgiveness can signify strength and resilience, as well as a mechanism for self‐protection (Stackhouse et al. 2018), therefore justifying the need to study unforgiveness as a separate construct.

Dimensions of Unforgiveness

Building upon the exploration of unforgiveness, it is important to delve deeper into the three dimensions of unforgiveness proposed by Stackhouse et al. (2018), which encompass cognitive‐evaluative, emotional‐ruminative, and offender reconstrual. These dimensions shed light on the multifaceted nature of unforgiveness and its potential impact on individuals' responses to relational transgressions. Cognitive‐evaluative unforgiveness represents the cognitive dimension of unforgiveness, encapsulating individuals' beliefs regarding the forgivability of an offense (Stackhouse et al. 2018; Zechmeister and Romero 2002). Emotional‐ruminative unforgiveness delves into the emotional dimension, where anger and resentment experienced due to the transgression hinder the offended partner's decision to forgive their partner. This dimension is associated with rumination and potential negative consequences such as negative affect and feelings of revenge (Stackhouse et al. 2018; Worthington 2001). Finally, offender reconstrual revolves around the offended partner's perception of the offender post‐transgression. This dimension involves a change in the offended partner's perception of the offender, as they struggle to connect the offender's actions with their previous beliefs about them, which prevents them to move on from the transgression (Stackhouse et al. 2018; Zechmeister and Romero 2002). Understanding these dimensions of unforgiveness provides a nuanced perspective on individuals' responses to wrongdoing within romantic relationships. It underscores the complexity of the unforgiveness process and highlights the diverse factors at play when someone does not forgive. However, currently, little is known about the factors involved in this process. By studying these dimensions, researchers and practitioners can develop more tailored interventions to support individuals experiencing unforgiveness and promote healthy resolution strategies within relationships.

It is important to note that there are transgression‐related factors that might influence the offended partner's experiences of unforgiveness. Previous research has demonstrated that the transgression must be important for the offended partner before unforgiveness can be considered as a potential response (Waldron and Kelley 2005). Multiple variables are involved in determining if the transgression is deemed important, including the severity of the transgression (Miczo et al. 2018) and the time since the transgression (Waldron and Kelley 2005). These factors are therefore important to consider to better understand reactions to relational transgressions, such as unforgiveness. While several studies have investigated many individual (e.g., empathy, agreeableness, Riek and Mania 2012) and situational factors that may be involved in the forgiveness process (Dillow and Denes 2022), factors specific to romantic relationships have been overlooked in the literature on forgiveness and could also contribute to the experience of unforgiveness.

Unforgiveness and Perceived Partner Responsiveness

There are multiple factors that may contribute to the offended partner's unforgiveness, such as their empathy toward their partner (Kimmes and Durtschi 2016), their relationship satisfaction (Wu et al. 2022), and their perception of the offending partner's responsiveness following the relational transgression. Among these factors, perceived partner responsiveness stands out as an encompassing dimension, as it integrates both the actions of the offending partner and the perspective of the offended partner, making it a particularly relevant variable to study. Previously, perceived partner responsiveness had been associated with forgiveness (Pansera and La Guardia 2012), highlighting its pivotal role in fostering relationship resilience. However, the association between perceived partner responsiveness and unforgiveness has not been examined. Considering that forgiveness and unforgiveness are not the two poles of the same construct, examining the link between perceived partner responsiveness and unforgiveness becomes critical to better understand unforgiveness as a separate construct. Perceived partner responsiveness is defined “as a process by which individuals come to believe that relationship partners both attend to and react supportively to central, core defining features of the self” (Reis et al. 2004, p. 203). Perceived partner responsiveness can be conceptualized as per two dimensions: insensitivity and responsiveness (Crasta et al. 2021). Insensitivity refers to perceiving the partner as detached from the relationship and insensitive to one's needs, whereas responsiveness represents the degree to which individuals feel understood, validated, and cared for by their partner (Crasta et al. 2021). Perceived partner responsiveness provides both objective and subjective qualities pertaining to the partner. It includes objective qualities of the response of the partner (such as their communication skills) as well as the subjective qualities, such as the partner's motivated interpretation of the response (Crasta et al. 2021).

It has been shown that perceived partner responsiveness protects the relationship by increasing the likelihood that social support is experienced as helpful (Maisel and Gable 2009), buffering emotional reactivity, and instilling a sense of trust, after negative events (Crasta et al. 2021). As such, perceived partner responsiveness could be associated with lower levels of unforgiveness. In other words, if the offending partner demonstrates empathy and care following a relational transgression, it could reduce the likelihood of unforgiveness. For instance, emotional‐ruminative unforgiveness could be lower since the offended partner might be less inclined to ruminate over the transgression after receiving validation for their feelings. Similarly, offender reconstrual unforgiveness might be less likely, as the offended partner would perceive the offending partner as caring and present in the relationship once again. Although partner responsiveness cannot undo the transgression, it might still reduce the chances of experiencing cognitive‐evaluative unforgiveness by reassuring the offended partner about the strength and stability of the relationship, potentially reducing the perception of how unforgivable the transgression is. Alternatively, all these positive effects could be reversed if the offending partner is insensitive toward the offended partner, resulting in higher levels of unforgiveness across all dimensions.

Aim and Hypothesis

This study's aim was to examine the associations between perceived partner responsiveness and unforgiveness following a relational transgression in couples. It was hypothesized that offended partners reporting lower levels of perceived partner responsiveness and higher levels of perceived partner insensitivity would exhibit higher levels of unforgiveness across all dimensions of unforgiveness following a relational transgression while controlling for the time since the relational transgression and the perceived severity of the relational transgression. The aim of this study was to provide insights into the link between unforgiveness and relationship functioning in the aftermath of relational transgressions.

Methods

Participants

To partake in this study, couples were required to meet several eligibility criteria: they had to be 18 years of age or older, reside in Canada, and be in a romantic relationship for at least 2 years. Also, partners needed to cohabitate, speak and understand French, and have access to Internet as well as a personal e‐mail address. Participation from both partners was mandatory.

The final sample consisted of 512 individuals (256 couples) with an average age of 32.55 years of age (SD = 9.39; ranging from 20 to 76). Most of the participants identified as woman (52.9%), were heterosexual (72.5%), and White (94.1%). In terms of employment status, most participants were employed full‐time (61.5%), while 21.1% were students. A significant portion held a bachelor's degree (39.6%). On average, participants had been together for 8.01 years (SD = 6.54; ranging from 2 years to 33 years). Additional information on sociodemographic data is presented in Table 1.

Procedure

This study is part of a larger longitudinal study on behaviors and emotions in couple relationships. Recruitment took place between December 2021 and April 2023. Participants were mostly recruited using social media (Facebook, Instagram). A research assistant conducted a brief phone interview with potential couples to present the study and verify eligibility. Upon consenting to participate, participants were asked to complete the baseline questionnaires online, which took about 75 min to fill out. When both partners had filled out the baseline questionnaire, they were invited to complete a 10‐day daily diary study, and four subsequent questionnaires over a 12‐month period (3‐, 6‐, 9‐ and 12‐month follow‐up). At the end of their participation, participants were compensated for up to $140 per couple. The current study used the baseline measures only.

Measures

Sociodemographic Questionnaire

Participants were asked to provide personal and relationship information, such as their age, gender, sexual orientation, ethnicity, relationship status, and relationship duration. Validated questionnaires were used to assess the study variables.

Transgression Related Questions

Participants (n = 511) were asked to recall and describe a previous relational transgression they experienced within their current relationship, with the following instructions: “It's not uncommon for people to experience hurtful situations in their relationship with their partner. Indeed, it can happen that a partner acts in a way that goes against the norms established in the relationship (implicit or explicit norms) or that doesn't respect our personal values or expectations. These events are often hurtful and can have negative consequences on the couple's relationship. For example, it could be a lie, a broken promise, a blatant lack of consideration, inappropriate or aggressive behavior, infidelity, and so on. Think of a time when you were hurt by your current partner's behavior. Please describe what your partner did and what aspect(s) of the situation hurt you.” Following the description of the transgression, participants were asked to indicate when the event occurred, utilizing a six‐point rating scale ranging from “in the last month” to “more than 2 years ago.” Relational transgressions reported by participants varied greatly, from broken promises, infidelity, criticizing to making jokes. Transgressions that were perceived to be more severe (scores of 7 or 6 on the severity scale) included events such as infidelity or invasion of privacy. Events that were perceived to be less severe (scores of 1 or 2) included invents related to lack of communication, or making jokes. Participants were also asked how severe the incident was considering its negative consequences on themselves and their relationship. This question was rated on a seven‐point scale ranging from “did not have an impact” to “extreme negative impact on me and my relationship.” Only one participant did not report experiencing a relational transgression.

The Unforgiveness Measure

After reporting on this past relational transgression, participants were asked to complete the unforgiveness measure thinking back about this specific event. Unforgiveness was assessed using the French translated version of the 13‐item Unforgiveness Measure (Stackhouse et al. 2018; Jean et al. 2024). This measure comprises three subscales: the 4‐item cognitive evaluative sub‐scale (e.g., “What this person did is unforgiveable”), 6‐item emotional ruminative sub‐scale (e.g., “I continue to feel hurt by what happened”) and 3‐item offender reconstrual sub‐scale (e.g., “The event changed the way I see this person”). Items ranged from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 7 (Strongly agree), with higher scores indicating more unforgiveness. Previous research has demonstrated the reliability of this measure with Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.84 to 0.90 for the cognitive‐evaluative subscale, 0.84 to 0.85 for the emotional‐ruminative subscale and from 0.74 to 0.83 for the offender reconstrual subscale (Stackhouse et al. 2018). The reliability for the current study was 0.88 for the cognitive‐evaluative subscale, 0.79 for the emotional‐ruminative subscale, and 0.81 for the offender reconstrual subscale.

Perceived Partner Responsiveness

Perceived partner responsiveness was measured using the French translation of the 16‐item Perceived Partner Responsiveness and Insensitivity scale (PRI, Crasta et al. 2021). Items ranged from 0 (Not at all) to 5 (Completely). This measure comprised two subscales: the 8‐item Responsiveness subscale and the 8‐item Insensitivity subscale, where higher scores indicated greater responsiveness and insensitivity, respectively. The responsiveness subscale measures the degree to which individuals recently feel understood, validated, and cared for by their partner (e.g., “my partner really listens to me”), whereas the insensitivity subscale measures the degree to which they recently perceive their partner as detached or insensitive (e.g., “my partner does NOT accept my feelings and concerns”; Crasta et al. 2021). Total scores were calculated for each sub‐scale. This measure has been shown to be reliable for the responsiveness subscale (α = 0.98) and for the insensitivity scale (α = 0.93, Crasta et al. 2021). We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis which indicated that the French version of the PRI retained the original two‐factor structure (responsiveness and insensitivity), with overall good model fit (χ²(456) = 717.44, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.046 (90% CI [0.039, 0.052]), SRMR = 0.055). Equality‐constrained factor loadings across partners produced a satisfactory fit, and residual correlations between partners were included to account for dyadic interdependence. Cronbach's alphas for this study were 0.88 for the insensitivity subscale, and 0.94 for the responsiveness subscale.

Data Analysis

Preliminary Analyses

Preliminary analyses were performed using SPSS. All variables were screened for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Cognitive evaluative unforgiveness (2.55) and perceived insensitivity (1.84) were positively skewed. When conducting the main analyses, the robust maximum likelihood estimator was used in Mplus to account for the skewness of the data. Preliminary bivariate correlational analyses between study variables (the three unforgiveness dimensions and perceived partner responsiveness and insensitivity) and transgression‐related variables (severity of the transgression, time since the transgression occurred) were conducted.

Main Analyses

To test associations among the three subscales of unforgiveness and two subscales of partner responsiveness, two path analyses models were performed, using Mplus. Dyadic distinguishability by gender was tested by comparing a distinguishable APIM model (gender‐labeled paths) with an indistinguishable APIM model (equality constraints on means, variances, covariances, and actor/partner paths). The distinguishable model failed to converge, whereas the indistinguishable model converged and showed adequate fit. Supplemental analyses testing gender moderation confirmed that actor and partner effects did not differ by gender. Taken together, these findings supported our decision to treat dyads as statistically indistinguishable. Partners were therefore constrained to be equal in the two models (Kenny et al. 2006). They were randomly assigned the roles of partner 1 and partner 2 (regardless of their gender), and all parameters were set equal in the model between partners 1 or 2 as per the standard procedures of Kenny et al. (2006) for dealing with indistinguishable dyads in dyadic analyses. Direct paths were then drawn from individuals' perceived responsiveness to their own three dimensions of unforgiveness. Several indices were examined to asses the model fit to our data: the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR) goodness of fit index (values of 0.08 or less suggest a good fit), the comparative fit index (CFI; values greater than 0.90 suggest a good fit), and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; values of 0.08 or less suggest a model that fits well) and its 90% confidence interval (Hu and Bentler 1999). To ensure that model results were not dependent on the random partner assignment (partner 1 vs. partner 2), we re‐ran all models using a different random assignment; the pattern of results and statistical conclusions remained identical.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between perceived partner responsiveness and unforgiveness among couples who experienced a relational transgression. As anticipated, the findings demonstrated that the offended partners tended to be more unforgiving when they perceived their partner as less responsive. Conversely, when the offended partners perceived their partner as more insensitive, they tended to experience more unforgiveness towards their partner. Additionally, the perceived severity of the transgression as well as the time since the transgression occurred were also associated with levels of unforgiveness, suggesting that these variables should also be taken into account when working with couples who have experienced relational transgressions.

Perceived Partner Responsiveness, Perceived Partner Insensitivity, and Unforgiveness

The associations between perceived partner responsiveness and lower scores on the three dimensions of unforgiveness, as well as the association between perceived partner insensitivity and higher scores on the three dimensions of unforgiveness could be explained by how the feeling that one's partner is responsive or not can modify one's perception of the relational transgression and their partner.

Research suggests that high perceived partner responsiveness is associated with offended partner's commitment, motivating them to remain invested in the relationship (Segal and Fraley 2016), and potentially indicating a desire to reconcile and continue the relationship from both partners. Believing in their partners' will to reconciliate and to invest in the relationship could reduce offender reconstrual unforgiveness by affirming that the offending partner is still the same person who has been part of this relationship and will remain so. While perceiving that one's partner is insensitive after perpetrating a transgression could result in the offended partner feeling even more estranged from their partner. Moreover, the lack of care and sensitivity from their partner might exacerbate the negative consequences of a relational transgression, making it harder for the offended partner to believe that this is the same person they knew before. This lack of thoughtfulness could also contribute to building the negative perception of their partner since they not only perpetrated a relational transgression but also lack sensitivity in their relationship.

It is also known that after a relational transgression, offended partners experience relational and psychological difficulties (Auger et al. 2017; Bachman and Guerrero 2006). Furthermore, offended partners experiencing emotional‐ruminative unforgiveness tend to experience many negative emotions such as anger and resentment (Stackhouse et al. 2018). However, the responsive and caring behaviour of the offending partner may soothe the offended partner and reduce their tendency to ruminate and hold on to anger in relation to the hurtful events, as our results suggest. Similarly, levels of emotional‐ruminative unforgiveness may change after a transgression when the offending partner fails to be understanding towards the offended partner. This lack of acknowledgment and sensitivity may be associated with the offended partner feeling even more anger and resentment towards their partner, and potentially contribute to the existing feelings of distress and rumination regarding the transgression and its implications.

Finally, cognitive‐evaluative unforgiveness is related to the act of relational transgression itself, as it is about how unforgivable the offended partner perceives the transgression to be (e.g., “What this person did is unforgivable”). It may be expected that this dimension would be less strongly associated to partner responsiveness because the degree to which the partner is seen as responsive might not be as strong in determining how unacceptable a transgression is perceived. However, even for this dimension, a notable association was observed between perceived partner responsiveness and cognitive‐evaluative unforgiveness. This could be explained by the perception of the offending partner based on their actions after the relational transgression: when the offending partner shows empathy towards the offended partner's feelings, this might prompt the offended partner to reconsider how unforgivable the relational transgression is and make it easier for the offended partner to perceive the relational transgression as forgivable. As for an insensitive offending partner, this might amplify cognitive‐evaluative unforgiveness, as their insensitive reaction seems to fuel the negative emotions and offender reconstrual experienced by the offended partner, as well as the negative thoughts related to the relational transgression and, in turn, promote the belief that the relational transgression is difficult or impossible to forgive. For example, if the offended partner is experiencing negative emotions towards their partner and feels like they do not know who their partner is anymore, they might end up ruminating over the relational transgression, which in turn could amplify the perceived the severity of the relational transgression and increase the cognitive‐evaluative unforgiveness experienced.

Strengths and Limitations

This study presents multiple methodological strengths, including the use of a large and inclusive sample of couples. This study also focused on the unforgiveness experienced after a relational transgression, which has been left out of most transgression‐related research, by considering different dimensions of the experience of unforgiveness. However, this study presents some limitations that are important to mention. First, the cross‐sectional study design makes it impossible to infer causality between the variables. It may be that if offended partners are more inclined to forgive their partner and work on repairing the relationship, their offending partner may be perceived as being more responsive. Another limitation is the lack of ethnic diversity of the sample, with 94% identifying as white. The lack of diversity in our sample limits our ability to examine how social positioning may influence responses to relational transgressions. It is possible that individuals from marginalized or racialized backgrounds might react differently to a relational transgression. For instance, social norms and expectations might make it difficult for people from marginalized backgrounds to experience and express their unforgiveness while these pressures might not be present for privileged groups (Cherry 2021; Smith and McFarland 2015). These hypotheses warrant further exploration in future research. Nonetheless, there was diversity in terms of sexual orientation, with more than a quarter identifying with a sexual orientation other than heterosexual. A final limitation is the fact that, on average, the relational transgressions were perceived as moderately severe, and most participants reported relatively low levels of unforgiveness. Different results might be obtained in a sample that experienced more severe transgressions and events that are more difficult to forgive. In these couples, it is possible that the behaviors of the offending partner post‐transgression might have more modest effects on the offended partner's inclination to forgive the offence. Future research could investigate this research question.

Future Research and Clinical Implications

The findings of this study carry notable implications for addressing unforgiveness following relational transgressions. Overall, the results suggest that it could be interesting to investigate if perceived partner responsiveness can reduce the levels of unforgiveness overtime or help the resolution after a relational transgression. Future research could also examine other variables relevant to this association to create a comprehensive model of resolution after a relational transgression. Variables that have been associated with resolution and forgiveness could be examined in relation to unforgiveness as well, including the offer of apologies from the offending partner (Schumann and Orehek 2019), empathy towards the offending partner (Kimmes and Durtschi 2016), and the guilt experienced by the offending partner (Tilghman‐Osborne et al. 2010). Investigating these factors could provide a more nuanced understanding of the dynamics involved in resolving issues after a relational transgression, further enhancing interventions and support strategies for couples facing such challenges. Furthermore, it is important to mention that the study of unforgiveness as a separate construct from forgiveness is very recent in the literature, and additional research is necessary to provide unequivocal methodological proofs of this conceptual distinction.

Although the results of this study are based on a non‐clinical sample, where most participants perceive their partners as highly responsive and minimally insensitive, clinically, the findings from this study could be particularly useful for professionals working with couples who have experienced relational transgressions. Promoting responsiveness in the offending partner might contribute to enhancing the couple's well‐being by decreasing the level of unforgiveness due to the relational transgression. However, responsiveness from the offending partner is only one of the steps necessary to reduce unforgiveness experienced by the offended partner. Previous clinical studies have identified multiple stages involved in resolving such situations (Makinen and Johnson 2006). Nonetheless, it is important to remember that although unforgiveness is seen as a negative response for the resolution of a relational transgression, it could also be adaptive for the offended partner in certain situations, such as severe offences, where empowerment is necessary to avoid re‐victimization (Stackhouse et al. 2018). Previous research suggests that to create change in the offending partner, the offended partner needs to establish boundaries before forgiving (Russell et al. 2018). The current findings support this point of view by highlighting the negative association between feeling understood by the offending partner and unforgiveness. Indeed, this study promotes an approach that places responsibility on both partners to achieve resolution following a transgression. Specifically, the offending partner plays a crucial role in listening, understanding, and validating the experiences of their partner after a relational transgression, demonstrating their continued involvement in the relationship. At the same time, the therapists can accompany the offended partners in their healing process, including supporting them in hearing and understanding the point of their offending partner, as well as considering the context surrounding the relational transgression. This reciprocal process emphasizes the importance of mutual understanding and effort in navigating the aftermath of a relational transgression.

Conclusion

This study brings novelty to the field by examining the association between unforgiveness experienced after a relational transgression and a relationship process such as perceived partner responsiveness. Findings of this study highlight that it might be important to consider relational behaviors post‐transgression, such as partner responsiveness to understand the process of unforgiveness and ultimately facilitate processes of repair and reconciliation in couples.
