ABSTRACT
AbstractParental attachment plays a crucial role in shaping internal working models that foster a sense of security for navigating romantic relationships in adulthood. Alexithymia is a personality trait characterised by difficulties in identifying, describing, and processing one’s emotions, which may hinder an individual’s engagement in romantic relationships. The present study investigated whether alexithymia mediates the relationship between parental attachment and romantic social interest, with mother and father attachment examined separately. Using a cross-sectional design, data were collected from 305 young adults in Sri Lanka through self-report questionnaires. Mediation analyses conducted using PROCESS Macro indicated that alexithymia has significant indirect effects on the relationship between both mother and father attachment and romantic social interest. These findings contribute to the understanding of how emotional processing mechanisms link early attachment experiences to prosocial tendencies in romantic contexts, particularly within an Asian cultural setting, which is understudied.Keywords: Alexithymia, Parental attachment, Maternal attachment, Paternal attachment, Romantic social interestSubject terms: Cultural and media studies, Cultural and media studies, Psychology, Psychology

BODY
Introduction

Various psychological factors influence the establishment of meaningful connections in relationships. One such factor is attachment style, which plays a significant role in shaping close interpersonal relationships1. Attachment styles develop through early interactions with primary caregivers and tend to remain relatively stable across the lifespan2. These early experiences contribute to the development of either secure or insecure attachment patterns, which in turn influence the quality of interpersonal relationships. Individuals with more secure attachment styles are generally more likely to experience fulfilling and meaningful relationships3.

In addition to attachment, alexithymia is another important personality trait that may influence interpersonal functioning. Alexithymia is characterised by difficulties in identifying, describing, and processing emotions, as well as an externally oriented cognitive pattern that may lead to infrequent reflection on individual feelings and internal experiences4. It is thought to develop during childhood5 and has been linked to challenges in social relationships6. Individuals with higher levels of alexithymia may struggle with emotional expression and understanding, which can negatively affect communication and contribute to dissatisfaction in romantic relationships7.

Attachment and romantic relationships

Attachment patterns are shaped during early childhood through interactions with primary caregivers and continue to influence interpersonal functioning into adulthood8–11. These experiences contribute to the formation of internal working models, which shape individuals’ perceptions of themselves and others in relationships12. Such models influence expectations of support, trust, and emotional availability, thereby affecting relationship dynamics in adulthood13–15. Romantic relationships, in particular, have been conceptualised as attachment relationships, where partners function as sources of emotional security16.

Adult attachment refers to an individual’s ability to establish and maintain close relationships that promote a sense of safety and well-being17. Secure attachment is associated with better emotional regulation and more satisfying relationships, whereas insecure attachment styles, such as avoidance and ambivalence, are linked to poorer relationship outcomes18.

While many studies have examined parental attachment as a unified construct, fewer have explored mother attachment and father attachment separately. Existing research suggests that these relationships may have distinct influences on development19,20. For instance, positive maternal relationships have been associated with higher self-esteem, emotional awareness, and help-seeking behaviours14. Collins and Read14 found that parental connections that were imprinted in an individual’s mind as loving and accepting were linked to perceptions that others would be there to support them. Notably, attachment avoidance was found to have a stronger negative effect than ambivalent attachment types. This distinction arises from the consideration that ambivalently attached people can appreciate and enjoy their relationships21, conversely, avoidantly attached people tend to keep their relationships from being a significant part of their lives22.

Maternal support was found to contribute to life satisfaction among adolescents as well23. Research also indicates that the mother figure tends to remain an important attachment figure throughout adolescence and even into adulthood24,25. This suggests that the mother figure continues to serve as a key source of security even throughout adolescence and into adulthood. However, it should be considered that family structures vary, and not all individuals grow up with a maternal presence.

A study of young adults found that those with secure attachment in romantic relationships described their early parental experiences more positively, viewing their mothers as confident and compassionate, and their fathers as fair, understanding, and attentive26. In contrast, those who were avoidantly attached, portrayed their mother as erratic and incoherent, and their father as unstable and apathetic, with the overall impression that the relationship between their parents was unsatisfactory26.

Interestingly, evidence suggests a gendered pattern in parent–child attachment, wherein the strength of the father-child bond is more strongly associated with boys’ self-esteem, while the quality of the mother–child bond shows a stronger association with girls’ self-esteem27. Conversely, some studies have indicated that the father-child bond may exert a stronger influence on the developmental outcomes of female children. A study found that for early adolescent girls, father-child bonds of attachment impacted self-esteem more strongly than mother–child bond relationships28. A study on 542 Dutch adolescents further supported this notion and found that variations in the perceived quality of a father attachment significantly impacted self-esteem of daughters and not in sons29.

On the other hand, a study on a sample of emerging adults (ages 24–28 years) from divorced families depicted that higher levels of support from the father serve a protective effect, to secure individuals from the adverse effects of a poorer quality mother-adolescent connection30. Seiffge-Krenke et al.31 indicated that when the father-child relationship is distant during adolescence, feelings of anxiousness may develop in later romantic relationships. In romantic relationships, however, it has been discovered that the mother-adolescent relationship is more substantial than the father-adolescent relationship32. Therefore, further investigation is warranted into the influence of father attachment on young adults’ romantic relationships, given the inconsistent findings in prior research regarding the paternal role in influencing romantic relationships among young adults.

Attachment, alexithymia, and romantic social interest

Early caregiver relationships play a central role in shaping emotional and cognitive development33. Alexithymia is a personality construct defined by difficulties in identifying and articulating emotions, accompanied by an externally oriented cognitive style, which is characterized by a focus on concrete, external events rather than internal emotional experiences34. It is regarded as a stable personality feature that develops between childhood and early adulthood35. Alexithymia is associated with difficulties in emotion regulation and an increased risk of both mental and physical health disorders4. Literature shows that alexithymia may develop because of trauma from childhood36 or from negative interactions with primary caregivers37. According to research, individuals with higher levels of alexithymia have difficulty establishing healthy and pleasant interpersonal interactions38, which can have a substantial impact on their overall well-being39. Prolonged exposure to highly dysfunctional, emotionally charged situations during critical periods of childhood emotional and cognitive development may contribute to elevated levels of alexithymia36. Individuals who experience emotional neglect, inconsistent affective responses, and rejection of emotional responses exerted in order to control the child, are particularly at risk of developing higher levels of alexithymia later in life34,40.

Recent research indicates that an early caregiver’s sensitivity and responsiveness to the child’s emotions are fundamental in predicting coping ability when feeling distressed with others41. Research among adolescents and young adults indicates that individuals with secure attachment experience fewer negative emotions and are better able to form close relationships that provide support during emotionally challenging times42. While individuals with an insecure attachment, exhibit lower levels of pleasant emotions than those with a secure attachment, at the same time struggle to manage stress, worry, and sadness, as a result of their difficulties in self-regulation and other negative effects (Montebarocchi et al. 2004).

According to Hazan and Shaver16, parental attachment strongly predicts the quality of attachment that a child will have in romantic relationships as an adult. Beyond influencing romantic relationships, both parental and peer attachments play a significant role in shaping mental well-being. Securely attached individuals are generally more capable of regulating their emotions and exhibit lower levels of alexithymia, while those with insecure attachment often struggle with emotional regulation, experience fewer positive emotions, and face greater difficulties managing stress and anxiety.

Romantic relationships are associated with increased self-esteem, stress reduction, and loneliness reduction43. Qualter et al.6 found that alexithymia is directly associated with social, family, and romantic loneliness, with interpersonal distrust playing a key role in this relationship and interacting with alexithymia to predict social and family loneliness. This suggests that individuals with higher levels of alexithymia may develop greater interpersonal distrust, which in turn contributes to increased feelings of loneliness. The presence of alexithymia brings difficulties in cognitive and emotional expressiveness, which in turn has a negative impact on intimate relationships44.

According to past studies, high levels of alexithymia are linked to lower quality of romantic relationships and commitment45. In 18.5% of the undergraduate sample, participants exhibited high levels of alexithymia, which correlated with greater depression and negative affect, as well as lower life satisfaction, happiness, and positive affect. Participants with higher alexithymia also reported poorer quality in their romantic relationships, including reduced commitment, trust, romance, satisfaction, and love45. Individuals grappling with alexithymia often encounter challenges in sustaining personal relationships due to their struggles in conveying their emotions thus serving as a hindrance to forming meaningful relationships46. Individuals with greater levels of alexithymia have been shown to have elevated levels of attachment anxiety and avoidance, including loneliness47.

According to research, gender differences in alexithymia were observed. Males have a higher rate of alexithymia than females48. Males who scored higher on alexithymia were observed to have less positive support from their partners. Individuals with greater levels of alexithymia appear to have a cold and distant interpersonal style49, and their partners respond to them in the same way. As a result, people with greater degrees of alexithymia have previously suffered from interpersonal issues and may hold diminished expectations for supportive assistance from their partners38.

Parental attachment plays a pivotal role in shaping emotion regulation abilities. A national survey carried out on a sample of 29,911 adolescents in Sri Lanka displayed that the family is thought to play a significant role in promoting an individual’s psychosocial well-being. The study displayed that 52% of participants reported that their family was a refuge for their problems50. In a qualitative study involving Sri Lankan adolescents aged 15–18 years, found that when parents were perceived as welcoming and receptive, adolescents experienced greater comfort and openness in the parent–child relationship51. They reported feeling able to share freely, supported by a sense of unconditional love and acceptance. In contrast, when the relationship was seen as distant, unsympathetic, or emotionally disengaged, adolescents viewed the parent–child bond more negatively.

Another study conducted in Sri Lanka found that if positive attachment with parents is not established, the child’s focus shifts to attachment with peers52. The study also found a negative relationship between parent–child attachment and emotion regulation difficulties. The impact of attachment with the father was a stronger predictor of emotional regulation ability amongst adolescents.

Furthermore, Fiske and Fiske53 assert that social relationships are guided based on the cultural context and that behaviours may serve different meanings depending on cultural backgrounds. Hence, it is imperative to consider cultural contexts, and considerations influenced by cultural beliefs and norms, in the context of understanding young people’s behaviour. Different cultures have different expectations and norms concerning interpersonal behaviours54. Therefore, behaviours considered to be socially acceptable in one culture may not be deemed so in another.

Sri Lanka is a developing country in South Asia and its social structure is deeply rooted in its historical, religious, and social factors. The cultural landscape of Sri Lanka has been described as collectivistic55. Sri Lanka’s collectivist cultural orientation significantly influences family dynamics and preservation of social harmony56, while being interdependent with family and friends51.

A 30-year ethnic conflict has had a major impact on the interpersonal aspects of Sri Lankan society, both directly and indirectly owing to the high degree of violence, suffering, loss of lives and destruction of property that was experienced57. Hence, this may have led to generational psychological trauma leaving people to be weary of one another under the guise of protecting themselves. On the other hand, it is thought that individuals in collectivistic societies prefer to experience low-arousal feelings over high-arousal feelings58. Findings from a study carried out in Sri Lanka depicted that individuals tend to believe that it is appropriate to feel some positive emotions (e.g., happiness) and some negative emotions (e.g., sadness and surprise), but not feelings such as anger or contempt59. This may be attributed to a desire to preserve harmony and avoid escalating conflicts, particularly when experiencing intense emotions like anger.

While previous research has established that alexithymia is associated with poorer relationship quality and emotional difficulties, its specific link to romantic social interest warrants further clarification44,45. Romantic social interest extends beyond relationship satisfaction and reflects an individual’s prosocial orientations within romantic contexts, including empathy, cooperation, emotional investment, and responsiveness to a partner’s needs60.

Individuals with higher levels of alexithymia experience difficulties in identifying, interpreting, and expressing emotions, as well as an externally oriented cognitive style4. These characteristics may limit their capacity for empathic understanding, reduce sensitivity to a partner’s emotional cues, and hinder engagement in mutually supportive and cooperative behaviours38. As a result, such individuals may exhibit lower levels of prosocial engagement in romantic contexts, which are central to romantic social interest. Therefore, alexithymia may function as an important psychological mechanism linking early attachment experiences with the capacity to engage in emotionally responsive and prosocial romantic relationships.

To date, there is limited research in Sri Lanka, a developing and lower middle-income country, that has studied parental attachment, specifically in examining paternal and maternal attachment amongst young adults. Furthermore, no evidence draws links to relationship challenges with alexithymia and attachment in Sri Lanka, which is also in an Asian context which has been understudied in the context of psychological research. A better understanding of the relationship between parental attachment and social interest in romantic relationships along with alexithymia would provide a better insight into individuals struggling in their own relationships. This study contributes to the limited body of research within the Sri Lankan context, which has been relatively overlooked in the examination of parenting, alexithymia, and young adults’relationships. Through these understandings, individuals who have difficulties in romantic relationships would have better insights into their situations, adopt better coping abilities, and work towards forming healthy relationships. Also, this study would contribute to understanding of young adults functioning within a non-WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) society.

Purpose of the present study

The current research investigates the relationship between parent attachment and romantic social interest with alexithymia as a mediator. The following are the hypotheses of the study: (1) Parental attachment (father and mother) would predict romantic social interest among young adults. (2) Alexithymia would mediate the relationship between mother attachment and romantic social interest, and (3) Alexithymia would mediate the relationship between father attachment and romantic social interest (Fig. 1).

Conceptual Framework. The independent variable is parental attachment (mother attachment and father attachment), the dependent variable is romantic social interest, and the mediating variable is alexithymia.

Methods

Participants

The present study adopted a purposive sample method to recruit 305 young adults between the ages of 18–25 years old from Sri Lanka. Participants met the criteria of (a) Sri Lankan nationality, (b) proficient in basic English language, (c) proficient in basic computer literacy for answering surveys, and (d) not diagnosed with a mental disorder.

According to Table 1, the majority of participants were females (n = 194, 63.6%), followed by males (n = 107, 35.1%), with a small proportion preferring not to disclose their gender (n = 4, 1.3%). The mean age of participants was M = 22.26 years (SD = 2.19), with ages ranging from 18 to 25 years. The largest age group comprised participants aged 25 years (n = 73, 23.91%), followed by those aged 22 years (n = 50, 16.4%), 23 years (n = 44, 14.4%), and 21 years (n = 44, 14.4%).

Demographic data of respondents.

The majority of participants identified as Sinhalese (n = 230, 75.4%), followed by Muslim (n = 40, 13.1%), Tamil (n = 19, 6.2%), Burgher (n = 9, 3.0%), Indian (n = 2, 0.7%), Malay (n = 2, 0.7%), Others (n = 2, 1%). Most participants reported being single (n = 183, 60.0%) followed by those in a relationship (n = 107, 35.1%), married (n = 9, 3.0%), and divorced (n = 2, 0.7%).

The majority indicated their primary caregiver as their mother (n = 174, 57.0%), followed by their father (n = 124, 40.7%). A small number reported other caregivers, including an uncle (n = 1, 0.3%), grandmother (n = 1, 0.3%), and others (n = 5, 1.6%). Most participants reported living with both parents (n = 187, 61.3%), followed by living with their mother only (n = 49, 16.1%), father only (n = 6, 2.0%), and living alone (n = 14, 4.6%). Participants diagnosed with a mental disorder were excluded from the study.

Measures

We utilized a survey questionnaire to collect data. All instruments were administered in English, a language which is widely used in Sri Lankan education61, and participants were required to have basic English proficiency to ensure comprehension of the questionnaire items. Within the survey, we asked basic demographic questions and used the following measures to measure the key variables:

Perth alexithymia questionnaire (PAQ)

The Perth Alexithymia Questionnaire (PAQ) includes 24 questions to assess self-reported feelings on how one perceives, and experiences emotions was administered62. It assesses difficulty in identifying one’s own feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and externally oriented thinking style, whereby one tends to not focus their attention on their emotions. Participants are asked to respond on a 7-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” to 7 indicating “strongly agree”. Higher scores indicate a higher level of alexithymia. Examples of items are: “I tend to ignore how I feel”, “When something bad happens, it’s hard for me to put into words how I’m feeling”, and “It’s strange for me to think about my emotions”. PAQ demonstrated a satisfactory reliability alpha value of 0.945 in the present study.

The inventory of parent and peer attachment (IPPA)

The Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment (IPPA)63 contains 75 items that assess attachment security and insecurity across three subscales: (1) Trust, which refers to an individual’s belief that their parents and peers understand and respect their needs and desires; (2) Communication, which refers to an individual’s belief that their parents and peers are sensitive to and responsive to their emotional states and evaluates the quality of involvement and verbal communication with them; and (3) Alienation, which refers to an individual’s feelings of isolation, anger, and detachment depicted in attachment relationships with parents and peers. The present study utilized only the parent attachment scale to measure parental attachment, both paternal and maternal attachment separately. The inventory includes a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (nearly never or never) to 5 (almost always or always). Higher scores indicate higher attachment. Some examples of the items are as follows, “I wish I had a different mother”, “My mother accepts me as I am”, “When I am angry about something, my father tries to be understanding”, and “I trust my father”. The IPPA demonstrated a satisfactory reliability alpha value of 0.947 and 0.947 for mother and father attachment respectively in this study.

Adlerian social interest scale-romantic relationship (ASIS-RR)

The Adlerian Social Interest Scale-Romantic Relationship (ASIS-RR)60 is a 24-item self-report questionnaire with a 5-point Likert scale with 1 indicating strongly disagree to 5 indicating strongly agree. The scale assesses an individual’s prosocial orientation within romantic contexts, including dimensions such as friendliness, empathy, cooperation, tolerance, nurturance, constructive independence, and identification in a romantic relationship. Higher scores indicate higher levels of romantic social interest. Example items include, “I can sacrifice to promote my partner’s growth” and “I show verbal affection to my partner,” as well as reverse-scored items such as “Men/women cannot be trusted”, and “I prefer orgiastic sexuality rather than a personalized love.” Participants who are not currently in a romantic relationship were asked to respond based on their general perceptions of the items, instead of specific towards someone, to ensure suitability of items for both individuals who are currently not in or in a relationship. The scale demonstrated a satisfactory reliability alpha value of 0.850 in the present study.

Procedure

The study obtained ethical approval prior to the study and data collection. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. An information sheet detailing the study was given and informed consent was obtained from the participants prior to the study and all participation was voluntary. Participants were recruited through online platforms, including social media channels such as WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook, where the survey link was distributed within personal and academic networks. The survey was administered online, and all items were set as mandatory; therefore, participants were required to complete all questions before submission. As a result, the dataset comprised fully completed responses only. Data were screened prior to analysis to ensure quality. Participants were provided with a food delivery voucher worth LKR 400 as an incentive for participating in the study.

Data analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 26 was used for data entry and analysis. A descriptive analysis including Pearson correlation analysis and independent samples t-tests were carried out to provide an overview for the data. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among the study variables. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to examine gender differences across the main variables. Lastly, mediation analyses were conducted using PROCESS macro64, version 4) for SPSS. All statistical tests were conducted using two-tailed significance levels (p < 0.05). Prior to analysis, the data were screened for outliers and adherence to statistical assumptions. Only two cases were identified as outliers via boxplots and excluded from subsequent analyses. Correlational analyses were conducted using pairwise deletion, allowing for the inclusion of all available data. Meanwhile, mediation analyses using PROCESS macro employed listwise deletion, resulting in a final sample size of n = 303.

Mediation analysis

A mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS macro (Model 464) to examine whether alexithymia mediates the relationship between mother attachment and romantic social interest.

Mother attachment significantly predicted alexithymia, b = − 0.2143, t = − 2.66, p < 0.05, 95% CI [− 0.3728, − 0.0559]. Alexithymia, in turn, significantly predicted romantic social interest, b = − 0.1207, t = − 6.32, p < 0.05, 95% CI [− 0.1583, − 0.0831] (see Table 3). The total effect of mother attachment on romantic social interest was not significant, b = 0.0493, p = 0.083, nor was the direct effect, b = 0.0234, p = 0.387. Nevertheless, the result indicated a significant indirect effect via alexithymia, b = 0.0259, 95% CI [0.0061, 0.0486].

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the mediating role of alexithymia on parental attachment and romantic social interest. The correlational analysis depicted that there is no significant relationship between mother attachment and romantic social interest and between father attachment and romantic social interest, when examined at bivariate level. However, a significant negative relationship between mother attachment and alexithymia, between father attachment and alexithymia, and between alexithymia and romantic social interest, were observed which is consistent with previous research conducted in this area12,38,68.

Mediation analysis revealed that alexithymia has an indirect effect in the relationship between parental attachment and romantic social interest, for both mother and father attachment. Accordingly, the findings indicate a significant indirect effect via alexithymia indicating that alexithymia plays a prominent role in this pathway. This suggests that the quality of attachment young adults have with their parents may influence their romantic social interest indirectly through the development of alexithymia traits. In other words, individuals with insecure or less optimal parental attachments may be more likely to develop difficulties in identifying and expressing emotions (i.e., alexithymia traits), which in turn may reduce their engagement in romantic social interactions.

This study emphasizes that parents play a vital role in forming a child’s characteristics, which in turn may affect potential romantic relationships due to formation of secure or insecure attachment of an individual69. Research has indicated that romantic social interest appears to be a predictor of relationship satisfaction70. Couples’ ability to adjust as spouses and partners and their capacity to respect one another’s feelings and wills are associated with the level of romantic social interest and such individuals can put their own interests aside to work in harmony with their partners71. Hence, if romantic social interest is impacted, individuals may be unable to have meaningful and healthy relationships.

Furthermore, individuals with more secure attachments appear to invest more effort in their relationships than those who are insecurely attached15,72. Research indicates that individuals with higher levels of alexithymia are more insecurely attached73, such individuals often suffer in their relationships as they are unable to express themselves and recognize the feelings of their partner leading to dissatisfying relationships45. Thus, highlighting the role of parental attachment in early childhood to foster a safe environment for children to express needs and feelings to form a secure attachment.

Although previous studies have consistently noted the role of mother attachment on adult romantic relationships, there were conflicting findings mainly denoting no significant relationship on the role of father attachment impacting adult romantic relationships31,74. The present study’s findings denoted the indirect effect of alexithymia on the relationship of father attachment on romantic social interest. These findings may be understood within the broader sociocultural context of Sri Lanka, which has been described as influenced by patriarchal norms that shape gender roles and interpersonal dynamics75. In such contexts, fathers are often positioned as authority figures and primary role models for social behaviour, which may influence how emotional awareness and expression are developed and subsequently related to individuals’ orientations towards romantic relationships. However, as the majority of the participants in the study were female, this should be taken into account when interpreting the findings, as female participants may experience and interpret paternal relationships, emotional socialisation, and expectations regarding romantic involvement differently due to gender-specific norms and socialisation processes within patriarchal contexts.

Beyond individual psychological factors, broader sociocultural contexts may also shape emotional expression and relational patterns. Culture affects many tenets of an individual’s life such as their behaviours, perceptions, and interpersonal dynamics. Women in such societies face challenges related to autonomy and empowerment to the extent that in collectivistic societies, parents voice out preferences on behalf of their children whereby the child may not fully grasp their own sense of autonomy76. Accordingly, the patriarchal nature of the country may influence the development of the parental attachment formed, thus influencing the development of higher levels of alexithymia traits and in turn, affect the expression of romantic social interest. A study found that females are more likely to be impacted by father attachment in their romantic relationships (Gunawardhana and Schuck 2021)52. Nevertheless, individuals who display insecure attachment styles do not necessarily need to remain as insecurely attached in their relationships. Insecurely attached individuals may feel loved, valued, and cared for when they engage in security-enhancing interactions, which can reinforce a cycle of security that eventually makes them feel more fundamentally secure in their relationships77. This is especially true when these interactions are routinely repeated over time in adulthood22.

The observed findings may be understood within the broader sociocultural context of Sri Lanka, which has been described as relatively collectivistic and influenced by traditional gender norms. Such cultural dynamics may shape interpersonal expectations and patterns of emotional expression within relationships. However, these interpretations should be approached with caution, as the present study did not directly examine cultural or gender-based differences in detail. Future research incorporating gender-stratified analyses or culturally sensitive measures would help to further clarify these relationships.

According to Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build cycle, positive emotions such as happiness, interest, satisfaction, and love expand individuals’ immediate thought-action repertoires and foster lasting resources across social, cognitive, and psychological domains78. Within this framework, secure parental attachment plays a pivotal role by providing consistent emotional support that helps individuals identify and articulate feelings, thereby reducing alexithymia traits. With diminished difficulties in recognizing and expressing emotions, individuals are more capable of engaging in open and responsive interactions, which enhances their capacity to experience and sustain positive emotions in close relationships. Consequently, secure parental attachment might function not only to support emotional clarity but also to strengthen positive romantic and social interest, as individuals are better equipped to build intimacy, trust, and satisfaction in adult relationships. Supported by literature, the relational deficit of alexithymia may be lessened if people who have high levels of alexithymia can be trained to cultivate more affectionate behaviours, potentially enhancing their ability to establish and sustain relationships79.

Maintaining a balance within relationships is crucial; persistent role imbalances between partners where one consistently plays the role of caretaker, can compromise relationship quality and stability22. Although some individuals may exhibit higher levels of alexithymia, it remains important for partners to accept each other’s shortcomings and differences. Relationship distress often arises not simply from the presence of incompatibilities but from how individuals emotionally react to those differences80. These findings highlight the importance of self reflection and adaptive relational patterns in maintaining healthy relationships. It is also important to consider that a substantial portion of the sample comprised individuals who were single. While this may limit the extent to which findings can be generalised to active romantic relationships, romantic social interest reflects an individual’s attitudes, tendencies, and predispositions toward romantic engagement, rather than solely behaviours within an ongoing relationship. Therefore, even among individuals who are single, the construct of romantic social interest reflects underlying orientations toward intimacy, empathy, and relational investment, making it applicable to both single individuals and those in relationships. Nevertheless, future research may benefit from comparing individuals who are in romantic relationships with those who are not, to further validate these findings.

Implications

The present study contributes to the understanding of parental attachment and its influence on romantic social interest among young adults, with a specific focus on the underexplored Sri Lankan context. The findings highlight the importance of raising awareness about alexithymia in order to strengthen parent–child and partner relationships, thereby fostering more satisfying and meaningful connections. Clinically, these insights can help practitioners tailor their communication strategies when working with clients who exhibit high levels of alexithymia. By adopting approaches that accommodate difficulties in identifying, describing, and understanding emotions, clinicians can build stronger therapeutic alliances and provide more effective care that supports individuals in expressing their thoughts and feelings.

Most existing research on alexithymia, parental attachment, and romantic relationships has been conducted in Western contexts; thus, this study contributes novel insights within an Asian setting, where the impact of alexithymia has been less explored. These findings offer valuable guidance for practitioners, social workers, and counselors in designing interventions that promote healthier communication and deeper understanding in both parent–child and partner relationships among young adults.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationships among parental attachment, alexithymia and romantic social interest. However, examining indirect effects using cross-sectional data can serve as a practical alternative under resource constraints, as it is more cost-effective and enables preliminary testing of mediation models when longitudinal designs are not feasible81. Accordingly, the mediation should be interpreted as an indirect relationship consistent with the theoretical framework rather than evidence of a causal pathway. Therefore, alternative explanations remain possible, including reverse directionality (e.g., alexithymia influencing perceptions of attachment) or reciprocal relationships over time. In future, a longitudinal study is needed to establish temporal precedence and clarify directionality, especially in examining parental attachment variables.

The study relied exclusively on self-report measures, and responses may therefore have been influenced by social desirability bias. To mitigate this concern, participant anonymity was ensured, and respondents were reassured that their data would be analysed only at the group level, such that individual identities could not be identified. Besides, the key variables examined in this study have been widely assessed using self-report measures in prior research, supporting the suitability of the usage of self-report measures.

We also acknowledge that although English is widely used in higher education in Sri Lanka, administering English-language instruments in a context where English is not the first language may not be optimal. Nevertheless, the measures demonstrated satisfactory reliability in the present sample, and the instruments were reviewed by a researcher who is familiar with the local language to ensure appropriate use of language within the local community.

As our data collection is conducted among populations who understand English, this may result in samples that are more educated or reside in urban areas, which could limit the generalisability of findings. Furthermore, while certain demographic variables were considered, other potentially relevant confounding factors were not included in the analyses, which may have influenced the observed relationships. Additionally, the exclusion of participants with mental disorders was based on self-report rather than clinical verification, which may limit the accuracy of this criterion.

Lastly, the predominance of female respondents also introduces potential gender bias, as differences in coping and interpersonal styles may shape the interpretation of findings. Moreover, the role of fathers in influencing romantic social interest may not be fully captured, given that many participants identified their mothers as their primary caregivers. The high proportion of single participants may limit the generalisability of the findings. While respondents were instructed to base their answers on general perceptions on romantic relationships, such responses may reflect dispositional or hypothetical tendencies rather than dynamics within active romantic relationships. Future research should address these limitations to provide a more nuanced understanding of the variables under study.

In conclusion, this study enhances our understanding towards the multifaceted nature of relationships and provides a foundation for future research to expand upon, especially in the area of parental attachment, alexithymia, and romantic social interest. Its implications are especially meaningful within the Sri Lankan context, where it enhances understanding of attachment dynamics in a culturally diverse setting. By examining the interplay between parental attachment, alexithymia, and romantic social interest, the study offers a nuanced perspective that advances the field and deepens our insight into the complexity of human relationships.
