ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale reproductive health vulnerabilities are influenced by socio-demographic factors and domestic power imbalances. In Indonesia, investigating how relational empowerment affects sexual health outcomes remains a critical public health necessity.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the association between sexual autonomy, socio-demographic determinants, and self-reported sexually transmitted infection (STI) symptoms among coupled women in Indonesia.MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted using nationally representative data from the 2017 Indonesia Demographic and Health Survey (IDHS). The analytical sample comprised 28,264 currently married or cohabiting coupled women. A composite index for sexual autonomy was constructed based on standardized sexual negotiation indicators. Survey-weighted binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to calculate adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe multivariate analysis demonstrated a significant association between sexual autonomy and self-reported STIs. Women with high sexual autonomy had 1.51 times higher odds of reporting STI symptoms than those with low sexual autonomy (AOR = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.23-1.84). Rather than indicating greater biological risk, this association suggest a symptom disclosure effect related to greater health awareness. In contrast, structural vulnerabilities independently reduced protective capacity; lower decision-making autonomy (AOR = 1.14), primary education (AOR = 1.29), and a strong justification of intimate partner violence (AOR = 2.08) were all significantly associated with higher odds of experiencing and reporting STIs.ConclusionThe association between high individual sexual autonomy and elevated STI symptom reporting highlights a health disclosure mechanism rather than biological disease incidence. Among coupled Indonesian women, reproductive health outcomes are deeply shaped by gender power relations. Public health and community nursing interventions should therefore prioritize addressing structural domestic coercion, normalizing the rejection of violence, and creating safe environments that support health transparency.Keywords: sexual autonomy, sexually transmitted infections, women’s empowerment, Indonesia

BODY
Background

Millions of lives across the globe continue to be quietly devastated by pathogens transmitted through sexual contact. The scale is staggering. In 2019 alone, the Global Burden of Disease study recorded roughly 769 million incident cases of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) worldwide (Fu et al., 2022). When we consider only the four primary curable pathogens, the annual toll remains alarming, with more than 370 million new infections (Rowley et al., 2019; Unemo et al., 2017; WHO, 2025b). Developing countries, the heavy epicenter of this crisis, see women endure profound reproductive morbidities, losing years of healthy life to complications that are entirely preventable (Masanja et al., 2021; Mayaud & Mabey, 2004).

While the global burden of STIs remains substantial, the epidemiological landscape in the Southeast Asian region, particularly in Indonesia, requires urgent academic scrutiny. Recent evidence indicates that in 2021, Indonesia recorded approximately 34 million incident STI cases, positioning it behind only China (148 million cases) and India (94 million cases) globally, and making it the nation with the highest STI prevalence in Southeast Asia at 12.3%. Furthermore, longitudinal data reveal an alarming escalation of 90% to 130% in STI incidence within Indonesia from 1990 to 2021 (Deng et al., 2025). The highest prevalence disproportionately clustered among individuals within the productive age demographic (Lumbantoruan et al., 2025). Beneath these broad national statistics, coupled women face a disproportionate, invisible vulnerability. They often suffer from severe, silent complications, ranging from pelvic inflammatory disease to irreversible infertility (Siregar et al., 2021). Indonesia vividly exemplifies this struggle. Despite ongoing public health interventions, national STI rates continue to rise, pointing to a largely hidden epidemic (Indonesia Ministry of Health, 2021). This massive epidemiological burden underscores the critical need to investigate underlying relational determinants, such as sexual autonomy, among coupled women within the country.

At the absolute core of this vulnerability lies the battle for sexual autonomy. It is a fundamental human right, not a mere behavioral metric. Sexual health, by definition, requires a positive approach to sexuality—one strictly free from coercion, discrimination, and violence (WHO, 2025a). In practice, sexual autonomy embodies a woman’s agency to navigate intimate relationships: her unassailable right to refuse unwanted sex and her leverage to mandate contraceptive use. Yet, the architecture of STI epidemics is deeply rooted in underlying sociocultural determinants that systematically strip women of this agency (Aral, 2002). Beyond this, for millions of Indonesian women, asserting sexual autonomy remains a theoretical luxury. Submissive roles for wives are forced by deeply embedded gender norms, obliterating their capacity to negotiate safer sex or refuse intercourse—even when they suspect their partners of infidelity (Puspita, 2017; Simbolon & Budiarti, 2020).

What actively dismantles this agency? The foundation of sexual vulnerability rests on intersecting pillars: socioeconomic disenfranchisement and the normalization of physical violence. Relational power is heavily dictated by economic dependence. When trapped in poverty or deprived of formal education, women rely entirely on male partners for survival. This dependency wipes out bargaining power and blocks access to healthcare. Additionally, intimacy is weaponized by deep-seated cultural norms. Environments that justify physical violence against wives create a coercive atmosphere built on fear (Kishor, 2012). This violence directly restricts protective behaviors. It makes demanding a condom dangerous, which ultimately exploits biological susceptibility and violently amplifies exposure to STI pathogens.

Isolated socio-demographic variables or narrow indicators of female sexual autonomy have long been examined. However, a glaring empirical void persists in the Indonesian context. Mapping a composite score of sexual autonomy, systematically stratified into Low, Medium, and High tiers within a nationally representative framework, has received little attention. Existing models fail to show how composite autonomy interacts with factors like household wealth and violence normalization to influence STI incidences. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the composite sexual autonomy of Indonesian women and its association with socio-demographic factors and STI incidences using a nationally representative dataset.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

Our analysis was based on the 2017 Indonesia Demographic and Health Survey (IDHS), a nationally representative dataset that provides a comprehensive lens into the reproductive health and socio-demographic realities of Indonesian women (National Population Family Planning Board et al., 2018). Utilizing a cross-sectional, observational framework, the IDHS employed a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling technique, which captures Indonesia’s geographic and cultural diversity, supporting broadly generalizable public health inferences. The secondary datasets in this study are available from the DHS Program repository via https://dhsprogram.com/data/Using-Datasets-for-Analysis.cfm upon registration and institutional approval.

Sample/Participants

The initial demographic repository for currently married or cohabiting coupled women within the 2017 IDHS encompassed 34,467 respondents. For this analysis, a complete-case approach was adopted. Observations containing missing values across the target explanatory and outcome indicators were systematically excluded, yielding a final analytical cohort of 28,264 women with fully completed data vectors. This listwise deletion approach presents a negligible risk of selection bias or parameter distortion, as the excluded cases constitute a minor fraction of the total sample, thereby preserving the statistical power and national representativeness of the weighted multivariate frameworks.

The inclusion criteria were strictly restricted to currently married or cohabiting coupled women. This restriction was structurally necessitated by the analytical model, which incorporates key dyadic and partner-specific covariates, such as the partner’s educational attainment, occupational profile, and the inter-spousal age gap. Consequently, unmarried sexually active women, divorced individuals, and widows were excluded, as these variables are systematically missing for these demographic groups within the IDHS framework.

Variable Operationalization

Outcome Variable: To capture the burden of infection, the primary dependent variable was operationalized as the self-reported incidence of STIs. Drawing on established epidemiological methodologies for DHS data, women were classified as reporting an STI (coded as 1) if they reported having a genital sore, ulcer, abnormal/bad-smelling discharge, or received a formal STI diagnosis in the 12 months preceding the interview (Adu et al., 2022; Jubayer Biswas et al., 2022; Kishor, 2012). Those reporting no such symptoms were coded as 0.

Primary Explanatory Variable: Rather than relying on isolated indicators, we constructed a composite measure of sexual autonomy to better reflect the multidimensional and often complex nature of female autonomy (Adetutu et al., 2024; Atteraya et al., 2014). Following established empirical frameworks, this index synthesizes critical dimensions of sexual negotiation, adapted from previous studies (Adu et al., 2022; Crissman et al., 2012; Memiah et al., 2019; Midaksa et al., 2022; Solanke et al., 2022; Viswan et al., 2017). The composite measures a woman’s ability to refuse sex, negotiate condom use, and refuse if she suspects her partner has an STI or another woman. Validation confirmed its reliability, with internal consistency (item-total alpha) coefficients from 0.678 to 0.697, exceeding the threshold. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation extracted two distinct components with eigenvalues above 1.0, cumulatively explaining 59.79% of the total variance. Component 1 captures direct behavioral negotiation, while Component 2 measures normative boundary setting. Based on their cumulative scores across these domains, women were stratified into three hierarchical tiers of autonomy: Low (least autonomous), Moderate, and High (most autonomous). To ensure the robustness of the self-reported measures, a sensitivity analysis was performed by removing individual items from the Sexual Autonomy Index. The model estimations remained stable across coding scenarios, confirming the index’s reliability.

Covariates: Recognizing that sexual health is embedded within broader structural determinants, our models adjusted for various covariates. These included individual and household characteristics, such as age, autonomy (decision-making), educational attainment for respondent and partner, employment, residence, and the household wealth index. To address cultural and behavioral confounders, we also included HIV/AIDS knowledge, media exposure, spousal age disparities, and attitudes toward the justification of physical violence (Jubayer Biswas et al., 2022; Nankinga et al., 2016).

To isolate the independent effects of sexual autonomy, several socio-demographic and behavioral covariates from the 2017 IDHS were integrated into the analytical framework. Respondents’ age was recorded as the chronological age at their last birthday and grouped into four ordinal cohorts (15–24, 25–34, 35–44, and 45–49 years), while parity was classified as nullipara, primipara, or multipara. Formal education levels for both the respondent and their partner were ordered into four distinct institutional tiers (no education, primary, secondary, and higher education). Employment status during the preceding 12 months was operationalized as a binary nominal variable distinguished employed (coded as 0) from unemployed (coded as 1) individuals. Marital dynamics were captured using early marriage thresholds, distinguishing entry ages of >18 years (coded as 0) from ≤18 years (coded as 1), and cohabitation status, which indicated whether the partner currently resided in the same household (yes, coded as 0; no, coded as 1). The chronological age gap between partners was categorized into four tiers: ≤ the respondent’s age, 1–5 years older, 6–10 years older, and >10 years older. Household socio-economic status was controlled using the standardized DHS wealth index quintiles (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest) derived via principal component analysis. Place of residence was operationalized as a binary nominal variable that classified households as urban (coded as 1) or rural (coded as 0) based on Statistics Indonesia (BPS) administrative criteria that evaluated population density, the percentage of agricultural households, and local access to urban infrastructure. General decision-making autonomy was operationalized as a binary index that synthesized economic, healthcare, and social choices, stratified into high (score ≥ sample median; coded as 0) and low (score 2 sources (coded as 0) and low access for ≤ sources (coded as 1)—alongside an assessment of comprehensive HIV/AIDS and STI knowledge based on 10 technical items, stratified into high knowledge (score ≥ sample median; coded as 0) and low knowledge (score < sample median; coded as 1). Multicollinearity among the adjusted socioeconomic covariates (including respondents’ education, partners’ education, and wealth index) was formally assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF); all predictors yielded values below ten, indicating no significant collinearity in the final model.

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software version 15.0. To account for the complex, multi-stage stratified cluster sampling design of the IDHS, sample weights, stratification, and primary sampling units (PSUs) were integrated into the analytical models using the survey (svy) command prefix. Data analysis involved three steps. First, univariate analysis was conducted using survey-weighted frequency distributions to describe the baseline socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents. Second, bivariate analysis was performed using design-adjusted Chi-square tests to evaluate the proportion and unadjusted associations of self-reported STI symptoms across all explanatory variables. Third, a survey-weighted multivariate binary logistic regression model was constructed. All explanatory variables and covariates were included in the initial multivariate model simultaneously, regardless of their bivariate statistical significance. This approach was adopted based on prior literature indicating their epidemiological and theoretical relevance to the outcome. Results from the multivariate models are reported as Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). To facilitate precise parameter estimation, multi-categorical predictors were integrated into multivariate models using dummy coding, with the baseline serving as the reference category.

Ethical Consideration

This study received formal ethical approval from the Research and Community Engagement Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Public Health, Universitas Indonesia, under protocol number: Ket-650/UN2.F10.D11/PPM.00.02/2024. Secondary data for this analysis were obtained from the 2017 IDHS with official permission granted by ICF International. The original DHS data collection complied with all requirements for the protection of human subjects as outlined in 45 CFR 46, as approved by the ICF Institutional Review Board. All datasets were strictly de-identified by the data provider to ensure the confidentiality and anonymity of respondents, households, and communities, preventing any possibility of identifying individual participants.

The survey-weighted multivariate logistic regression model demonstrated a highly robust overall fit parameter (F(31, 1873) = 8.50, Prob > F < 0.0001. After controlling for all socio-demographic factors, a clear, significant non-linear effect was identified for female sexual autonomy. After adjusting for all covariates, sexual autonomy emerged as a significant independent predictor of self-reported STI, with women reporting high sexual autonomy having 51% higher odds of STI reporting compared to those with low autonomy (AOR = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.23–1.84; p < 0.001), while moderate autonomy showed a borderline non-significant association (AOR = 1.22; p = 0.053). Beyond sexual autonomy, several other factors independently predicted STI reporting. Younger age was the strongest predictor, as women aged 15–24 years had more than double the odds of reporting an STI compared to those aged 45–49 years (AOR = 2.08; p < 0.001), with a clear age gradient observed across age groups. Low decision-making autonomy was associated with a 14% increase in STI odds (AOR = 1.14; p = 0.013). Lower education significantly increased risk, as women with primary and secondary education had 29% and 34% higher odds, respectively, compared to those with higher education (p = 0.007 and p < 0.001).

Low HIV/AIDS and STI knowledge also raised the likelihood of STI reporting by 26% (AOR = 1.26; p < 0.001). Acceptance of physical violence against wives was strongly associated with women who agreed, having 56% higher odds (AOR = 1.56; p < 0.001), and those who strongly agreed having more than double the odds (AOR = 2.08; p < 0.001) compared to those who disagreed. Urban residence was associated with 37% higher STI odds after adjustment (AOR = 1.37; p = 0.022), reversing the protective pattern seen in bivariate analysis. Women in the poorest wealth quintile had 21% higher odds than those in the richest quintile (AOR = 1.21; p = 0.036), while nulliparous women had higher odds than primiparous women (AOR = 0.80; p = 0.006) and employed women had higher odds than unemployed women (AOR = 0.85; p = 0.001). A significant interaction was observed between high sexual autonomy and urban residence (AOR = 0.74; p = 0.044), indicating that the effect of sexual autonomy on STI reporting differed by place of residence. In contrast, early marriage, media exposure, partner’s education, age gap with partner, and living together with partner were no longer significant after adjustment.

Discussion

The multivariate analysis revealed a crucial finding regarding women’s reproductive health in Indonesia. Women with high sexual autonomy had 1.51 times greater odds of reporting symptoms of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) compared to those with low sexual autonomy (AOR = 1.51; 95% CI: 1.23–1.84). This aligns with research in sub-Saharan Africa. This positive correlation does not indicate an increase in biological risk. Instead, it represents a reporting disclosure phenomenon. Regarding potential omitted variable bias, where autonomous women are assumed to have more sexual partners, the Indonesian cultural context emphasizes strict monogamy for wives within marriage. Thus, sexual autonomy reflects a capacity for domestic negotiation to dismantle the “culture of silence.” High reporting among women with high sexual autonomy indicates health transparency and accessibility, not high-risk extramarital behavior (Ibitoye & Ajagunna, 2021). Although these data rely on self-reported symptoms without clinical confirmation, they reflect higher health literacy and body awareness. Autonomous women possess the cognitive agency to recognize urogenital anomalies and the assertive courage to report them. Less empowered women tend to normalize such symptoms as shame or biological destiny. Empowered women overcome the deep-seated stigma associated with sexual health, making them far more likely to report infections and seek timely treatment (Nankinga et al., 2016).

Furthermore, the directionality of reporting bias warrants critical attention. Women exhibiting higher sexual autonomy are inherently more health-literate and empowered, which increases their propensity to recognize and voluntarily disclose reproductive symptoms during national surveys. Consequently, ‘willingness to report’ acts as a prominent confounding variable that may artificially inflate the reported symptom rates among autonomous women, thereby masking the true underlying relationship between empowerment and biological STI prevalence. Utilizing self-reported syndromic data predominantly captures proactive health-seeking behaviors and symptom recognition rather than purely biological disease incidence. Because the demographic instrument restricts this module to married or cohabiting individuals, these findings apply strictly to coupled women in Indonesia, excluding unmarried populations who face different structural dynamics. The strongest structural predictor in this model was attitudes toward physical violence.

Women who strongly agreed with justifying intimate partner violence (IPV) had twice the likelihood of reporting an STI (AOR= 2.08). This illustrates the reality of coercive sexuality, consistent with studies by Kishor (2012). In Bangladesh, women who justify wife-beating internalize patriarchal norms that strip them of bodily autonomy, coercing them into unprotected encounters (Jubayer Biswas et al., 2022). In line with this result, acceptance of beating a wife for refusing to have sex with her husband was associated with STIs in all countries in South East Asia (including Indonesia) except Myanmar (Yamani et al., 2025). Beyond direct causal risk, this correlation indicates marriage market sorting. Women permissive toward violence tend to become trapped with partners who have high-risk behavioral profiles outside the home. Within a domestic ecosystem characterized by intimidation, fear paralyzes wives’ bargaining power to negotiate safe sex. This exploits women’s anatomical vulnerability to pathogen exposure (Hayati et al., 2011; Solanke et al., 2022).

General decision-making autonomy was also significantly associated with reproductive health. Women with low autonomy had 1.14 times greater odds of reporting an STI (AOR = 1.14). This mirrors findings from Ethiopia and Nepal, confirming that women’s marginalization in daily domestic decisions leads to a loss of bargaining power to protect themselves (Asabu, 2021; Atteraya et al., 2014). Decision-making autonomy reflects the power distribution map within marriage. Women excluded from daily decision-making typically lose their bargaining power entirely, including negotiating condom use or refusing risky intercourse. Primary (AOR = 1.29) and secondary (AOR = 1.34) education were independently associated with higher STI reporting compared to higher education. This study avoids claims of formal statistical mediation. Instead, it views these variables as intertwined paths or structural overlays. Lower education often coincides with economic dependence in the lowest wealth quintile (AOR = 1.21) and lower technical health literacy (AOR = 1.26) for low knowledge) (Adetutu et al., 2024; Siregar et al., 2021). Consistent with Resource Theory, financial independence within the richest wealth quintile provides women with equitable bargaining power (Jubayer Biswas et al., 2022). In Ethiopia, women from richer households are significantly more likely to refuse risky encounters than their poorer counterparts (Asabu, 2021). In Indonesia, low socioeconomic status drives women into structural vulnerabilities, forcing economic dependencies that elevate STI risks (Refti, 2018).

Furthermore, women with low HIV/AIDS and STI knowledge had 1.26 times greater odds of reporting STIs or symptoms compared to women with high knowledge. This aligns with studies in Indonesia (Siregar et al., 2021), Nigeria (Adetutu et al., 2024), and research by Osborne et al. (2023). Low health literacy directly increases women’s vulnerability to STIs. This lack of knowledge reinforces authoritative husband power amidst cultural constructions that treat discussions of sexuality as taboo (Ningsih, 2022). A unique finding emerged regarding the interaction between sexual autonomy and place of residence. The effect of autonomy on STI reporting weakened in urban areas (Interaction AOR = 0.74). This highlights the uniqueness of Indonesia’s local context. Institutional advantages in urban areas, such as the density of health facilities and media exposure, reduce the social friction in recognizing health problems. In rural settings, a woman’s individual agency acts as the primary catalyst for overcoming structural gender barriers. Only highly autonomous rural women possess the relative empowerment needed to recognize and openly vocalize reproductive tract anomalies.

Conversely, in urban centers, individual autonomy becomes less dominant. Urban environments offer superior institutional advantages, including higher baseline health infrastructure, denser geographic proximity to clinics, and broader exposure to public health campaigns. Urban women face lower social friction when recognizing or reporting health problems. This attenuates the statistical weight of individual sexual agency compared to rural counterparts. Global studies from Bangladesh or Ethiopia serve as benchmarks, but Indonesia’s geospatial landscape provides distinct reporting dynamics. Variables such as age and employment status further shape the risk profile. Younger women (15–24 years) showed more than double the odds of reporting symptoms (AOR = 2.08) compared to the oldest cohort. This aligns with research in Indonesia by Lumbantoruan et al. (2025), confirming higher biological and behavioral vulnerability among younger groups. Physiologically, the reproductive organ maturity of older women provides better biological protection (Simbolon & Budiarti, 2020). Genital hygiene is also better maintained by older women due to greater experience regarding personal hygiene (Karahan et al., 2025). Conversely, unemployed women (AOR = 0.85) demonstrated a lower reporting risk. In Lampung Province, employment status determines exposure to risky external environments (Refti, 2018). Limited social mobility causes the source of transmission to be uniform and unidirectional, originating solely from husbands’ extramarital behaviors. Additionally, many employed women work in the informal sector, such as trading or farming. This results in limited access to adequate sanitation and reduced time for personal hygiene (Sundari et al., 2025). Overall, the non-significance of early marriage and media exposure conveys a clear sociological message. The problem of STIs in Indonesia is not merely an information deficit. It is a direct consequence of unequal gender power relations. From a public health nursing perspective, this underscores that the core nursing problem within the community is Ineffective Family Health Management, where deeply entrenched social norms paralyze women’s protective behaviors and structure the disclosure patterns of reproductive tract anomalies.

Limitations

The strengths of this study lie in the utilization of a robust, nationally representative dataset from the IDHS, ensuring high external validity to evaluate these complex vulnerabilities on a massive scale. However, structural limitations must be acknowledged. Primarily, the reliance on self-reported data for both sexual autonomy and STI symptoms render the study susceptible to reporting bias. Self-reported STI status lacks clinical verification. Genital ulcers or abnormal discharge lack diagnostic specificity and can be caused by non-venereal factors like trauma or Behçet’s disease. Furthermore, reporting a formal diagnosis requires healthcare access. Women with lower sexual autonomy often face barriers to health services, leading to systematic under-diagnosis. This lack of access may artificially distort the statistical associations. Stigma may also lead to underreporting among disempowered women, while empowered women might seemingly overreport relative to their peers (Adu et al., 2022; Kishor, 2012).

Additionally, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish definitive causal inferences due to the absence of temporal sequencing. Because both sexual autonomy and the presence of reproductive symptoms were measured simultaneously, a bidirectional relationship or reverse causality regarding reporting behavior cannot be entirely ruled out. While our framework positions sexual autonomy as a predictor of symptom disclosure, it is equally plausible that the chronic experience of distressing, self-reported reproductive symptoms could, over time, erode a woman’s interpersonal bargaining capacity and subsequently diminish her sexual autonomy within the domestic sphere. Furthermore, we acknowledge an inherent proxy-validity gap regarding the measurement of empowerment. The sexual autonomy index was constructed using available composite items from a secondary national survey rather than utilizing a dedicated, validated psychometric instrument such as the full Women’s Sexual Autonomy Scale. Consequently, this survey-derived proxy may not fully capture the broader, multidimensional nuances of sexual autonomy established in contemporary sexual health literature, which ideally encompasses broader sexual cultural scripting and communication (Willie et al., 2023).

We acknowledge that this study relies on data from the 2017 Indonesia Demographic and Health Survey (IDHS). Although this dataset reflects a pre-pandemic baseline, it remains the most recent, comprehensive, and legally accessible national-scale dataset available for analyzing the specific linkages between female sexual autonomy and self-reported STIs in Indonesia. While localized sub-national studies exist, they lack the nationwide representative scale and the specific, fully validated variables on domestic sexual negotiation that are essential for this multivariate analysis. Therefore, because subsequent national survey data are restricted from public dissemination, the 2017 IDHS framework currently provides the most reliable and empirically sound evidence available for understanding reproductive health power dynamics at the national level.

Our sample was strictly restricted to currently married or cohabiting coupled women. This decision intentionally excludes other high-risk populations, such as unmarried sexually active women, divorced individuals, and widows. This boundary was structurally dictated by the research design, which requires partner-specific covariates to analyze interpersonal dynamics. Future studies should explore alternative datasets to capture the reproductive health vulnerabilities of these uncoupled demographic groups. Lastly, this study did not perform advanced mathematical sensitivity metrics, such as E-value calculations to quantify residual unmeasured confounding. While exploratory testing confirmed that alternative grouping thresholds did not alter the directional significance of our core parameters, the absence of extensive sensitivity modeling remains a methodological constraint inherent to the secondary nature of the IDHS dataset.

Implications

Ultimately, these findings necessitate a shift in public health policy and clinical nursing practice. Traditional STI prevention programs in Indonesia that focus solely on clinical management and condom distribution are structurally inadequate if they ignore gender-based power dynamics. Public health models must integrate primary STI services with routine screening for intimate partner violence. This study clarifies findings within the Indonesian context. Higher sexual autonomy corresponds with greater odds of reporting STI symptoms (AOR = 1.51). This association is not a failure of independence. Instead, it indicates that empowered women possess the health literacy to recognize and the confidence to disclose symptoms that are typically hidden due to deep-seated social stigma. Conversely, the normalization of domestic violence remains the strongest structural predictor of reported symptoms (AOR = 2.08), fostering coercive sexuality that strips women of their capacity to negotiate for safe practices.

These findings establish a clear mandate for the nursing discipline, shifting the focus from individual-level clinical interventions to structural public health nursing interventions. From a community health perspective, the core nursing problem within this ecosystem is: Ineffective Family Health Management, driven by unequal gender power relations. Nurses in community, maternal, and reproductive health services must look beyond biological symptoms and systematically assess the relational dynamics of their patients. From a clinical nursing perspective, these findings demand a paradigm shift in reproductive health intake protocols, particularly within remote Indonesian clinics. Rather than relying solely on passive, patient-initiated symptom disclosure, community and primary care nurses must implement proactive, culturally sensitive screening environments. Intake interviews should utilize non-judgmental, indirect questioning techniques regarding sexual health to bypass the barriers of low sexual autonomy and stigma. Furthermore, nurses should integrate sexual empowerment counseling into routine maternal and reproductive health visits, creating a safe, confidential space that facilitates disclosure and early STI management among marginalized women. Reproductive nursing care must involve screening for both physical violence acceptance and sexual autonomy thresholds. By providing safe spaces for counseling and structural advocacy, community nurses can directly target the social and relational vulnerabilities that drive reproductive health disparities in Indonesia.

Conclusion

Higher sexual autonomy among coupled women in Indonesia is significantly linked to an increased likelihood of self-reporting STI symptoms, which reflects enhanced health disclosure and bodily awareness rather than an escalation in biological vulnerability. This reporting pattern is structurally bounded by spatial geography, with the autonomy effect becoming attenuated in urban centers. Concurrently, the independent and profound risks associated with poverty and the deeply entrenched normalization of domestic abuse underscore that individual female agency operates within rigid socioeconomic and normative constraints. Because this observational study demonstrates that unequal gender power relations and structural disadvantages independently drive the risk profile, national reproductive health strategies must move beyond basic information dissemination. Interventions should prioritize integrating structural gender-sensitization models into primary healthcare services, strengthening community-based legal protections against domestic violence, and expanding rural reproductive health infrastructure to lower the social friction of disclosure for marginalized women.
