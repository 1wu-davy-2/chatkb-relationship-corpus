ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResearch demonstrates a robust link between marriage and wealth. Wealth facilitates marriage, which then fosters wealth accumulation, resulting in significant net worth disparities between married and cohabiting couples. Does the decline of marriage and growth of cohabitation alter this relationship? Previous research suggests variable outcomes across societies and groups. Wealth gaps may persist (or even widen) where cohabitation emerges primarily due to economic uncertainty or as a prolonged stage preceding marriage. However, gaps might narrow substantially where cohabitation diffuses as an alternative to marriage. Using Canada as a comparative case, this paper explores these dynamics. It contrasts the province of Québec, where cohabitation expanded largely as an alternative to marriage among French‐speaking residents, with other provinces as well as Québec's anglophones, where cohabitation spread mostly as a preceding stage to marriage and due to economic insecurity. Leveraging data from Canada's Survey of Financial Security and the Longitudinal Administrative Databank, results indicate that marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps have narrowed substantially in French‐speaking Québec since the 1990s compared to other provinces and Québec's English‐speakers, in line with theoretical predictions. This change appears to hold for both men and women and to stem largely from weakened associations between wealth and marriage entry, alongside more modest reductions in the wealth gains of marriage among men in Québec. These results suggest that when marriage loses cultural centrality and is replaced by alternative union types, as it has in Québec, its link with wealth accumulation may weaken considerably.

BODY
Introduction

Because governments and employers increasingly shift responsibility for retirement security onto individuals, private wealth accumulation has become crucial for financial well‐being, particularly later in life (Pfeffer and Killewald 2018). A substantial body of social science research has identified marriage as a significant driver of wealth accumulation, with married couples typically acquiring more wealth compared to singles, but also cohabiting couples over their life‐course. This is because wealth facilitates marriage, while marriage contributes to further wealth accumulation (Addo and Lichter 2013; Frémeaux and Leturcq 2022; Kapelle and Lersch 2020; Lersch 2017; Niimi 2022). Yet, marriage has sharply declined in recent decades, replaced largely by cohabitation. Does the widespread shift toward cohabitation alter the historical association between marriage and wealth, in particular the marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps?

This matter remains largely unexplored, as previous studies on the evolution of marriage‐cohabitation gaps have primarily investigated other outcomes, such as infant health (Torche and Abufhele 2021), life‐satisfaction (Perelli‐Harris et al. 2019), gender equality (Yang 2025), and educational attainment (Perelli‐Harris et al. 2010; Wright 2019). Nonetheless, the question is compelling. Not only does it address marriage's intersection with inequalities in evolving intimacy landscapes, in addition, theories on the rise of cohabitation predict quite contrasting outcomes. On the one hand, scholars have attributed this conjugal transformation to soaring insecurity, as well as to marriage's evolution into a “capstone” event that is delayed by prolonged periods of cohabitation (Cherlin 2004; McLanahan 2004). As is detailed below, those perspectives suggest minimal changes to marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps in the process of cohabitation diffusion. However, in frameworks such as the Second Demographic Transition, cohabitation expands due to declining interest in traditional marriage and as an alternative to it (Lesthaeghe 2010). Such perspectives anticipate a significant weakening of the association between marriage and wealth, as I argue below.

Although those various explanatory theories have sometimes been presented as competing, recent research suggests that they are best viewed as ideal‐typical varieties of cohabitation diffusion patterns characterizing (more or less closely) trajectories in specific societies (Perelli‐Harris and Bernardi 2015). As a result, the repercussions of cohabitation's expansion on the marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps should not be uniform, nor universal. They should vary across societies where cohabitation spread primarily as a longer transitional stage toward marriage, those where it grew mostly due to insecurity, and those where it diffused more as an alternative to marriage. Acknowledging this point, this paper's objective and contribution is to explore whether the spread of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage may, as predicted, challenge marriage's long‐standing association with wealth and shrink marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps. To this end, I employ a comparative approach centered on a well‐documented case of cohabitation emerging largely as a normalized alternative to marriage: the Canadian province of Québec, particularly its French‐speaking community (Laplante and Fostik 2016; Le Bourdais and Lapierre‐Adamcyk 2004). The study contrasts this context with the three other most populous Canadian provinces (Ontario, Alberta, and British Columbia) and the English‐speaking group of Québec, where cohabitation is also institutionally recognized but has primarily diffused due to a mix of growing insecurity and its adoption as a longer prelude to marriage.

The analyses draw on five waves of the Survey of Financial Security (SFS) conducted between 1999 and 2019. I first test whether wealth gaps between cohabiting and married couples are generally narrower in Québec than in other provinces. Second, I explore whether Québec's weaker link between marriage and wealth can be attributed to its unique pattern of cohabitation diffusion (as an alternative to marriage) rather than to other factors, such as its generous social policies. To this end, I compare marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps among French and English‐Speakers within Québec (who are exposed to the same policies, but experienced different cohabitation diffusion trajectories) and the gaps between married and unpartnered people across provinces. Overall, the findings indicate that marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps have narrowed in Québec relative to other provinces since 1999, and that this divergence is likely driven by the province's diffusion of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage, not its social policies.

The SFS, however, has two key limitations: it captures wealth only at the household level, preventing a gender‐specific analysis, and its cross‐sectional design limits the ability to identify the mechanisms underlying Québec's shrinking wealth gaps by union type. To address these issues, I supplement the main analyses with data from the Longitudinal Administrative Databank (LAD), which offers high‐quality individual‐level, longitudinal data (2009–2022) on financial assets in Tax‐Free Savings Accounts (TFSAs), a major wealth component in Canada that is also strongly correlated with overall wealth levels, and on a number of other asset types such as workplace pension wealth. Those additional analyses suggest that marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps are narrower in Québec than other provinces among both men and women. They also indicate that this largely due to a reduced predictive power of wealth (and other socio‐economic indicators) for transitioning from cohabitation to marriage and to somewhat diminished wealth gains from marriage, particularly among men.

Overall, those findings suggest that when cohabitation spreads as an alternative to marriage, it can fundamentally reshape how marriage relates to wealth accumulation for both genders. This insight extends previous research by contextualizing the links between marriage and wealth; it highlights how macro‐social forces deeply shape their interplay and stresses that marriage does not carry intrinsic, universal benefits. The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows: I begin by reviewing existing research on why marriage is linked with wealth, then I outline theoretical perspectives on the spread of cohabitation and their implications for the marriage–wealth relationship. I next present the Canadian context used in the comparative analysis and develop hypotheses, followed by a description of data, methods, and results.

Why Are Married People Wealthier Than Cohabitors?

There is no shortage of evidence that married people own more wealth not just compared to singles but also cohabiting couples (Addo and Lichter 2013; Halpern‐Manners et al. 2015; Kapelle and Vidal 2022; Painter et al. 2015; Schmidt and Sevak 2006; Vespa and Painter 2011; Wilmoth and Koso 2002). This is true for both men and women in Germany (Kapelle and Lersch 2020; Lersch 2017), though findings are mixed elsewhere: in France, the marriage–wealth link among seniors appears only for women (Bonnet et al. 2023), whereas in Japan, it is not observed among women (Niimi 2022).

When explaining the wealth advantage of married couples, scholars have highlighted the dominant Western norms that value marriage and associate it with ideals of economic independence, stability, and prestige (Cherlin 2004; Gerstel and Sarkisian 2006; Oppenheimer 1994; Ruggles 2015). Marriage also remains the central institution for childrearing and meeting the increasing expectations for parental investments (Bandelj and Grigoryeva 2021). For all these reasons, marriage is a widely shared aspiration, and the decision to marry is partly one of timing based on the availability of economic resources. Indeed, people tend to wait to marry until they and a partner meet economic thresholds—the “marriage bar”—reflecting the financial and employment stability expected in marriage (Edin 2000; Oppenheimer 1988). While men's economic status has long shaped their marriage prospects (Kalmijn 2011), it now plays a similar role for women, given the prevalence of dual‐earner households (Sweeney 2002). In this context, wealth and other socio‐economic indicators associated with future wealth accumulation (e.g., income, education, occupational status, etc.) strongly predict entering stable marriages for both genders, according to several US studies (Eads and Tach 2016; Gibson‐Davis et al. 2018; Ishizuka 2018; Killewald et al. 2023; McDonald 2020; Schneider 2011). Wealth homogamy is also common (Lersch and Schunck 2023).

While much of the wealth gap between married and unmarried groups reflects this selection of wealthier individuals into marriage (Frémeaux and Leturcq 2022), it is also due to a positive effect of marriage on wealth (especially among men and white people), according to longitudinal studies (Kapelle and Lersch 2020; Lersch 2017). As a long‐term commitment, marriage encourages couples to engage in income pooling, joint investments, specialization, or other forms of coordination, which induce scale economies that ease savings and homeownership entry (Pepin 2019; Pugliese and Belleau 2022). By contrast, cohabitors share less due to weaker commitments (Hiekel et al. 2014). Married couples in many societies also benefit from institutional advantages (fiscal benefits, survivor pensions, etc.) that cohabitors do not have access to (Schechtl and Kapelle 2024), which provides the former with further wealth advantages.

In sum, cultural norms and institutions have created a feedback loop where marriage both requires and generates wealth, creating large wealth gaps between married and cohabiting people (as well as singles). Marriage in this context appears as both a marker of social class and, as Toft and Jarness (2021) argued, a closure strategy among the upper classes to protect and transmit wealth down their family lines (see also O'Brien 2024 on this point).

Theorizing the Impact of Growing Cohabitation on the Marriage–Wealth Link

Across Europe and North America, intimate relationships are undergoing major transformations, as marriage falls, and cohabitation rises (Sassler and Lichter 2020; Sobotka and Berghammer 2021). When considering how those shifts might impact the long‐standing marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps, one must reflect on their potential repercussions for wealth's predictive power for marriage entry and the wealth gains that follow it. Previous studies have emphasized that the growing prevalence of cohabitation often comes in tandem with its social normalization, which reduces the social stigmas faced by cohabitors and facilitates the extension to them of institutional benefits once exclusive to marriage. These processes appear to reduce marriage premiums in infant health and life‐satisfaction (Soons and Kalmijn 2009; Torche and Abufhele 2021). Likewise, they may enhance cohabiting couples' capacity to accumulate wealth, and the diffusion of cohabitation may generally diminish the relative gains of marriage and the size of marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps.

Yet beyond this scholarship on cohabitation's normalization, research has also examined why marriage is declining and cohabitation rising. Three main classes of explanatory theories can be distinguished, and they do not anticipate the same changes in the predictive power of wealth ownership for marriage, nor in couples' tendency to pool resources following cohabitation's diffusion and the wealth gains of marriage. More specifically, in several accounts, falling marriage rates are attributed to deteriorating economic conditions and “diverging destinies” (McLanahan 2004; Perelli‐Harris et al. 2010), meaning that stagnant wages and insecurity, particularly among men and lower‐educated groups, have curtailed the pool of candidates deemed economically ready to marry (Palumbo et al. 2025; Schneider et al. 2018). This perspective assumes that the core logic of marriage decisions has remained intact: cohabitation continues to be a temporary stage prior to being ready for the deeper commitment and independent lifestyle of marriage, which timing is decided in large part based on economic readiness (Kalmijn 2011). What changed are rather the conditions in which people make those decisions. Hence, wealth and socio‐economic indicators associated with future wealth accumulation should remain robust predictors of marriage even as it falls. Likewise, no significant change is expected in resource pooling among cohabitors. Overall, this perspective anticipates stable marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps amidst the growth of cohabitation.

Other perspectives instead see cohabitation spread as a new, extended life stage that precedes marriage, delays it at older ages and transforms its meaning, from a commitment to a symbolic “capstone” that signals the couple's relational and economic success (Billari and Liefbroer 2016; Cherlin 2004; Sassler and Miller 2023). This perspective posits that marriage decisions continue to center around timing and economic readiness criteria. But it suggests that the marriage bar may rise as cohabitation becomes a longer prelude, to include not just having achieved stability, but also markers of success matching marriage new status as a capstone, such as homeownership, possessing retirement savings, and the capacity to finance an elaborate wedding (Schneider 2011; Smock et al. 2005). Thus, according to this approach, wealth may come to predict marriage even more strongly with the growth of cohabitation. At the same time, resource pooling among cohabitors may increase as they strive to meet the heightened marriage thresholds, potentially narrowing the wealth gains of marriage relative to cohabitation. Altogether, this framework is therefore consistent with either stable or potentially even larger wealth gaps between marriage and cohabitation as the latter diffuses.

The third class of explanatory theories share the general hypothesis that marriage is declining due to disinterest or dissatisfaction with the institution (although they give different reasons why), with cohabitation spreading as an alternative for long‐term partnerships and family formation. For example, the second demographic transition approach posits that individualistic values have undermined the appeal of marriage and that they are better aligned with the flexibility of cohabitation (Lesthaeghe 2010). The theory of the unfinished gender revolution holds that women hesitate to marry due to men's limited engagement in domestic labor (Esping‐Andersen and Billari 2015; Goldscheider et al. 2015). Cohabitation offers an alternative that is easier to exit and is devoid of marriage's historical connection with patriarchy. Such perspectives suggest a profound shift in decision‐making around marriage. No longer a universal aspiration whose timing is governed largely by economic readiness, people come to choose between relationship types based on their distinct values. As those additional considerations enter the equation, economic ones should become less salient to the marriage decision, leading to a weaker predictive power of wealth (and other socio‐economic characteristics) on marriage.

For example, wealthy people passing the “marriage bar” may prefer cohabitation for its distinct meaning and feel no pressure to get married, while economic requirements for long‐term cohabitation may resemble those for marriage as it becomes an equally legitimate setting for family formation. Moreover, the cohabitation as alternative to marriage approach is highly consistent with intensified resource pooling among cohabitors (in particular through joint home ownership), given that having children is a major driver of those practices and that cohabitors are increasingly expected to form families as this union type replaces marriage (Hiekel et al. 2014). Hence, this framework predicts narrowing wealth gaps between married and cohabiting people with the growth of cohabitation. Cohabitation as a longer prelude

to marriage

As noted in the introduction, the various explanatory perspectives on the spread of cohabitation are not mutually exclusive; rather, they reflect multiple forces that operate simultaneously within most societies, with their relative influence shaped by local cultural, social, economic, and political conditions (Laplante 2006; Perelli‐Harris et al. 2010; Perelli‐Harris and Bernardi 2015). As a result, societies and cultural groups may follow distinct trajectories in the decline of marriage and spread of cohabitation. In some settings, growing insecurity predominates; in others, cohabitation's extension as a prelude to marriage is more salient; and in still others, its institutionalization as a socially legitimate alternative to marriage is the central driver (Laplante 2006; Perelli‐Harris et al. 2010; Perelli‐Harris and Bernardi 2015). The implications of cohabitation's rise for marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps should therefore not be universal, but context‐dependent, even though the normalization of cohabitation that generally coincides with its spread may everywhere tend to reduce wealth‐related institutional advantages associated with marriage, as noted previously. More specifically (as summarized in Table 1), wealth gaps by union type should remain stable where cohabitation spreads mainly in response to insecurity, and they may even widen where it is adopted as an extended prelude to marriage that reinforces its status as a capstone achievement. However, these gaps should narrow in societies where cohabitation diffuses as a viable long‐term alternative to marriage: where marriage is increasingly questioned as a family ideal and cohabitation gains legitimacy as a distinct, but equally valid, union type.

Previous empirical research supports this expectation of variable repercussions from the diffusion of cohabitation. Although relatively few studies have directly examined how marriage–cohabitation gaps in wealth or other economic resources evolve as cohabitation spreads, their findings are highly uneven. In the United States, Gruijters et al. (2023) observed larger wealth returns to married life courses among millennials than baby boomers (who married more than the former), while Kamp Dush et al. (2018) found little change in the association between employment and marriage across similar cohorts. In Sweden, in contrast, Lauster and Fransson (2006) documented a significantly looser association between marriage and homeownership during a period of cohabitation diffusion (1975–1997). Yet, Kapelle et al. (2025) compared the wealth gains of entering cohabitation in France and Germany and found no clear differences tied to cohabitation's prevalence. Likewise, studies examining conjugal pooling practices show substantial cross‐country variation in married versus cohabiting couples' economic arrangements, but no consistent link with cohabitation rates (Evans and Gray 2021; Hiekel et al. 2014).

In this context, this paper does not aim to identify a general association between marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps and cohabitation's prevalence, as was done in some previous studies of marriage‐cohabitation gaps in other outcomes, including life satisfaction and infant health, that were focused on the general effects of cohabitation's normalization (Soons and Kalmijn 2009; Torche and Abufhele 2021). Rather, I focus on the core prediction that cohabitation's diffusion as an alternative to marriage may significantly challenge married people's historical wealth advantage compared to cohabiting couples and empirically assess it through a comparative approach. The following section describes the comparative case.

Canada as a Comparative Case: The Diverging Cohabitation Diffusion Paths of Québec, Ontario, Alberta, and British Columbia

This study focuses on Canada's four most populous provinces, Québec, Ontario, British Columbia (B.C.), and Alberta, which account for about 85% of the population (because sample sizes in the data employed in the study are too small to include other regions). While cohabitation has increased throughout Canada in previous decades, the change has been uneven and caused by different processes across provinces (Statistics Canada 2022). 1 Outside of Québec, studies suggest cohabitation grew both because it became a longer and socially accepted (even expected) prelude to marriage and due to growing insecurity (Laplante and Fostik 2016). Indeed, aligning with the latter explanation, Wright (2019) found that the fall of marriage is concentrated in lower‐educated groups outside Québec. Moreover, consistent with the longer prelude to marriage perspective, Table 2 below shows that the growth of cohabitation between the 2001 and 2021 censuses was concentrated among younger groups in Alberta, B.C., and Ontario. The proportion of partnered people that are cohabiting rather than married nearly doubled from 27% to 50% among people aged between 25 and 29 during this period, but it barely changed among the 45–49‐year‐olds, remaining low at around 10% in all three provinces in 2021. In those provinces, then, marriage remains a central institution and a widely shared aspiration prior to family formation, as evidenced by the fact that over 70% of births were still to married mothers in 2021 (see Table 2 below). However, marriage is increasingly delayed, and cohabitation grows in popularity both as a prelude and trial before marriage and in response to insecurity, which reduces the pool of people feeling economically stable enough to wed.

In Québec, too, those factors have shaped the fall of marriage to some degree. Yet, they have played a comparatively smaller role than in the other provinces. For example, although cohabitation is also associated with lower education in Québec, it is far less so than in other provinces (Laplante and Fostik 2016; Wright 2019). According to most experts, the key driver of cohabitation's growth in Québec was instead its institutionalization as an alternative way to be in a union that is as socially legitimate as marriage for family formation and long‐term relationships (Heuveline and Timberlake 2004; Le Bourdais and Lapierre‐Adamcyk 2004; Mills 2004). This pattern is evident in the statistics on the prevalence of cohabitation presented in Table 1 above. First, in 2021, 23% of adults in Québec cohabited: twice the rate of the other provinces. More importantly, although cohabitation remains more prevalent in younger than older groups in Québec, it grew sharply even in older age categories in the last decades, with the proportion of partnered individuals aged 45–49 who are unmarried jumping from 25% to 44% between 2001 and 2021. This underscores the unusual long‐term stability of cohabiting unions in Québec, which was also observed by Wright (2019). Furthermore, cohabitation is now the dominant relationship type for childbearing in Québec: only 33% of births are to married mothers, down from 41% in 2001 as shown in Table 2, and nearly 60% are to cohabiting ones (Institut de la statistique du Québec 2024, 42).

The bedrock for this unique pattern of cohabitation growth in Québec was laid in the 1960s by a social movement dubbed the “silent revolution” among the province's majority French‐speaking and historically catholic population (Belleau 2012). Before then, resulting from the specificities of French colonialism in Québec, the catholic Church wielded enormous cultural and political power over this community. The silent revolution rejected its influence in public institutions and as a moral authority, engendering distance from its various symbols, including marriage (Laplante 2006). In the 1980s, federal and provincial legislation mandated an equal treatment of married and unmarried couples (after 1 year) by third parties throughout Canada, following the integration of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms in the constitution (Laplante 2014). This resulted in equal fiscal treatment, access to survivors' pension rights and insurance protections from employers, equal legal status of children, etc. across union types in all provinces. In Québec, these changes accelerated the retreat from marriage that had been brewing for decades, leading to the strong growth of cohabitation from the 1980s onwards that has since increasingly set Québec apart from other provinces (Laplante 2014). Importantly, because the cultural changes underpinning dissatisfaction with marriage touched the historically catholic and French‐speaking majority of Québec, they did not occur in the other primary linguistic group of (historically protestant) English‐speakers. For example, Table 3 below shows the proportion of married couples in Québec among francophones and anglophones from the Survey of Financial Security data employed in this study. On average for the period between 2005 and 2019, just 45% of couples were married among French speakers, compared to 69% among anglophones.

Another key factor shaping the particular path of cohabitation spread in Québec was the sharp differences its family laws drew between union types (Laplante and Fostik 2016). In 1989, Québec introduced the “family patrimony”, which demands equal division of several assets (such as the home and pensions) for married couples at divorce, regardless of their matrimonial regime. This is unlike other provinces where prenuptial agreements can allow for a full individualization of assets (Le Bourdais et al. 2016). This particularly stringent regulation of marriage was a demand of Quebec's government advisory council on women affairs (Conseil du statut de la femme, CSF) seeking protection for married women facing growing divorce rates. Yet, the CSF gave equal importance to promoting women's independence and equality through the preservation of cohabitation as a different union type not premised on the traditional sexual division of labor entailed in marriage. Thus, Québec historically rejected imposing any mutual obligations on cohabitors at separation, a stance reversed only recently in 2025 for couples with children. In contrast, the other provinces have long extended several of the protections of marriage to cohabiting couples, such as alimony rights (Laplante 2014). The legal distinctions between marriage and cohabitation may have spurred the latter's appeal in Québec as the only option for couples to fully keep their assets separate (Frémeaux and Leturcq 2020). More importantly, they contributed to marking cohabitation as a fundamentally different and less traditional type of union than marriage with regards to gender roles and independence.

In this context, marriage is neither an expected life course transition nor a widely shared ideal in today's Québec. Quebecers view cohabitation as a different, but equally acceptable, type of relationship for the long‐term and family formation that they choose to remain in rather than marry in part based on its associated values. For example, Belleau (2012) found that many decide between marriage or cohabitation based on their preference regarding formal ceremonies. Studies also note that cohabitors in Québec strongly diverge from the married in the importance they give to autonomy, something not observed in the other provinces (Lapierre‐Adamcyk et al. 2004; Laplante and Fostik 2016). Likewise, Yang (2025) showed that married and cohabiting couples differ more in Québec than in other provinces in terms of specialization and gender roles, with cohabiting couples more similar in their organization to the married outside of Québec, despite the much lower prevalence of cohabitation in those regions. This suggests that opting for marriage or cohabitation in Québec is in part a matter of attitudes regarding gender roles, autonomy, and tradition.

In sum, Canada experienced diverse processes of cohabitation diffusion. In Québec, especially among French‐speakers, cohabitation diffused widely and was strongly marked by its adoption as a long‐term alternative to marriage. In other provinces (Ontario, Alberta, and B.C.) and among Québec's anglophones, cohabitation spread more modestly and mostly in response to rising economic insecurity and as an extended prelude to marriage. These patterns make Canada an ideal setting to examine whether the spread of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage weakens the relationship between marriage and wealth, as posited in the theoretical framework and summarized in Table 1. To do so, I proceed in two steps. First, I test the core prediction that marriage–wealth associations are generally weaker in Québec (where cohabitation has diffused more strongly as an alternative to marriage than in other provinces), resulting in narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps. To this end, I explore four hypotheses that are derived from the theoretical framework. They are described in Panel a of Table 4 below. If I indeed find narrower marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps in Québec than in other provinces, my second step is to evaluate whether this difference may genuinely be attributed to Québec's distinct cohabitation diffusion trajectory, rather than to other interprovincial differences. The main potential alternative explanation regards social policies. Although federal rules require that cohabiting couples (after 1 year of coresidence) be treated equally to married ones by third parties in all provinces, as noted above, Québec differs from Ontario, Alberta, and British Columbia in its more generous and defamilialistic social policies, including longer parental leaves, a public childcare network, and more generous child benefits, especially for single parents (Berg et al. 2018; Mathieu 2023). Those policies may reduce the gains of specialization/money pooling in marriage for wealth accumulation by discharging all families from some care work (Van Winkle 2017). This potential explanation for Québec's narrower marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps and the one centered on its distinct cohabitation trajectory make competing predictions regarding two points: (1) expected differences in wealth gaps between married and single people across provinces, and (2) expected differences in marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps between linguistic groups within Québec. I test them to parse between explanations of Québec's narrower marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps and clarify whether they are attributable to Québec's unique cohabitation diffusion pattern.

In summary, the analytical strategy pursues two key objectives. First, I test whether Québec exhibits weaker associations between marriage and wealth than other provinces (Hypotheses 1–4). Second, I evaluate whether this regional difference likely stem from Québec's unique cohabitation diffusion pattern rather than alternative explanations rooted in its social policy environment (Hypotheses 5a–6b). Combined, those analyses help determine whether the diffusion of cohabitation as a legitimate alternative, but different type of relationship than marriage, tends to weaken marriage's long‐standing association with wealth, as posited in the theoretical framework (summarized in Table 1 above). The following sections describe the data and methods.

Data

Data come primarily from the Survey of Financial Security (SFS), a cross‐sectional survey conducted by Statistics Canada. It is representative of private households in the 10 provinces, excluding those on First Nations reserves or military bases. It uses stratified sampling and oversamples high‐income households. All analyses apply the provided probability weights to account for sampling and non‐response. I use the 1999, 2005, 2012, 2016, and 2019 waves (the 2023 wave was unavailable at the time of writing). The sample is restricted to households in Québec, Ontario, B.C., and Alberta (N ≈ 30,000), as smaller provinces lack sufficient sample sizes. I excluded households where the “primary economic support” (i.e., the household head) is over 60 to limit retirement‐related wealth effects (removing ∼10,000 cases). Most analyses focus on households headed by a couple (married or cohabiting), removing ∼5000 cases. About 300 cases had missing data on key variables, resulting in a final sample of about 15,000 households. 2

Variables and Measurements

The dependent variable is net worth, defined as the total value of financial and non‐financial assets—both within and outside of occupational pension plans—minus all debts. These values are for the entire household 3 . Except for pension wealth, they are self‐reported (with one member estimating the values of assets/debts of all household members). Pension wealth is estimated by Statistics Canada using employer‐reported pension benefits data matched to the job histories each household member reported to the SFS (for details, see Maser and Dorman 2001). Missing wealth values were imputed by Statistics Canada using the nearest neighbor method (Statistics Canada 2019). Because net worth is highly skewed, I transformed net worth values into a rank scale, which gives each household's percentile rank (from 1 to 100) within its survey year and province. In addition to reducing skewness, this transformation allows meaningful comparisons of wealth gaps across provinces, which differ in average wealth levels. Table A1 in the Supporting Information S1 presents descriptive statistics for net worth and all other variables by province.

The first main independent variable is union type coded as married versus cohabiting. One model also includes households headed by an unpartnered person, distinguishing between men and women. The second main predictor is the province of residence, distinguishing between Québec and the other three (Ontario, Alberta, and B. C.). The latter are grouped for concision, although some models differentiate between them for robustness checks. For models focused on variation by linguistic groups within Québec, the main predictor is childhood language of the household head (French vs. English), defined as the language learned during childhood and still understood (not available in 1999). Respondents reporting both French and English are classified as French. I excluded respondents reporting only another language from analyses focused on Québec due to small sample sizes and because most of those cases are immigrants to Canada who are too different from English Speakers, who are mainly natives, to be pooled with them.

Some models integrate socio‐economic characteristics that are predictive of future wealth accumulation as mediator variables to explore Hypotheses 3 and 4. They are household post‐tax income and the highest educational attainment of the household head (education being highly correlated within unions). Our models aimed at exploring Hypotheses 3 and 4 also include control variables which may be correlated with both wealth and union type in different ways across provinces and could also explain why marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps are narrower in Québec. They include whether the head was born outside of Canada (the variable is correlated within unions), their age and age squared (to capture the nonlinearity), the presence of children in the household, household size, and the population size of the region of residence.

Analytical Strategy

As a reminder, the study hypotheses are summarized in Table 4 above. To test Hypothesis 1, which predicts generally narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps in Québec than in other provinces, I estimate a linear regression model of net worth rank as a function of union type interacted with province of residence, and with fixed effects for survey year. To address Hypothesis 2, concerning widening differences between Québec and the other provinces over time, I extend this model by adding a three‐way interaction among union type, province, and survey year. Hypotheses 3 and 4 posit that Québec's narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps reflect a weaker predictive effect of wealth and other socio‐economic characteristics on marriage (H3) and reduced wealth gains to marriage (H4). I examine these propositions using a set of nested models.

The first one corresponds to the first model specification and estimates the gross differences in marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps between Québec and other provinces. The second model adds the control variables—age, immigration status, presence of children in the household, household size, and population size of the region of residence—to assess whether Québec continues to exhibit narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps once these factors are held constant. The third and fourth models then add educational attainment and household income, respectively. If Québec's narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gap is partly reduced after introducing these socio‐economic characteristics, this would be consistent with Hypothesis 3 (weaker selection into marriage based on socio‐economic indicators in Québec). Conversely, any remaining provincial differences after including these indicators would be consistent with Hypothesis 4 (lower wealth premium to marriage in Québec).

After having examined provincial differences in the relationship between marriage and wealth, I turn to the second set of hypotheses (5a–b and 6a–b). They assess whether Québec's narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps can be attributed to its distinct diffusion of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage rather than to its policies. They do so by testing competing predictions (H5a‐b and H6a‐b) generated by these two explanations (see Table 4 for the detailed hypotheses and their theoretical links). Hypotheses 5a and 5b concern the existence of provincial differences in wealth gaps between married and unpartnered individuals. To test them, I expand the analytic sample to include households headed by unpartnered men and women and estimate a linear regression of net worth rank on conjugal status, interacted with province of residence, and year fixed effects. Competing Hypotheses 6a and 6b focus on differences within Québec between linguistic groups. To test them, I restrict the sample to Québec and estimate a model predicting net worth rank using an interaction between union type and language, controlling for survey year.

Supplementary Analyses Using the Longitudinal Administrative Databank (LAD)

The SFS employed in the main analyses described above has two important limitations. First, like many wealth surveys, it collects data on assets and debts at the household level only, without providing information on their intra‐household distribution. This precludes a gendered analysis: examining whether marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps differ across provinces for both men and women. Second, the SFS has a cross‐sectional rather than longitudinal design. This constrains the ability to explore Hypotheses 3 and 4, which posit that narrower wealth gaps in Québec stem from weaker selection in marriage based on wealth and other socio‐economic characteristics (H3) and smaller wealth gains to marriage (H4). Indeed, in the main SFS analyses, I examine these hypotheses using nested models, as described above. But those analyses can only offer indirect evidence for H3 and H4. For directly assessing how wealth and other socio‐economic indicators predict marriage and how marriage affects wealth accumulation across provinces, longitudinal data are essential.

To help address those limitations of the SFS, I conduct supplementary analyses using The Longitudinal Administrative Databank (LAD). It is a 20% annual sample drawn from the T1 Family File, a longitudinal database constructed from federal tax returns data, currently covering the period from 1982 to 2022. Although the LAD does not include comprehensive wealth data, reflecting the federal tax system's focus on income rather than assets, it provides high‐quality, individual‐level information on a key component of wealth: financial assets held in Tax‐Free Savings Accounts (TFSAs). These accounts have allowed adults aged 18 and over to invest annually up to a ceiling (currently $10,000) since 2009. TFSAs can hold various financial assets, and both returns and withdrawals are tax‐exempt. By 2023, they accounted for nearly 20% of Canada's non‐pension financial assets (Statistics Canada 2024). As shown in Supporting Information S1: Table A5, TFSA holdings are also closely correlated with overall household wealth and can serve as a proxy for it.

The additional analyses thus marshal this TFSA data (from 2009 to 2022) to conduct, first, a gendered analysis, exploring whether wealth gaps across union types seem narrower in Québec for both men and women. To this effect, I use a rank‐transformed TFSA wealth variable as main outcome. Tax filers who have no TFSA assets (about 55%) are coded zero. For those with positive TFSA holdings, the rank‐transformed variable represents their percentile rank in their province for each fiscal year. For men and women separately, I estimate linear regressions predicting the TFSA wealth rank variable using the interaction of union type (marriage vs. cohabitation) with the province of residence, controlling for tax year. The Supporting Information S1 contains several robustness checks with alternative specifications of the TFSA variable.

Second, the additional analyses leverage LAD's longitudinal structure to directly assess whether the predictive effects of wealth and socio‐economic characteristics on transitions to marriage and the wealth gains to marriage are weaker in Québec than other provinces. For the selection effect, I focus on people observed since their first year in cohabitation and fit Cox proportional hazards models that predict the hazards of marriage and separation (between 2009 and 2022). The main predictor is TFSA wealth ownership (yes vs. no). In addition, I use three other indicators of asset ownership available in the LAD data: contributions into workplace pension plans (indicating ownership of workplace pension wealth), contributions to Registered Retirement Saving Plans (RRSPs, indicating ownership of personal pension funds), and having declared taxable investment incomes (indicating ownership of non‐pension, income‐generating financial assets or real estate). No indicator for homeownership is available. I also use labor income as another key socio‐economic indicator; educational attainment information is not available. Control factors in those models include fiscal year, age, immigration status, population size of region of residence, and the presence of children in the household. For exploring wealth gains to marriage between provinces, I focus on the set of cohabitors who do marry (between 2009 and 2022) and use fixed‐effects linear models to measure changes in their TFSA wealth ranks post‐versus pre‐marriage by province. Full methodological details and results are in the Supporting Information S1; the main text only summarizes key findings from those additional analyses.

Supplementary Results From the Longitudinal Administrative Databank

I use the LAD data to help address two limitations of the above SFS analyses. First, this dataset could not support a gendered analysis of marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps across provinces due to wealth measurement at the household level. To help address this issue, I used the LAD's information on individual wealth held in TFSAs. Table A6 in the Supporting Information S1 presents results from linear models predicting TFSA wealth rank using an interaction between union type and province of residence among men and women. The results show that, among both men and women, the marriage‐cohabitation wealth gap is about 3 points larger on the TFSA wealth rank scale in other provinces than in Québec. Hence, cohabitation‐marriage wealth gaps appear narrower in Québec than other provinces for both gender groups.

Second, I used the LAD to offer a finer analysis of whether narrower marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps in Québec reflect weaker selection in marriage based on wealth and other socio‐economic characteristics (H3) and smaller wealth gains to marriage (H4). While the nested models conducted with the SFS data above provided indirect evidence consistent with both these mechanisms, I tapped the longitudinal structure of the LAD to test each one directly. Beginning with selection in marriage based on wealth and other socio‐economic indicators, Table A9 of the Supporting Information S1 presents results from Cox proportional hazards models predicting union outcomes among cohabitors with several indicators of asset ownership and labor income. In Québec, all indicators of asset ownership (e.g., TFSA wealth ownership, having contributed to RRSPs and workplace pension accounts, and having taxable investment incomes) as well as labor income reduce the risk that cohabitors separate. All those indicators also reduce the risk of transitioning to marriage, except for TFSA wealth ownership and labor income. Those patterns hold for both men and women. In other provinces, however, indicators of wealth ownership and labor income do not reduce the risk of separation as strongly as in Québec, and they are much more strongly associated with transitions to marriage than in Québec. In other words, in Québec having a higher income and owning assets is associated with remaining longer in cohabitation rather than marrying, while those indicators correlate with marriage transition in other provinces. Those results confirm that Québec exhibits a weaker selection in marriage based on wealth and income, and that the province's narrower wealth gaps between married and cohabiting couples partly reflects this reality.

Regarding differences in the wealth gains from marriage, Supporting Information S1: Table A10 shows results from fixed‐effects models that explore changes in TFSA wealth rank after marriage transitions (among cohabitors who did marry) in Québec and other provinces. For both men and women, there are modest gains in TFSA wealth rank after marriage in Québec. Among men, those gains are slightly larger in the other provinces than in Québec. There are no significant regional differences among women, however. This suggest that the narrower wealth gaps in Québec also derive from slightly lower wealth returns to marriage, at least among men.

Discussion and Conclusion

A substantial body of research shows that individuals in long‐term marriages tend to accumulate more wealth than those in cohabiting relationships. My contribution to this literature was to assess whether the spread of cohabitation can weaken this long‐standing link between marriage and wealth. I emphasized that the main theoretical perspectives on the growth of cohabitation make sharply divergent predictions. Arguments that view the spread of cohabitation as a response to insecurity suggest that the marriage–wealth correlation should remain stable as cohabitation diffuses; whereas perspectives that conceptualize cohabitation as a prolonged prelude to marriage imply that this association may even intensify as cohabitation becomes more common. However, theories that portray cohabitation as an increasingly legitimate long‐term alternative to marriage rising due to disinterest in the latter anticipate a substantial weakening of the marriage–wealth association.

To investigate these theoretical insights, I drew on prior work that treats the three explanations for cohabitation's diffusion not as competing accounts, but as distinct forces that operate simultaneously, with varying predominance depending on local context. Building on this perspective, I proposed a comparative research design to test the central prediction that when cohabitation spreads largely as an alternative to marriage for long‐term relationships, marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps should shrink. I identified a well‐documented case that fits this pattern, the Canadian province of Québec, and contrasted it with the three other most populous provinces of Canada (Ontario, Alberta, and British Columbia) where cohabitation diffused more modestly and mostly as a prelude to marriage and a response to economic insecurity. Consistent with the theoretical argument, I found that marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps narrowed substantially in Québec relative to the comparison provinces since the early 2000s and as cohabitation diffused, and that they had in effect disappeared by 2019.

Importantly, I also examined whether Québec's considerably narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps compared to other provinces could genuinely be traced to its distinct pattern of cohabitation diffusion, rather than to a key alternative explanation rooted in its more generous and defamilialistic social policies. To this end, I tested competing predictions about differences between linguistic groups in Québec and those between married and unpartnered people across provinces. In contradiction to the predictions of the policy‐based account of narrower marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps in Québec, and in support of the cohabitation diffusion trajectory account, I found that the wealth gaps between married and unpartnered individuals were similar in Québec and other provinces, and that marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps were larger among Québec's Anglophones than Francophones, despite their shared policy environment. Although a comparative research design cannot establish causality, these patterns strongly suggest that Québec's unique trajectory of cohabitation diffusion helped drive its narrower marriage–cohabitation wealth gaps. Indeed, the contrast between French and English speakers within Québec helps rule out that policies or any other interprovincial differences induced the change. These results thus align with the theoretical argument that, when cohabitation diffuses largely due to dissatisfaction with marriage and as an alternative to it for the long‐term premised on different values, as it has in Québec, the association between marriage and wealth tends to weaken.

This insight extends prior research on the consequences of cohabitation diffusion, which had thus far examined marriage–cohabitation gaps in outcomes other than wealth, such as infant health (Torche and Abufhele 2021), life‐satisfaction (Perelli‐Harris et al. 2019), and gender equality (Yang 2025). Moreover, earlier studies in this field had tended to emphasize the effects of cohabitation's normalization, in arguing that greater acceptance of this union form reduces its institutional disadvantages, and its gaps compared to marriage in a range of well‐being outcomes. This paper, in contrast, underscored that why and how cohabitation becomes normalized, whether as an alternative to marriage, a prelude to it, or a response to economic insecurity, also deeply shapes how its rising adoption transforms marriage–cohabitation gaps, at least with regards to wealth. This insight encourages future research to pay more attention to cohabitation diffusion trajectories when interpreting their repercussions. The paper also positions Canada as a useful comparative setting for scholars interested in examining how different patterns of cohabitation spread affect marriage–cohabitation gaps in outcomes beyond wealth, such as health or life satisfaction.

This study also contributed to research on the economic determinants of marriage. Prior work has shown that socio‐economic indicators such as wealth and income have long strongly predicted marriage entry across several societies for men, and that this association intensified among women as their labor force participation increased (Goldstein and Kenney 2001; Ishizuka 2018; Mäenpää 2009; Schwartz and Gonalons‐Pons 2016; Sweeney 2002; Wiik et al. 2010). This selection effect reflects that marriage is a widely shared family aspiration whose timing is governed partly by notions of economic readiness in a cultural context that links marriage with expectations of economic independence. This dynamic contributes to class inequalities in intimate experiences and concentrates economic resources within married families (Bloome and Ang 2020). The present study extended this line of research by identifying one condition under which wealth and other socio‐economic indicators may lose predictive strength for transitions to marriage: the diffusion of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage.

Indeed, in Québec, the marriage–cohabitation wealth gap narrowed markedly relative to other provinces and this pattern largely reflected the diminished predictive role of wealth, income, and education for marriage transitions among cohabitors. Evidence for this came indirectly, from the attenuation of interprovincial differences in wealth gaps after adjusting for income and education. More directly, supplementary analyses using longitudinal LAD data found weaker predictive effects of various indicators of asset ownership and income on marriage transitions among cohabitors in Québec. These findings suggest that, when marriage loses its status as a universal normative endpoint and cohabitation attains comparable social legitimacy and represents an alternative union type for the long‐term and even family formation, economic status becomes less central to marriage decisions. Socio‐economic indicators such as wealth play a reduced role in shaping union transitions, with cohabiting couples passing the marriage bar appearing likely to remain in cohabiting unions. To paraphrase Jane Austin, in such contexts: it is no longer a “truth universally acknowledged that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife”. This shift in the economic determinants of transitions to marriage in societies such as Québec raises important questions about its implications for wealth concentration and social stratification. Given that upper‐class family reproduction strategies have historically been anchored in marriage (Bessière and Gollac 2023; O'Brien 2024), it is essential to assess how they adapt in contexts where both cohabitation and marriage can fulfill this role.

This paper also contributes to research on the “marriage wealth premium,” which posits that marriage directly increases wealth. Consistent with recent accounts (Cross et al. 2022), the findings of this paper suggest that differences in the economic situation of married and unmarried people derive more from selection into marriage than marriage‐induced gains. Yet, modest TFSA wealth gains following marriage were found, and they were smaller in Québec, albeit only among men. This may reflect greater resource pooling in cohabitation alongside its spread as an alternative to marriage for childrearing, reducing the distinct economic advantages of marriage. Overall, these findings align with previous research underscoring that any gains from marriage are neither universal nor intrinsic, but shaped by institutional and cultural contexts (Addo and Lichter 2013; Schechtl and Kapelle 2024).

There are limitations to this study that open avenues for future research. First, although the SFS enabled inter‐provincial comparisons of marriage–cohabitation gaps in overall wealth levels, its cross‐sectional design restrained deeper analyses of regional variation in wealth‐based selection into marriage and subsequent wealth gains. I partly addressed this issue using longitudinal LAD data, which allowed me to examine how ownership of several asset types (including financial wealth held in TFSAs, RRSPs, and employer pension plans) predicted marriage and separation among cohabitors across provinces. The LAD also permitted an assessment of regional variation in the effect of marriage on TFSA wealth accumulation, which is a key component of financial assets in Canada and strongly correlated with overall net worth, as I noted before in the methods section. Yet, the LAD lacks information on homeownership, a particularly strong predictor of marriage (Schneider 2011) and an asset that marriage tends to increase more than others (Kapelle and Lersch 2020). Future research should therefore investigate how the diffusion of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage reshapes the role of housing wealth in union formation, as well as how marriage sustains its accumulation, in contexts where the necessary data are available.

Another limitation concerns the inability to assess gender differences in the SFS, which collects wealth at the household rather than the individual level. The LAD provided a partial remedy by offering individual‐level data on TFSA holdings. Available evidence suggests minimal gender variation: both men and women exhibited similarly reduced associations between TFSA wealth and marriage in Québec compared with other provinces. Future research should nonetheless investigate whether the rise of cohabitation has distinct implications for gendered patterns of asset accumulation across different asset classes, particularly housing, which remains the principal form of household wealth in many countries, including Canada.

The household‐level wealth measures in the SFS also prevented an analysis of joint versus separate asset ownership within cohabiting and married unions in Québec and other provinces. Although the spread of cohabitation as an alternative to marriage in Québec was fundamentally rooted in a backlash against the Catholic Church among the French‐speaking population dating back to at least the 1960s, legal changes in the 1980s that mandated equal division of several assets upon divorce may have reinforced cohabitation's appeal as the sole avenue for keeping property divided within unions, as I explained before in the presentation of the comparative cases. Due to the absence of data on joint versus individual ownership, I could not assess whether cohabitors in Québec are indeed more likely than married couples, or than cohabitors elsewhere, to keep their assets separate. This question warrants future investigation for a deeper understanding of why cohabitation has gained such prominence as an alternative to marriage in Québec.

Finally, this paper examined the consequences of the spread of cohabitation on marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps in a society where it expanded as an alternative to marriage after the latter had lost appeal and prominence as a family aspiration. In other societies, such as the US, cohabitation appears to have spread instead as a new stage in the marriage process that recasts it as a capstone achievement and as response to economic insecurity, much as it has in Alberta, Ontario, and British‐Columbia that were compared to Québec in this study (Sassler and Miller 2023). In such contexts, growing cohabitation may not affect the marriage‐wealth link, and it may even strengthen it if longer spells of cohabitation raise the economic bar for marriage entry. Consistent with this view, the (more modest) growth of cohabitation in provinces other than Québec since the 2000s appears to have had few impacts on marriage‐cohabitation wealth gaps, which remained roughly stable over time. Yet, future studies should continue to test how the spread of cohabitation reshapes marriage‐cohabitation gaps in diverse cultural and institutional settings and depending on their particular trajectory of marriage decline and cohabitation growth to explore the generalizability of the results obtained in Canada.
