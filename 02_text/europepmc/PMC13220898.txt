ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe lack of a valid and reliable Thai version of the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) applicable across relationship statuses and sexual orientations represents an important gap in Thailand.AimThis study aimed to develop and validate a Thai version of the FSFI (FSFI-TH) for use among Thai women with varied marital statuses and sexual orientations.MethodsA total of 243 women aged 18-66 years (mean age 31.96 ± 9.78 years) participated in the study. The FSFI-TH was developed through forward translation, back translation, expert committee review, and a pilot study. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients and test–retest reliability with intraclass correlation coefficients. Validity was evaluated on aspects of content and construct validity.OutcomesThe primary outcomes were the psychometric reliability and validity of the FSFI-TH.ResultsItem-Objective Congruence demonstrated good content validity, with linguistic refinements informed by the pilot study. The FSFI-TH showed strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values above .80 for the total scale and most domains. Construct validity assessed using principal component analysis revealed a five-factor structure, with the Desire and Arousal domains loading onto a single factor.Clinical ImplicationsThe FSFI-TH is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing sexual function among Thai women.Strengths and LimitationsA key strength of this study is the inclusive applicability of the FSFI-TH across diverse relationship statuses and sexual orientations. However, the limited representation of postmenopausal women means that its applicability in this group should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionThe FSFI-TH is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring multidimensional aspects of sexual function in Thai women.Keywords: FSFI, Thai, validation, female sexual function index, female sexual dysfunction, psychometrics

BODY
Introduction

Female sexual dysfunction (FSD) is a prevalent sexual health disorder that affects many women. Research indicated that its prevalence is approximately 40%-60%.1–7 Previous research reported that Asian women were the least likely to discuss their sexual health with doctors.8 Several sociocultural factors are barriers that prevent individuals from seeking medical advice for sexual problems. The belief that sexual dysfunction is not a medical problem is strongly observed in many Asian countries, especially in Thailand.8 Another factor that can prevent individuals from seeking medical advice for sexual problems is the embarrassment of discussing sexual problems with healthcare providers.8,9 In Thailand, women have been reported to disclose fewer sexual problems than men despite comparable prevalence,10 which may reflect underreporting. This may be influenced by embarrassment, perceived judgment from healthcare providers, and stigma surrounding female sexuality, all of which may limit open discussion in clinical settings.11

Patients often experience embarrassment and shyness, which may reduce their willingness to discuss sexual concerns during clinical consultations.12 Previous research has shown that sexually active patients generally accept the administration of the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) as part of routine assessment and demonstrate high levels of questionnaire compliance.13 This suggests that many patients are willing to engage with structured self-report measures when discussing sexual health issues.

The FSFI is a well-developed and widely utilized questionnaire for assessing sexual function in women. It consists of 6 domains of FSD, including desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain, and is comprised of 19 items.14 The questionnaire was initially developed based on a study of its construct validity and reliability involving women between the ages of 21 and 70. The FSFI was designed to be applicable for assessing sexual function in women of all sexual orientations, whether they are heterosexual or homosexual, single or in a relationship. The FSFI has also been validated and translated into multiple languages.14,15

Two prior studies translated the FSFI into Thai as part of broader research, rather than as a primary focus of psychometric validation. In 2008, the first study translated the English version of the FSFI into Thai, followed by an independent back-translation check. Then, a sample of 20 postmenopausal women was used to evaluate the questionnaire’s wording and comprehension. In their study, the authors reported only a reliability coefficient of 0.90 for the Thai-language questionnaire.16 However, no information on validity or additional reliability testing, such as test–retest reliability, was provided. Another study, published in 2015, utilized the Thai version, whose translation has been tested with high reliability coefficients and internal consistency (r = 0.79-0.86, Cronbach’s alpha = .86). The FSFI was translated into Thai, and experts agree on its validity. The reliability of this FSFI version was also evaluated among Thai postmenopausal women.17 However, details regarding the sample size used in the Thai version development phase were not provided, and comprehensive psychometric evaluation, such as construct validity or factor analysis, was not reported. Overall, both studies provided limited information on comprehensive psychometric evaluation, with several aspects of reliability and validity remaining insufficiently assessed. Importantly, validation in both studies focused primarily on postmenopausal women, with little information on applicability to women of different age groups, relationship statuses, or sexual orientations. This highlights the need for a comprehensive translation and validation of the FSFI in a more diverse Thai population.

Our study aims to translate and validate the Thai version of the FSFI, including individuals of varied ages, marital statuses, and sexual orientations, as in the original development of the FSFI. By validating a more inclusive version of the FSFI, our research could contribute to the improvement of sexual health outcomes for women in Thailand, particularly those who were previously overlooked or excluded from sexual health research.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February and July 2024. Study participants were a convenience sample of 243 participants from a general outpatient clinic and a sexual health clinic at Ramathibodi Hospital, and through social media platforms (eg, Facebook, X, Line). Inclusion criteria were (1) individuals whose sex assigned at birth is female, (2) age 18 years old and over, (3) sexually active within the past 4 weeks, and (4) can use the Thai language for communication. Exclusion criteria included individuals unable to provide informed consent or independently complete the questionnaire.

Participation was entirely voluntary. The recruitment strategy involved offering participants the motivation to complete the questionnaire twice over a 2 to 4-week interval and receive feedback on their scores. Participants who consented to follow-up provided an email address and were contacted by email to complete the questionnaire for the second time.

Translation process

The translation process followed the guidelines of Guillemin and colleagues.18 The original version of the FSFI was translated into Thai by 2 bilingual translators. One translator was a professional linguist from a language center who was not familiar with the FSFI (T1), while the other translator was a native Thai family physician with expertise in sexual health and proficiency in both Thai and English (T2). The 2 translations were compared, and any discrepancies in the terms or expressions used were identified. The principal investigator recorded the data and synthesized a new version of the questionnaire (T-12) based on the input from the 2 translators. Then, the Thai version was back-translated to English by 2 family physicians (B1) and (B2), who are native English speakers and proficient in Thai without any prior knowledge of the original FSFI.

Consolidation and discussion of the questionnaires (T1, T2, B1, B2, and T-12) were undertaken by a committee of 6 experts, comprising 2 family physicians, a gynecologist, and a psychiatrist, all of whom hold diplomas in sexual medicine, along with a translator and a back translator. The original questionnaire, along with each translated version (T1, T2, B1, B2, and T-12), was reviewed to assess content validity. The committee aimed to achieve equivalence in 4 domains: semantic, idiomatic, conceptual, and experiential.

A pilot study of the Thai version of FSFI (FSFI-TH) was conducted with 40 Thai females aged 18 years and older. The pilot study revealed that women without a sexual partner were unable to respond to the item assessing satisfaction with their sexual relationship (Q15). In response, and in line with the expert committee’s recommendation, a “no partner” response option was added. Feedback from the pilot study also indicated the need for clearer wording in relation to “emotional closeness” (Q14) and “overall sexual life” (Q16), and corresponding explanations were therefore incorporated.

Sample size

The required sample size for the study’s primary psychometric analyses was determined through formal power calculations. For the assessment of internal consistency, the minimum sample size for Cronbach’s alpha was calculated using hypothesis testing19 to be N = 195. Similarly, hypothesis testing20 indicated that a minimum of N = 130 participants was required for the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis. Based on these estimates, a target of N = 195 participants was set for the initial time point.

A total of N = 243 participants successfully completed the questionnaire at both time points. This final analytical sample of N = 243 was used for all psychometric analyses to ensure consistency in participant eligibility and data quality across internal consistency, construct validity, and temporal reliability assessments.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were described using mean and standard deviation, or median and range if the distribution was not normal. Categorical data were described using frequency and percentages. All statistical analyses were performed by using Stata Statistical Software: Release 18.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX; 2023).

Validity

Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Principal component analysis (PCA) was used as the extraction method, with Varimax rotation applied. The number of components was determined using scree plot inspection and the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues >1). Factor loadings were then reviewed to evaluate how well each item corresponded to the theoretically established six-factor structure.

Reliability

Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with values above .70 considered acceptable. Test–retest reliability was evaluated using the ICC, with values above 0.75 generally considered good and above 0.90 excellent. To ensure the psychometric integrity of the final analytical sample, responses were screened for implausible straight-lining (non-differentiation) patterns, and cases showing these patterns were excluded.

Ethical consideration

This research project was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, under project number MURA 2023/523 on July 4, 2023. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

Construct validity

Construct validity was evaluated using PCA with Varimax rotation and Kaiser Normalization. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure was 0.817, indicating meritorious sampling adequacy. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 2944.912, df = 171, P < .001), confirming that the correlations between variables were suitable for factor analysis.

Although the original FSFI is based on a six-factor structure, exploratory analysis of the Thai version suggested a five-factor solution. Specifically, items from the desire and arousal domains loaded onto a single factor, indicating overlap between these constructs in this sample. Discussion

This study aimed to translate and validate the FSFI into Thai (FSFI-TH). Overall, the results demonstrated that the FSFI-TH possesses robust psychometric integrity for use among Thai women across diverse age groups, marital statuses, sexual orientations, and varied physical and mental health backgrounds.

The FSFI-TH showed high content validity, reflecting strong congruence and relevance of the items, as confirmed by the expert committee. Feedback from the pilot study also helped refine linguistic clarity and appropriateness, further strengthening the quality and cultural fit of the instrument.

Regarding reliability, the FSFI-TH’s internal consistency was strong, with all Cronbach’s alpha coefficients meeting the minimum threshold of .70. Test–retest reliability demonstrated acceptable temporal stability. Specifically, most domains showed moderate ICCs when using the total sample, but improved to good stability after excluding participants with non-differentiation response patterns.

Variation in ICC values may be partly influenced by the mode of questionnaire distribution, as data were collected using web-based surveys in both clinical settings and via social media. Previous studies indicate that respondents’ effort in online surveys can be influenced by factors such as incentives and questionnaire design, thereby increasing the risk of inattentive or invalid responding.21,22 In this study, participants were informed that they would receive their FSFI results after completing both time points, which may have influenced how some participants approached the retest questionnaire.

Regarding structural validity across international adaptations, the FSFI has shown factor structures ranging from 3 to 6 factors. These variations suggest that cultural and linguistic factors may influence how women interpret and differentiate aspects of sexual functioning.23 For example, the Chinese validation identified a clear six-factor structure, attributing the separation of desire and arousal to specific linguistic features.24 Given this variability, a methodological decision in this study was the use of EFA, employing PCA. The EFA was strategically implemented to adapt the scale for cross-cultural and linguistic use, specifically to test for psychometric divergence in the Thai context.

In this study, the Desire and Arousal domains merged into a single factor, resulting in a five-factor solution. This finding is consistent with the statistical analysis in the original FSFI14 and other translated versions,25,26 all of which also reported overlap between these 2 domains. Previous research has also consistently demonstrated a high correlation between sexual desire and arousal.27

The interconnection between sexual desire and arousal is strongly supported by the circular sexual response model by Basson, where arousal often precedes and triggers desire (responsive desire), making the 2 experiences phenomenologically inseparable for many women.28

However, the Arousal domain appears to be predominantly linked to state-like (situational) factors. In contrast, the Desire domain encompasses both state and trait-like (stable) components.29 This distinction likely explains the relatively lower temporal stability observed in the Arousal domain’s ICC. Furthermore, this instability was likely exacerbated by an exogenous factor: the second questionnaire administration coincided with the “Valentine’s Day” period. Given that this event is associated with heightened sexual engagement in the Thai population, especially among women of reproductive age,30 behavioral frequency would naturally fluctuate. Critically, because the FSFI Arousal domain includes a “zero” option for “no sexual activity,” a behavioral surge during the Valentine’s period would mathematically result in significant score fluctuations compared to the baseline period, whereas the trait-based desire remained more constant.

A major strength of this study is its inclusive design. The translation and validation processes were intentionally developed to be accessible to women with diverse relationship statuses and sexual orientations, including women who are single or not heterosexual. The use of an online questionnaire supported this goal by enabling broader outreach and facilitating recruitment from a wider population. Another key strength is the rigorous and multidisciplinary translation process. The expert committee, comprising clinicians with experience in sexual medicine, played an important role in ensuring the cultural, conceptual, and linguistic appropriateness of the translated items. Their input significantly contributed to the strong content validity and helped refine the wording to ensure clarity and acceptability. The adaptation process also prioritized cultural sensitivity, enhancing the tool’s acceptability in a context where sexual health remains a sensitive and often taboo subject.

Despite its strengths, the study has certain limitations. First, given the sample’s primary composition of premenopausal women, the direct applicability of our findings to postmenopausal women should be considered with caution. Second, the use of online questionnaires may have excluded individuals without access to digital technology and may have influenced response behavior in the retest phase. Finally, discriminant validity was not evaluated because participants were drawn from the community rather than clinical subgroups. Future research could address this by including women with diagnosed sexual dysfunction to help determine appropriate FSFI-TH cut-off values for the Thai population.

Conclusions

The FSFI-TH shows satisfactory psychometric properties and can be applied to women in a range of relationship contexts and sexual orientations, supporting its use in both clinical and research settings.
