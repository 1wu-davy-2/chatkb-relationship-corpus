ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch on marital happiness in Middle Eastern cultures is lacking. This lack of understanding has created several challenges for policymakers and marriage counselors as divorce rates have increased over the past decade.MethodThis study aimed to assess the prevalence of marital happiness among Omani couples and its association with demographic variables, including gender, level of education, and duration of marriage. A convenience sample of 1137 married people from different regions in Oman responded to the Marital Happiness Scale.ResultsOmani couples, in general, indicated a high level of marital happiness. Married men seemed significantly happier about their marriages than wives did. Income and education level also affect perceived marital happiness. Marital happiness increased as income increased while level of education was partially associated with marital happiness. Place of living was not a significant predictor of marital happiness. Lower levels of perceived marital happiness among couples in polygamous marriages were observed. The spouses' kinship did not affect their perception of marital happiness.ConclusionThis study showed that financial stressors and education level significantly influence marital happiness. Men and women also perceive marital happiness differently. The results offer implications for research and practice, especially for policymakers and marital counselors.

BODY
Introduction

Marriage is an essential aspect of the human experience, a practice through which people establish emotional and social connections, reproduce [1, 2], satisfy intimacy needs, and raise a family [3]. In Middle Eastern cultures, marriage serves many purposes as adults marry into families rather than marrying a person. To this end, with marriage comes a substantial amount of responsibility and psychological pressure to fulfill familial expectations, such as child rearing, respecting norms, providing for the family, and inclusion of the extended family in decision-making [4]. High expectations can create tensions within the nuclear family and issues such as divorce are frowned upon within Middle Eastern cultures due to societal expectations [4, 5].

Marital happiness can determine life satisfaction and understanding between couples [3] and may also predict lifetime health outcomes [6]. The US General Social Survey (GSS) shows that happier couples are 40% less likely to become ill over their adult life and that strong relationships as well as adjustment were positive determinants of marital happiness [6]. Lack of adjustment can, however, lower marital happiness, and thus, many couples may choose to live peacefully but not happily, potentially leading to divorce. Lack of marital happiness may lead to decreased emotional and sexual intimacy [7], both of which are important for marital happiness.

Many countries such as Oman allocate substantial resources to help married couples achieve higher levels of satisfaction and happiness. However, the National Centre for Statistics and Information (NCSI) in Oman reported increased divorce rates since 2013, with approximately 8% of marriages ending in divorce [8]. Marital struggles or lack of marital happiness may have led to divorce, suggesting that a better understanding of marital happiness and its determinants is needed.

Literature review

Marital happiness

Marital happiness is “the subjective feeling of happiness and pleasure experienced by a spouse when considering all current aspects of his or her marriage” [17: p. 647]. It refers to general feeling of satisfaction about marriage through positive daily experiences [1]. Marital happiness may also reflect high levels of marital satisfaction, particularly in terms of meeting basic intimacy and psychological needs [9, 10].

Contemporary research refers to marital satisfaction as a primary component of marital happiness [10]. In specific, Middle Eastern cultures tend to consider marital satisfaction a form of marital happiness [11]. Research shows that levels of marital satisfaction can increase with time; and it is normal to experience rough beginnings in a marital relationship [12]. Variables such as better marital communication [2], increased sexual satisfaction [13], and increased marital adjustment may lead to higher levels of marital happiness [14]. In this study, marital happiness is defined as a feeling of harmony, satisfaction, respect, and care through acts of moral and financial contributions, while experiencing feelings of stability and trust [11, 14]. Literature shows that culture, gender, income, and educational level can influence perceived marital happiness [15]. In the following sections, we briefly discuss how each of these factors affects marital happiness.

Culture and marital happiness

Studies have shown mixed cross-cultural findings regarding marital happiness [16]. Cross-cultural data from 33 countries showed that determinants of marital happiness vary across cultures in that determinants in one culture can be irrelevant or less important in another culture [1]. In the United Arab Emirates, marital satisfaction was key to marital happiness [17] and that level of education, and communication style were also important for marital happiness [18]. In Jordan, a study showed that the level of marital happiness among 121 Jordanian married teachers was very low. Lack of life-work balance and marital demands may have affected couples' ability to experience marital happiness, with no significant differences in levels of perceived marital happiness associated with gender, age, the age gap between married couples, and years of marriage [19].

Gender, income and marital happiness

Gender and culture may influence levels of marital happiness with mixed cross-cultural findings [20]. Women in some non-Western societies such as Turkey tend to express higher levels of happiness than men, while women in some Western countries may express lower happiness levels [20]. In Middle Eastern cultures, men are the main breadwinners of the family while women are mainly expected to raise children, which may contribute to perceived marital satisfaction as child rearing is a difficult job [14]. On the contrary, in the US, women were more likely to express higher levels of marital happiness if they were satisfied in their intimate relationship, viewed themselves as middle class, were employed, were of Caucasian origin, and were educated [21]. Research has also shown that mental health status may influence gender expression of marital happiness [22]. In a US-based clinical sample, women seeking counseling tended to express lower satisfaction with their marriages than men [23]. On the contrary, Taiwanese men expressed lower marital happiness than women, especially after several years of marriage [24]. In Oman, one study showed that Omani men scored higher in marital adjustment (i.e., feeling of satisfaction and pleasure) than women, possibly due to increased pressure on women to raise children [14]. On the contrary, a study showed that marital happiness was higher among Turkish men than women [15]. The outcomes of the previous studies show mixed findings and that there is a need to understand culture specific determinants of marital happiness.

Income might also play an important role in determining marital happiness. Middle-income couples scored significantly lower in psychological distress and significantly higher in marital satisfaction compared to low-income couples seeking counseling from a community-based clinic in the US [25]. In the US, Caucasian women whose income was higher than that of their husbands reported lower marital happiness, especially when their families were under financial stress [26]. When wives were the main breadwinners, they experienced increasing psychological burnout [26, 27], which may decrease their perceived marital happiness. A meta-analysis study also showed that men generally felt slightly happier in their marriages, especially among couples seeking family therapy [23].

Educational level and marital happiness

Couples' educational level might affect marital happiness. In China, educational level has a significant positive impact on marital satisfaction [28]. Couples who were highly educated tended to score higher in marital satisfaction [28], which is an important indicator of marital happiness. In some Middle Eastern cultures, such as the United Arab Emirates, married couples with higher levels of education are more likely to report higher levels of marital happiness [4]. In another Chinese sample, however, wives might feel less happy in their marriage if their educational level was higher than that of their husbands, which is likely influenced by social norms and the view of gender roles within the Chinese culture [29].

Problem of the study

The literature documents that marriage is the legal path to societal prosperity and safeguards the rights of the wife, husband, and children [30]. Prior research has revealed several determinants of marital happiness and that married people are more likely to be happy than single people [31]. Unmarried, widowed, separated, or divorced individuals are less healthy and happy; and may live shorter than married couples [6, 31]. A better understanding of marital happiness is key to helping couples sustain healthy relationships [14, 32]. Marital happiness in Middle Eastern contexts is not fully researched [33], and this study attempts to fill that gap. The increase in divorce rates in Oman calls for an urgent understanding of marital happiness among Omani couples and whether it can be predicted by some demographic variables, such as gender, duration of marriage, level of education, type of marriage, and place of living. Such an understanding could help professionals and families find best practices that may help reduce divorce rates and help couples identify marital challenges. This study is one of the first attempts to explore determinants of perceived marital happiness among a large sample of Omani married couples.

Methods

Study design

The current study employed a descriptive quantitative design. Data was collected from Omani couples from all the governorates in Oman using a convenience sample technique. Demographic data included the duration of the marriage, the participant’s gender and age, place of living, educational level, occupation, number of children, number of wives, type of residency (independent, with family), type of marriage (kinship vs non-kinship), and monthly family income.

Participants

A convenience sample of 1137 married people participated in this study. The main inclusion criterion was that the respondent was already married. We chose a sample covering all the governorates of the Sultanate of Oman. Of the 1137 participants, 769 (67.6%) were women (Mage = 38.36 years; SDage = 7.97 years). Fifty of the respondents had a pre-secondary education level, 286 had a secondary education level, 644 had a bachelor's degree, and 157 had a postgraduate education level. Approximately 68% of the participants had jobs, while 32% were unemployed. Due to the prevalence of marriage to relatives in Omani culture, 44.7% of the sample was married to relatives, while the remaining sample was married to nonrelatives. The participants varied in their monthly income levels (14.5% had an income less than 500, 35% had an income ranging between 500 and 1000, and 41.3% had an income level of more than 1000 RO). Most of the sample was in a marriage with one wife; a small percentage (4%) were in polygamous marriages.

Instruments

The marital happiness scale

The Arabic version of the Marital Happiness Scale [11] was used to measure perceived marital happiness among married couples in Oman. The scale comprised 32 items distributed into two subscales: moral and financial contribution and marital relationship instability. The moral and financial contribution subscale includes 19 items. Examples of these items are: we help each other at home, I feel happy and satisfied in my marital relationship. The marital relationship instability comprises 13 items. Examples of these items are: I feel frustrated in my marital relationship, and our marital life is full of complicated problems. The scale used a three-point scale (always, sometimes, and never) to measure perceived marital happiness among couples in Oman. The total score for each item is 3 and the lowest is 1. Any scores between 1 and 1.66 are considered low, while means between 1.67 and 2.33 are considered moderate and 2.34 to 3 are high. In examining factorial validity, Al Fazari and colleagues reported strong reliability scores [11]. The reliability of the total scale (α = 0.940) and the two subscales (moral and financial contribution: α = 0.922 and marital relationship instability: α = 0.859); all of which indicate adequate reliability.

Results

General marital happiness in Oman

The present study explored the factors contributing to marital happiness among Omani couples. As shown in Table 1, the average level of perceived marital happiness was high (M = 2.52, SD = 0.35) out of 3. Moreover, marital relationships were perceived to bring high levels of moral and financial contributions (M = 2.53, SD = 0.38). However, the lowest levels were reported in the marital relationship instability (M = 1.57, SD = 0.36). These findings suggest that Omani couples prioritize participation in marital life and seek to avoid tension in their relationships. The results also indicate that moral participation, care for family stability, and the ability to resolve marital issues without third-party involvement are crucial factors in promoting marital satisfaction. Sociodemographic and marital happiness

The study investigated impact of sociodemographic differences (gender, income, education, and governorate) on couples’ level of marital happiness. The researchers used an independent sample t-test to compare men's and women's mean marital happiness scores (see Table 1). Men (M = 2.61, SD = 0.33) had a higher level of perceived moral and financial contribution than women (M = 2.49, SD = 0.40), and the difference between the two averages was statistically significant t = 5.10, p < 0.001. Thus, men tend to perceive themselves as more emotionally and financially involved than women. Furthermore, the level of tension (instability) in the marital relationship between spouses appeared to be low; however, women (M = 1.60, SD = 0.38) perceived more tension than men (M = 1.50, SD = 0.32), and the difference was statistically significant between the two groups, t = 4.84, p < 0.001. Thus, although the overall level of tension is low, females are still more likely to perceive higher tension within the marital relationship. Additionally, men (M = 2.60, SD = 0.30) reported a higher level of general marital happiness than women (M = 2.48, SD = 0.36), and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant t = 5.40, p < 0.001. This means that men generally report greater happiness within their marriages than women. To assess the effect sizes for these comparisons, both Cohen’s d and Hedges’s g were utilized, with Hedges’s g offering a slight adjustment for potential bias in cases of unequal sample sizes (Hedges & Olkin, 1985). Overall, the effect sizes for these comparisons provide further insight. For moral and financial contribution, Cohen's d = 0.327 and Hedges’s g = 0.317 suggest a small effect, reflecting a small practical difference in perceived contribution levels between genders. For marital instability, d = 0.285 and g = 0.276 indicate a small effect size, signifying a relatively minor practical difference in perceived tension. Lastly, the general marital happiness comparison yielded d = 0.362 and g = 0.351, reflecting a small effect but closer to a moderate impact, signifying a somewhat more noticeable difference in marital happiness perceptions between men and women [34].

The present study also aimed to examine the influence of family income, educational level, and governorate on marital happiness using the Kruskal–Wallis test due to the lack of homogeneity of variance among the groups. The results from the Kruskal–Wallis test showed statistically significant differences in marital happiness (participation in marital life) across different income levels. As shown in Table 2, the results indicated significant differences across income groups for moral and financial contribution, marital instability, and general marital happiness. For moral and financial contribution, individuals in the highest income group had the highest mean rank (M = 532.00), while those in the lowest income group had the lowest mean rank (M = 464.91), χ2(2) = 6.581, p = 0.037, with an Eta-squared η2 = 0.004, suggesting a small practical effect. In contrast, for marital instability, the lowest income group had the highest mean rank (M = 600.16), whereas the highest income group had the lowest mean rank (M = 486.23), χ2(2) = 17.982, p < 0.001, with an Eta-squared η2 = 0.014, indicating a slightly larger but still small effect. Similarly, for general marital happiness, the highest income group ranked highest (M = 543.60), while the lowest income group ranked lowest (M = 446.04), χ2(2) = 12.979, p = 0.002, with an Eta-squared η2 = 0.010, reflecting a small but statistically significant effect [40 & 41]. These results confirm the presence of statistically significant differences based on income levels, with a small practical effect size. This suggests that income may play a role in shaping marital satisfaction, particularly in relation to marital instability. Finally, we investigated the potential impact of geographic location, determined by place of living in which the respondents resided, on the level of general marital happiness and its dimensions (moral and financial contribution, marital instability). The Kruskal–Wallis analysis revealed no significant differences in levels of perceived marital happiness according to place of living (χ2(10) = 15.63, 13.59, 12.87; p = 0.111, 0.193, 0.231; η2 = 0.005, 0.003, 0.003, respectively). These findings suggest that geographic location may not significantly shape marital happiness. Other contextual factors, such as cultural and social norms, may have an impact on marital outcomes and warrant further investigation.

Cultural variables and marital happiness

The current study also examined the effect of some cultural variables on marital happiness, such as the number of wives and marriage to relatives/nonrelatives. Despite the small percentage of respondents in marriages with two wives (4%), the researchers were interested in the opinion of this group. The independent samples t-test was used to examine the differences in the marital happiness level among families living with one or two wives. Results indicated an increase in marital life participation for families with only one wife (M = 2.54, SD = 0.37) compared to that in families with two wives (M = 2.26, SD = 0.55), as the difference was statistically significant between the two groups (t = 3.46, p < 0.001). The level of perceived marital instability was higher in families with two wives (M = 1.72, SD = 0.48) than in those with only one wife (M = 1.56, SD = 0.36), and the difference in perceived tension was significant between these two groups (t = 2.16, p < 0.05). The perceived level of marital happiness, in general, was higher in families with only one wife (M = 2.53, SD = 0.34) than in families with two wives (M = 2.30, SD = 0.48), and the difference was statistically significant (t = 3.19, p < 0.01). According to Cohen’s d and Hedges’s g effect size indices, the difference in the level of marital instability can be considered small to moderate (d = 0.377, g = 0.438), while the difference is considered medium to large in marital engagement (d = 0.597, g = 0.739) and medium in marital happiness in general (d = 0.553, g = 0.664). However, the study stresses the importance of expanding the study of this variable by studying more diverse samples when studying the concept of marital happiness among Arab-speaking couples.

As for differences in marital happiness attributed to kinship, the t-test results for the independent groups indicated no statistically significant differences in marital happiness in general (t = 1.50, p = 0.134). The level of perceived marital happiness in the group representing those married to close relatives (M = 2.54, SD = 0.34) was noticeably close to that of the other group representing marriage to nonrelatives (M = 2.51, SD = 0.35). In addition, the results (t = 2.26, p < 0.05) indicated a significant increase in the level of perceived marital instability of nonrelatives (M = 1.59, SD = 0.37) than in marriages with relatives (M = 1.54, SD = 0.36). However, the effect size indicates a very low level of effects of this variable on the level of marital relationship instability (d = 0.137, g = 0.137). The difference between the two groups can is very small and can be neglected despite the presence of statistical significance.

Discussion

Marital happiness is an important factor influencing other life domains. Although widely researched, much remains unknown about the determinants of marital happiness in Middle Eastern cultures, particularly regarding how several variables affect couples’ perception of happiness within marriage. Building on happiness research, we examined reported marital happiness and the impact of gender, age of couples, family income, education level, and duration of marriage on levels of perceived marital happiness among a representative sample of married couples in Oman. Our results are consistent with previous research showing that culture and gender influence perceived marital happiness [31, 37].

Omani couples generally reported high levels of marital happiness and satisfaction. Effective communication and mutual respect between spouses emerged as important contributors to marital happiness. These findings are consistent with previous research that highlighted the importance of effective communication and mutual respect in promoting positive marital outcomes [17]. Moreover, the results highlight the importance of early problem solving in reducing tension in marital life and avoiding the use of hurtful language between spouses. Trust and a sense of psychological stability are also critical for promoting marital happiness [38], with the home serving as a refuge for both partners. Studies also showed that couples need social support to help in solving marital struggles [39]. Professional counselors can help couples promote marital satisfaction and happiness by understanding the different determinants of successful marriages. These findings suggest that promoting positive communication and mutual respect, along with early problem-solving and trust, may be important for enhancing marital satisfaction and reducing tension in marital relationships among Omani couples.

Omani couples also reported high levels of marital happiness particularly if they were middle-class educated couples. Previous research has indicated that money influences couples’ perception of happiness because they worry less about the family's financial struggles [40]. Omani couples whose income was above 500 R.O (approx. 1200$) reported higher marital happiness, which is unsurprising given the increasingly rising inflation rates that can drain family finances.

Our results indicated higher levels of perceived marital happiness among men than women in Oman. Previous research has revealed mixed findings on the impact of gender and perceived marital happiness [6]. Omani men reported significantly higher marital happiness, which could be explained by cultural gender norms. In Middle Eastern cultures, men tend to be less tied down by children’s daily needs, leaving most child-rearing tasks to women. The increased family responsibility within the house and varying gendered perceptions of happiness likely impacted levels of perceived marital happiness. Women in some Western cultures, such as the US, tend to report higher levels of marital happiness than men if they are white, working, and middle class [21]. However, other studies have shown that Taiwanese men report lower marital happiness levels than their partners. We suggest that there is an interaction between gender and culture in terms of the perception of marital happiness and the influence of uneven child-rearing responsibilities within different cultures [41].

In Middle Eastern cultures, women may have higher expectations in terms of childcare, which likely increases psychological pressure. Household responsibilities and childcare may negatively impact the perception of happiness, particularly regarding the unequal distribution of responsibilities within the family. Middle Eastern men, on the other hand, may perceive direct childcare and household responsibilities as part of women’s gender roles. Such a perception may indicate that men are mainly responsible for providing for the family, which may be less stressful than childcare and household chores. Thus, women are the main source of men’s happiness. Similar findings were reported by another study that included a sample of Indian couples [41]. Wives were less happy in their marriages than men, possibly due to high household demands [41].

Level of educational attainment appeared to impact perceived marital happiness. Our results indicate that couples with at least a high school education reported higher levels of marital happiness compared to those without a high school diploma. Interestingly, no significant differences in marital happiness were observed as couples attained more advanced degrees. Education was only significant to a certain degree, possibly due to the relative influence of educational level on family income. The US GSS study discussed how education level, marital happiness, and gender interact [21]. Data indicated that wives’ education was not a significant factor influencing men’s marital happiness, as men may not prioritize their wives’ education when choosing a partner [21]. Conversely, women’s marital happiness may be influenced by their husband’s education level, as this is often associated with family income [21, 40].

Family income is an important determinant of perceived marital happiness. Our results indicated that married couples whose gross income was approximately 1,200 US dollars scored lowest in perceived marital happiness. In contrast, couples whose income was 3,000 + US dollars scored significantly higher than low-income families in terms of perceived marital happiness. Gross family income may be associated with perceived marital happiness due to decreased financial strain [40]. Middle Eastern couples tend to spend money mainly on family necessities. However, family incomes of 3,000 + US dollars are likely to live a more relaxed lifestyle due to moderate cost of living in Oman compared to regional countries, thus allowing for some money spent on entertainment and travel, thus reducing perceived family pressure.

Our results demonstrated no significant differences in marital happiness between couples by place of living. Perceived marital happiness seemed similar across rural and urban parts of Oman, especially in terms of men and women’s fulfillment of family roles. Couples in the Omani culture share similar family norms, values, attitudes, responsibilities, and perceptions of marriage. Although living in urban cities is more costly, husband-wife roles may not be influenced by place of living. As in many cultures, wives are expected to spend substantial time raising children while men are expected to provide for the family [42], and thus levels of psychological pressure vary between the couples. For many Omani working mothers, life-work balance seems to be harder than that among men as work and family responsibilities can easily be overwhelming for working mothers.

The findings also showed that the perceived marital happiness was generally higher in families with only one wife than in families with multiple wives. Though the cost of living is moderate, men are expected to be the main breadwinners, which places higher financial strain and negatively affects the psychological well-being of men in polygamous marriages. Relational conflicts within both households and a significant age gap between children within both households may reflect a wide range of competing demands between households. Constant psychological pressure leaves little opportunity for self and psychological care for husbands and thus impacts their level of happiness. One study found that husbands experienced different psychological problems, such as anxiety and depression, and were more likely to feel less satisfied and have difficulties dealing with their children [43]. The results also showed no statistically significant differences in marital happiness in general in marriage into families of relatives/nonrelatives. Culturally, Omani couples may share the same perception and attitudes towards marriage across all the governorates, possibly due to the same family values adopted by nationwide policies and religious homogeneity.

Implications for research & practice

The current study established that there is a high level of marital happiness in Omani culture, particularly among men. It is unknown; however, why gender differences exist, and further qualitative research can investigate those reasons. Additionally, it is essential to assess marital happiness in relation to relation to job satisfaction, career success, marital adjustment, financial well-being and determining whether it changes over time [42].

Future research should also consider the potential influence of culture on the perception of marital happiness (Sandhya, 2009). In Middle Eastern cultures, marital happiness and satisfaction might be similar, and qualitative studies might help expose that, particularly among more mature couples. Prediction studies could also study the influence of upbringing, age at which couples got married, and work.

Our study provides significant implications for practice. Marriage and family counselors may likely find some of the results helpful when working with clients from Middle Eastern descent. Our study also helps correct misconceptions about polygamous marriages and its relation to higher happiness levels. Our results showed that such an idea is likely a myth, as happiness scores were significantly lower among men in polygamous marriages.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. One main limitation was that the study used convenience sampling, and random sampling could have been more representative of the local community. This might affect data generalizability and, thus, results must be interpreted with caution. The sample size of some governorates could have been more representative, especially for southern governorates due to data collection challenges. Future studies could include more representative samples based on the place of living within the country. The mean age of the sample (Mage = 38.36 years) was another limitation as a more diversified sample could yield different data and implications, particularly for younger couples. Further research could focus on determinants of marital happiness using a random sample technique that includes younger couples as they might be more prone to marital instability than older couples.

Conclusions

Despite some limitations to this study, it provides valuable insight into demographic determinants of marital happiness in a Middle Eastern context. In our study, men reported higher marital happiness than women, which raises the question of whether both men and women perceive marital happiness differently. Women, on the other hand, are usually responsible for house chores and child-rearing, which might cause a lot of psychological pressure. Our study also showed that education might matter in determining marital happiness, suggesting that educational attainment is a significant predictor. Income might also play a significant role in affecting marital happiness, which might be related to educational attainment. Although men seemed to be happier in their marital lives, drawing conclusions for counseling Omani couples should be taken with caution. Mental health professionals should take into consideration the importance of various wellness components that are unique to Middle Eastern people, thus enhancing wellbeing that could also lead to higher marital happiness [44]. More importantly, women, in Middle Eastern cultures, have more household responsibilities, and, thus, might undergo higher psychological pressure. Qualitative studies could reveal other variables leading to such an understanding of gender differences in marital happiness, particularly considering cultural expectations from women. Researchers and clinicians are urged to understand the cultural context well.
