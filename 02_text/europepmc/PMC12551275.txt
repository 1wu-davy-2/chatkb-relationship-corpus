ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexual and Gender Based Violence is a pervasive human rights violation that disproportionately affects vulnerable populations in conflict-affected settings. In Africa, regions such as the Demographic Republic of Congo, South Sudan, and Ethiopia’s Tigray have seen the systematic use of SGBV as a weapon of war, with devastating physical, psychological, and socio-economic consequences. Despite increased attention to this issue, significant barriers persist in addressing SGBV effectively.ObjectiveThis scoping review aims to map the nature, barriers, and facilitators in preventing and responding to sexual and gender based violence in African conflict zones. The review focuses particularly on contexts with ongoing or recent conflict, including Tigray, to inform interventions and identify knowledge gaps.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, guided by the methodological framework of Arksey and O’malley (2005), a comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple datasets including PubMed, CINAHL, Africa Journal Online, and Google Scholar. Both peer-reviewed and gray literatures from 2000 to 2024 were included focusing on African conflict-affected regions. Eligible studies were selected through a three - stage screening process and data were extracted and synthesized thematically.ResultsA total of 39 studies were included. The review identified diverse forms of Sexual and Gender Based Violence ranging from sexual slavery, rape, and torture to intimate partner violence and child marriage. Key barriers to effective response included deep-rooted socio-cultural stigma, weak legal systems, health system collapse, insecurity, limited awareness, and understanding. Facilitators included community engagement, survivor-centered and trauma informed care models, multi-sectorial coordination, legal reforms, and technological innovations such as mobile health services and digital reporting platforms.ConclusionAddressing Sexual and Gender Based Violence in conflict-affected African setting requires a holistic, survivor-centered, and multi-sectorial approach. Political commitment, investment in justice and health systems, and the integration of gender-sensitive strategies into peace building efforts are essential. The review underscores the urgency for coordinated, evidence-based interventions to support survivors, prevent future violence, and contribute to sustainable peace and gender equality. Future research should rigorously evaluate these interventions’ impact and scalability to inform policy and programs in African conflict settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24645-5.

BODY
Introduction

Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) is a pervasive global issue, disproportionately affecting vulnerable individuals in conflict zones [1, 2]. SGBV is defined by the United Nations as acts that inflict physical, sexual, or psychological harm rooted in gender inequality, disproportionately impacts women and girls, particularly in regions experiencing prolonged instability and conflict [3, 4]. In such settings, survivors endure severe physical, psychological, and socio-economic consequences, often compounded by disrupted social systems, limited access to justice, and heightened exposure to violence [2, 5, 6]. Addressing SGBV in conflict zones is not only a matter of human rights but also integral to fostering resilience, promoting community health, and advancing sustainable peace building [1, 7, 8].

The African continent has faced numerous armed conflicts over the decades, ranging from protracted civil wars to localized ethnic clashes [9]. Countries, such as South Sudan, Somalia, the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Sudan, and Ethiopia, face significant challenges related to SGBV due to ongoing political instability, ethnic tensions, and humanitarian crises. In these regions, sexual violence is often used as a weapon of war—a tactic to instill fear, displace communities, and destroy social cohesion [5, 10]. For instance, the Tigray war in northern Ethiopia (2020–2023) witnessed widespread reports of sexual violence, underscoring the urgent need for effective interventions [3, 5, 11]. Survivors in such contexts face formidable challenges, including inadequate healthcare services, limited psychosocial support, and pervasive impunity for perpetrators [5, 9]. Similarly, other conflict zones in Africa, such as the Central African Republic and the DRC, reveal endemic patterns of SGBV, highlighting its widespread nature in war-affected regions [6, 12].

Crises, including armed conflicts, natural disasters, and climate change, exacerbate the risks of SGBV, with estimates suggesting that up to 70% of women in humanitarian settings experience gender-based violence [7]. Conflict related sexual violence (CRSV)- encompassing acts such as rape, sexual slavery, forced marriage, and forced sterilization- affects women, men, girls, boys and other vulnerable individuals, leaving lasting scars on individuals and communities [6, 9]. Post-conflict periods further complicate the issue, as the cessation of active hostilities often fails to address the structural vulnerabilities that perpetuate SGBV [4, 8]. Stigma, fear of retribution, and a lack of adequate infrastructure, including healthcare and justice systems, deter survivors from seeking help, perpetuating cycles of silence and injustice [1, 7, 12, 13].

Despite growing attention, addressing SGBV in conflict and post-conflict settings remains a multifaceted challenge [8, 14]. This scoping review adopts a rights-based theoretical framework, emphasizing the inherent rights of individuals to safety, dignity, and protection, which aligns with the objectives of promoting justice and survivor-centered responses in these contexts. Promising context-tailored facilitating factors offer viable pathways for mitigating SGBV and supporting affected populations [7, 9]. Additionally, addressing SGBV is directly relevant to achieving several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including those related to gender equality, health, peace, and justice, underscoring the importance of integrating global development frameworks into local conflict settings [15]. Therefore, this review systematically explores and synthesizes peer-reviewed literature on the nature, barriers and facilitators to addressing SGBV in conflict-affected areas of Africa, with a particular focus on countries experiencing active conflict, such as those in East Africa, and the Tigray region of Ethiopia as a case study [2, 3, 12]. By critically analyzing existing research, the scoping aims to identify knowledge gaps, highlight best practices, and provide actionable insights to inform policy and program development [8, 9, 12].

Given the urgent need for evidence based solutions to combat SGBV in Africa’s conflict and post-conflict zones, this review contributes to the growing body of knowledge required to inform effective interventions [1, 5, 13, 16, 17]. By centering localized perspectives, such as those from Ethiopia’s Tigray, within the continental discourse, the findings of this review aims to support researchers, policy makers, and practitioners in alleviating the burden of SGBV and fostering resilience in communities by conflict [5, 11].

Research question

How is SGBV manifested in African conflict zones, and what factors hinder or enable effective prevention and response efforts?

Methods

Study design

This scoping review was conducted following the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’malley [17], which involves five key stages: (1) identifying the research questions (2), identifying relevant studies (3), selecting studies (4), charting the data, and (5) Summarizing, and reporting the results. Reporting adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [18].

The review protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/da9tm).

Results are presented in both tabular and narrative form, highlighting cross-cutting patterns and research gaps.

Characteristics of eligible studies

Appendix I present the characteristics of the included records. The studies were conducted and published between 2010 and 2024, with the exception of two articles published in 2005 and 2008, respectively [19, 20]. Nearly half of the articles (n=22) included were peer-reviewed [11, 21–33], while 13 were organizational reports, including five working papers [34–38]. The remaining four articles were policy papers and newsletters [39–43].

In terms of territorial or geographical classifications based on World Bank classification of African countries by region [44], the studies covered multiple regions across Africa. Two articles were from North Africa [35, 39], eight from West Africa [27, 28, 32, 36, 38, 45, 46], and five from Central Africa [33, 43, 46–48]. Twenty-one studies focused on East Africa [11, 21–24, 29–31, 37, 49–55], while three were from Southern Africa [25, 39, 40].

With respect to conflict timelines, approximately 41% (n=16) of the included studies examined ongoing conflicts, including the 2023 Sudan war [34, 41], Ethiopia's Tigray tensions [11, 24, 50, 52], Nigeria's [32, 45, 56], Burkina Faso’s displacement crisis [27], DRC’s resurgence [33, 46–48], South Sudan’s factionalism [30, 53, 57], and Eritrea’s militarization [55]. Fourteen studies focused on frozen conflicts in South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda [21–23, 25, 26, 29–31, 37, 39, 51, 53]. The remaining nine analyzed post-conflict recovery in Rwanda [58, 59], Liberia [26, 36], Sierra Leone [28, 36], Côte d'Ivoire [35], and Burundi’s instability [43].

Understanding the nature or forms of SGBV in conflict settings

The included studies reported multiple and overlapping forms of SGBV across conflict-affected regions in Africa. These manifestations are shaped by the intersection of conflict-related instability, entrenched gender inequalities, and socio-political oppression. The main forms of SGBV documented are summarized below.

Sexual violence as a weapon of war

Sexual violence has been systematically employed as a strategic weapon of war to terrorize, humiliate, and destabilize communities. In Ethiopia’s Tigray conflict, evidence shows that sexual violence was deliberately used to inflict terror on civilians, erode family bonds, and disrupt social cohesion, leaving deep psychological scars on survivors and their communities [11, 24, 50, 52]. Similar patterns were observed in Northern Uganda, where the Lord’s Resistance Army committed widespread rapes, sexual slavery, forced pregnancies, and exploitation of women as tools of war [21, 31, 54]. In the Eastern DRC, armed groups weaponized rape as a tactic for ethnic cleansing and asserting dominance [33, 46–48]. Survivors of the Rwandan genocide described rape and sexual torture as deliberate acts aimed at destroying cultural identity, particularly targeting Tutsi women [58, 59].

Rape in displacement settings

Included studies reported thatrefugee and displacement camps are high-risk environments for sexual violence due to inadequate security and protection mechanisms. Women and girls were found to face heightened risks of rape by armed groups, fellow refugees, and in some cases, humanitarian workers [22, 27, 49, 53]. Evidence from Uganda’s refugee camps indicated that lack of privacy and overcrowding in camps increased vulnerability to rape and sexual exploitation [21, 22, 30]. Similar patterns were documented in Rwanda, where adolescent girls were disproportionately targeted/affected by sexual violence [58, 59].

Intimate partner violence (IPV)

Conflict-induced displacement, the breakdown of social structures, and economic instability significantly amplify the prevalence of intimate partner violence (IPV). In South Sudanese refugee settings, women were subjected to physical, emotional, and sexual abuse, often driven by men’s frustration and stress associated with the loss of traditional provider roles [30, 53, 57]. Similar increases in IPV were reported among displaced women in Northern Nigeria, where displacement and insecurity heightened risks of domestic violence [32, 45, 56]. In Ethiopia, survivors of conflict-related sexual violence also described increased IPV linked to displacement and economic hardships [11, 24, 50, 52].

Child and forced marriage

Conflicts were found to contribute to rising rates of child and forced marriages across South Sudan, Uganda, Ethiopia, and Nigeria [31, 50, 57]. In South Sudan, child marriage was used as a survival mechanism within displaced communities [30, 57]. In Uganda, girls abducted by the Lord’s Resistance Army were forcibly married to combatants, resulting in prolonged sexual abuse and servitude [21, 31, 54]. In Ethiopia and Nigeria, child and forced marriages were also documented as widespread during conflict periods, with families arranging unions to secure resources or shield daughters from violence [11, 24, 45, 56]

Sexual exploitation and abuse

Resource scarcity in displacement settings forces many women and girls into exploitative sexual relationships to access food, shelter, or basic services. Studies from the Dadaab refugee camp in Kenya and Rwanda reveal that survival sex is a common coping mechanism for displaced women [49, 58]. In some area families, to protect their daughters from sexual violence, often marry them off at an early age. In Sudan and South Sudan, reports have highlighted widespread sexual exploitation, where women and girls engage in sexual acts in exchange for basic necessities [34, 57]. This exploitation is institutionalized in some settings, as both refugees and aid workers exploit the vulnerability of displaced women and girls [35].

Torture and sexualized violence

Sexualized forms of torture, including forced sterilization, genital mutilation, and sexual assault, are used by armed groups to punish and humiliate victims. Testimonies from, Ethiopia, Eritrea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone highlight the use of sexual violence as a method of interrogation and collective punishment during conflicts [24, 26, 28, 58]. For example, reports from Ethiopia’s Tigray war have shown that perpetrators tortured Tigrayan women while stating, ‘‘You did nothing wrong to us. Our problem is with your womb. Your womb gives birth to Woyane [a derogatory term used to refer to the Tigray People Liberation Front, a political parity]. A Tigrayan womb should never give birth”[11].

Sexual violence against men and boys

Included studies reported that men and boys in conflict settings also experience sexual violence, although it is often underreported. Documented forms include forced sodomy, genital mutilation, and sexual humiliation, used as tools of emasculation and control. Evidence from the DRC, Northern Uganda, Tigray (Ethiopia), and Eritrea highlighted significant underreporting due to stigma and fear of reprisals [11, 31, 46, 55].

Wartime sexual slavery

Included studies reported that women and girls in conflict zones were forcibly held by armed combatants for prolonged sexual servitude. Evidence from Tigray (Ethiopia), Northern Uganda (Kampala) and Rwanda documented such experiences of sexual slavery and exploitation [11, 31, 34, 59].

Psychological and symbolic forms of sexual violence

Studies reportednon-physical forms of sexual violence, such as forced nudity and public rape, used to humiliate victims and entire communities. Evidence from Rwandan genocide and Tigray’s Ethiopia highlighted repeated acts of sexual violence as deliberate strategies during conflict, causing severe psychological trauma among survivors described severe psychological trauma from witnessing or being forced to participate in acts of violence [11, 30, 59].

Impacts of sexual and Gender-Based violence (SGBV)

The consequences and impact of SGBV in conflict settings across Africa are extensive and multifaceted, affecting survivors, their families, services providers, and entire communities. These impacts can be categorized into the following key thematic areas:

Psychological and mental health consequences

Included studies reported that, survivors of SGBV in conflict setting frequently experience profound psychological distress, yielding conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [11, 28, 39, 45, 58], depression [25, 27, 34], anxiety [17, 22, 25, 37], and social ideation [24, 27, 29, 32, 57]. The trauma associated with sexual violence is often compounded by stigma, discrimination, rejection. And social isolation, preventing survivors from accessing support networks and reintegrating into their communities [24, 25, 30, 38, 54, 55] ). To cope with trauma, survivors often adopt maladaptive strategies such as avoidance, dissociation, or substance abuse, which complicate psychological recovery and create intergenerational consequences [33, 34, 42, 48, 56].

Physical health reproductive morbidities

The physical health consequences of SGBV are severe, often resulting in long-term medical conditions. Survivors commonly suffer from reproductive health complications, including obstetrics fistulas, chronic public pain, sexually transmitted infections, infertility, and complications from unsafe abortions [11, 21, 25]. Many women face unintended pregnancies, often with children born of rape, further exacerbating social stigma and economic hardship [27, 35, 59]. In Conflict settings, limited access to maternal and reproductive health care worsens these conditions, leading to preventable deaths and lifelong disabilities [11, 39, 47, 50].

Mortality: direct violence and neglect

Many survivors of sexual violence face fatal outcomes, either due to direct violence- such as honor killings or suicide driven by social stigma or through neglect arising from a lack of timely medical and psychological care. Studies from Uganda, Liberia, and Rwanda highlight how overwhelming vulnerability to HIV/AIDS, psychological distress, and social rejection often result in suicide among survivors [21, 25, 26, 59]. Neglect-related mortality has also been documented, with cases of survivors succumbing to untreated injuries, obstetric complications, or infections related to sexual violence [27, 39, 59]. In conflict-affected areas, the absence of comprehensive medical and mental health services further exacerbates these risks, thereby potentiating mortality among survivors [11, 23, 28, 57].

Vicarious or secondary trauma and stress among service providers

The effects of SGBV extend beyond survivors, significantly impacting healthcare providers, humanitarian workers, and law enforcement personnel who respond to cases of sexual violence. Continuous exposure to survivors’ trauma, combined with inadequate institutional support and high caseloads, leads to secondary traumatic stress, burnout, and emotional exhaustion among frontline workers [21, 22, 25, 38, 42, 43]. Many services providers also experience compassion fatigue, which reduces their capacity to deliver quality care and psychosocial support to survivors [25, 28, 45]. The lack of structural mental health support for providers, such as counseling, debriefing mechanisms, or peer-support systems - further exacerbates these challenges and creating an additional barrier to effective and survivor centered service delivery [52, 57].

Societal and community disintegration or breakdown

In conflict settings, SGBV disrupts social cohesion, trust, and disrupts family and community dynamics, leading to rejection, displacement, and breakdown of traditional support systems. Many survivors, particularly women and girls, are ostracized by their families and communities due to the stigma associated with sexual violence [11, 21, 28, 48, 53, 56]. Children born of wartime rape often face discrimination and neglect, growing up in unstable environments with limited access to education and social support [24, 27, 35, 37, 46] The rejection of survivors by their families contributes to increased rates of homelessness, child abandonment, and vulnerability to further exploitation [32, 33, 39].

The social and psychological consequences of SGBV are not limited to one generation. Several studies highlight the intergenerational transmission of trauma, where children growing up in contexts of sexual violence, or witnessing the rejection and marginalization of their mothers, are more likely to experience adverse outcomes such as poor mental health, substance abuse, and violence perpetration [24, 34, 35, 37, 46]. Survivors who lack adequate psychological support frequently struggle with parenting responsibilities, leading to strained family relationships and perpetuating cycles of violence and social breakdown [28, 32, 38, 45].

Economic disempowerment and poverty

SGBV significantly reduces survivors’ ability to engage in economic activities, leading to financial insecurity and dependence. Many survivors experience job loss or are unable to return to work due to trauma, physical injuries, or stigma from employers and communities [19, 20, 28, 30, 39]. For displaced survivors, access to education and employment is often severely limited, forcing them into exploitative labor or transactional sex as survival strategies [11, 36, 44].

The economic consequences of SGBV extend beyond individuals to families and communities.

The loss or incapacitation of reproductive-age survivors diminishes household income, increases dependency, and weakens overall community resilience and economic stability [32–34, 38, 59]. Survivors’ inability to fully participate in livelihoods perpetuates cycles of poverty, particularly in conflict-affected settings where social and economic support systems are already fragile.

Interconnection between SGBV and the SDGs in conflict settings of Africa

SGBV in African conflict settings profoundly undermines progress toward multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including poverty reduction (SDG 1), health (SDG 3), gender equality (SDG 5), economic growth (SDG 8), and justice (SDG 16). Survivors frequently experience job loss, school dropouts, and economic dependency, which hinder financial independence and perpetuate cycles of poverty and disempowerment, as documented among displaced women in Nigeria, Uganda, and Burkina Faso [27, 45, 56]. In refugee settings such as the Dadaab camp in Kenya and the Kiziba camp in Rwanda, women struggle to rebuild livelihoods due to limited financial support and employment opportunities, reflecting structural barriers to empowerment [49, 58, 59].

The health burden is equally severe. High rates of PTSD, depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation were reported among survivors in Liberia, Uganda, South Africa, the DRC, and Ethiopia [24, 28, 39, 51]. Physical consequences, including obstetric fistulas, chronic pelvic pain, and heightened HIV/AIDS vulnerability, were documented in the DRC, Nigeria, Kenya, and Eritrea [11, 20, 21, 39]. Social stigma further isolates survivors, while forced unions and child marriages were observed in Rwanda, South Sudan, Uganda, and Ethiopia, reinforcing entrenched gender inequalities [11, 23–25, 57].

Weak and often inaccessible justice systems exacerbate the problem. Survivors in Ethiopia, Uganda, South Sudan, and Kenya face significant barriers to justice and service access, including lack of legal recourse, inadequate protective mechanisms, and limited availability of survivor-centered care [11, 22, 35, 37].

Barriers to addressing SGBV in conflict zones of Africa

The review identified several key barriers in addressing SGBV in conflict zones across Africa. These barriers were categorized into socio-cultural, lack of awareness and education, security and mobility challenges, legal and policy barriers, and economic and institutional constraints.

Socio-cultural barriers

Deep-rooted socio-cultural norms significantly impede efforts to address SGBV in African conflict settings. Survivors often face intense social ostracization and stigma, which discourage help-seeking and engagement with available services [27, 31, 35]. Patriarchal structures reinforce gender inequalities, limiting women’s and girls’ access to justice and support systems [40, 51]. These harmful gender roles and expectations make it difficult for women to challenge their abusers or pursue justice. Cultural silence - norms discouraging discussions about sexual violence-further exacerbate the problem, increasing survivors’ vulnerability to secondary victimization [23, 43]. Societal pressures often compel survivors to remain silent to avoid bringing shame to their families or communities, perpetuating cycles of abuse and impunity [32, 47]. Intersectionality challenges arise when survivors belong to marginalized groups, including displaced persons, ethnic minorities, or individuals with disabilities. These groups experience compounded discrimination, further limiting their access to care, justice, and protective mechanisms [26, 47].

Lack of awareness, advocacy and education

Limited access to information on SGBV rights and services hinders efforts to combat sexual violence in conflict settings. Many survivors and their communities lack awareness of available legal and medical support, which delays or completely prevents help-seeking behavior ( [11, 32, 35, 49]. In several instances, survivors do not know that services exist or that they have legal rights to seek redress. Myths and misinformation about sexual violence and its consequences, including misconceptions that survivors are to blame or that reporting abuse could bring shame to the family, create additional obstacles [39, 58]. These misconceptions often deter victims from speaking out, fearing negative repercussions such as rejection from family members or retribution from perpetrators [11, 23, 40]. Furthermore, this review highlighted gaps in advocacy and political commitment to address SGBV in African conflict zones [19, 25, 38, 50].

Security and mobility challenges

Ongoing conflicts and persistent insecurity in war-affected regions severely limit survivors’ ability to report cases or access essential services. Geographic barriers, including remote locations, destroyed infrastructure, and lack of transportation, make it difficult for survivors to reach health and legal support [27, 35, 45, 52]. Impunity for perpetrators further discourages survivors from coming forward, as fear of retaliation and distrust in the justice system are widespread [25, 36, 38, 53, 55].

Armed groups and militias frequently employ sexual violence as a weapon of war, intensifying the challenges of ensuring justice and accountability [11, 25, 28]. In some contexts, legal institutions prioritize political stability over individual redress, effectively neglecting survivors’ rights [25, 28]. Political instability driven by conflict further weakens law enforcement and survivor protection mechanisms [36, 38]. Survivors attempting to seek justice often encounter threats from perpetrators, corruption within law enforcement, or outright inaction from authorities, creating additional barriers to legal recourse and perpetuating cycles of violence and vulnerability [25, 28, 52].

Legal and policy barriers

Weak legal frameworks and poor enforcement exacerbate SGBV in conflict settings. Survivors face corrupt judicial processes, lack of representation, and bureaucratic delays, limiting access to justice [21, 28]. Laws often fail to criminalize all forms of gender-based violence or protect conflict-affected survivors, while coordination between law enforcement, medical, and humanitarian services remains weak [23, 25]. Despite international and national policies, implementation is hindered by limited political will, funding gaps, and ongoing insecurity [11, 28]. Structural, community, and individual factors - including weak governance, patriarchal norms, displacement, stigma, economic dependency, trauma, and fear of retaliation, further restrict survivor support and recovery [25, 45].

Economic and institutional barriers

Poverty and financial dependency increase women’s and girls’ vulnerability to SGBV in conflict settings, limiting access to legal, medical, and psychosocial support [27, 45]. Economic constraints often force survivors to remain in abusive situations due to lack of financial independence [45, 49].

Conflict-related destruction of health facilities and infrastructure further impedes access to essential services, including post-rape medical care and psychological support [11, 46]. Weak and underfunded SGBV programs, constrained by limited government and humanitarian resources, reduce the availability of comprehensive, survivor-centered care [23]. Without sustained institutional investment, SGBV response efforts remain fragmented and ineffective [23, 40].

Facilitators and enablers in addressing SGBV in conflict zones of Africa

The review of the selected literature highlights various facilitators and enablers in addressing SGBV in conflict zones of Africa. These facilitators are categorized into key themes that reflect community engagement and social mobilization, multi-sectoral coordination, capacity building, survivor-centeric service delivery, trauma-informed care interventions, gender-sensitive peace building, technological innovations, and justice mechanisms.

Community engagement and social mobilization

Community engagement and social mobilization are critical components in addressing SGBV in conflict zones. Engaging religious and traditional leaders plays a significant role in shifting cultural perceptions about gender and violence, promoting survivor-centeric interventions, and fostering community support networks [35, 51, 56] These leaders have strong societal influence and can advocate behavioral and attitudinal change within their communities, encouraging acceptance and support for survivors. Survivor-led advocacy ensures that those directly affected by SGBV have a voice in policy-making and service provision, resulting in interventions that are more relevant and effective [25, 30, 43, 47]. Grassroots activism, survivor networks, and community dialogues empower individuals to challenge harmful norms and create safer environments for affected populations [27, 34].

Local initiatives are equally vital, leveraging community-driven solutions and grassroots organizations to address SGBV. Community-based protection networks engage local leaders, religious figures, and women's groups to raise awareness, mediate conflicts, and support survivors. Safe spaces for women and girls provide essential services, including psychosocial support, legal aid, and skills training, helping survivors rebuild their lives and regain autonomy [39].

Multi-sectoral coordination and response

A coordinated, multi-sectoral approach enhances the response to SGBV by integrating services across health, legal, and social domains. Health-legal-social collaboration fosters comprehensive care for survivors by ensuring they receive medical treatment, psychosocial support, and legal assistance in an integrated manner [11, 31, 40]. This triadic collaboration also helps eliminate service fragmentation, reducing the burden on survivors to navigate complex bureaucratic systems. The establishment of referral pathways is essential to improve access to essential services and ensure that survivors receive timely and effective interventions [34, 58]. Well-structured referral mechanisms between healthcare providers, legal institutions, and community organizations streamline support systems, increasing both efficiency and the reach of SGBV services for survivors [25, 43, 49].

Capacity Building and training of service providers

Strengthening the capacity of services providers is crucial for ensuring high-quality, survivor- centeric care. Specialized training for health care providers enhances their ability to offer trauma-informed care, ensuring that survivors receive compassionate and non-judgmental support [39, 57]. Additionally, training law enforcement officials equips them with the necessary skills to handle SGBV cases sensitively, avoid victim-blaming, and prioritize survivor safety [20, 28, 40] This is particularly important in conflict zones where impunity for perpetrators is high. Community education initiatives raise awareness about SGBV, reduce stigma, and encourage survivors to seek help [25, 27, 36]. Public awareness campaigns, school-based education programs, and interactive workshops contribute to long-term behavioral change and the prevention of gender-based violence [47, 51].

Survivor-centeric service delivery

A survivor-centered approach ensures that services prioritize the dignity, autonomy, and well-being of survivors. One-stop centers provide integrated services, reducing the logistical and emotional burden on survivors seeking help ( [28, 40, 54]. These centers offer medical care, psychological support, legal aid, and case management under one roof, facilitating access to comprehensive support. Mobile clinics and outreach services expand access for survivors in remote or conflict-affected areas where formal services are scarce [44, 46, 49]. Such initiatives are particularly beneficial in humanitarian settings, where displacement and insecurity create additional barriers to service utilization.

Trauma-informed care (TIC) interventions

Applying trauma-informed care (TIC) principles ensures that survivors receive support in a manner that acknowledges and minimizes re-traumatization. TIC emphasizes safety, trust, choice, collaboration, and empowerment [23]. Psychosocial and mental health supports are critical for survivor recovery, addressing issues such as PTSD, anxiety, and depression [25, 37, 52]. Health care responses and medical support focus on both immediate and long-term health needs, including forensic examinations, treatment for sexually transmitted infections, and reproductive health services [44, 46, 49, 60]. Survivor reintegration programs promote social acceptance, economic empowerment, and long-term resilience, assisting survivors rebuild their lives [38, 40].

Gender- sensitive peace Building and conflict prevention

Ensuring that women play an active role in peace building processes is vital for sustainable conflict resolution. Women’s leadership and inclusion in peace negotiations lead to more comprehensive policies that address gender-based violence and broader security concerns 28,40). Community-based early warning systems help prevent violence by identifying risks and mobilizing resources for intervention before conflicts escalate [37, 53]. These systems rely on local knowledge and community engagement to detect early signs of violence, facilitating proactive responses.

Technology and innovation in SGBV responses

Advancements in technology and digital tools have transformed SGBV responses. Digital solutions, such as mobile apps and hotlines, provide survivors with confidential support, information on available services, and emergency assistance [54, 55]. Online platforms enable safe reporting mechanisms and virtual counseling, overcoming barriers to in-person support [19, 49]. Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used for data analysis, helping organizations track trends, assess intervention effectiveness, and develop evidence-based policies [20]. AI-powered systems can also predict risk patterns, allowing for timely and targeted interventions in high-risk areas [20, 49].

Justice and accountability mechanisms

Addressing SGBV in conflict zones requires robust justice and accountability mechanisms to ensure perpetrators are held responsible and survivors receive legal redress. Strengthening judicial responses involves improving legal frameworks, training judges and prosecutors, and increasing access to legal aid for survivors [25, 36, 40]. Establishing specialized SGBV courts can expedite case processing and enhance survivor protection [45]. Community-based justice approaches offer alternative dispute resolution mechanisms that are contextually and culturally relevant and accessible, complementing formal legal systems [23, 25].Restorative justice programs also play a role in survivor healing by focusing on acknowledgment, reparation, and reintegration [57, 59].

Discussion

This scoping review of 39 studies on SGBV in conflict zones across Africa underscores the pervasive and mutifaced nature of the problem. The reviewed studies provide critical insights into the nature of SGBV, the barriers that hinder effective responses, and the facilitators that enhance survivor support and prevention efforts. This discussion synthesizes the key themes emerging from the review, compares them with a global perspective, and highlights implications for policy and practice.

The reviewed studies consistently highlight the widespread and systematic use of SGBV as a weapon of war in conflict-affected regions such as Northern Uganda [60], the DRC [61], South Sudan [62], and Ethiopia [63]. SGBV is not only a tactic of war but also a tool for community destruction, ethnic cleansing, and long-term social destabilization. The targeted nature of such violence disproportionately affects women and girls, but men and boys are also victims, often as a means of humiliation and emasculation. Survivors experience not only immediate physical harm but also long-term psychological and social consequences, including stigmatization, displacement, and economic deprivation [64, 65].

The review also demonstrates the health consequences of SGBV. Survivors are at elevated risk of acquiring HIV/AIDS and other sexually transmitted infections due to exposure to multiple perpetrators, limited access to post-rape care, and barriers to testing and treatment services in conflict affecting countries such as Eritrea and Liberia [13, 45, 59].

The inability to access testing, treatment, and support services exacerbates the health crisis, leaving survivors vulnerable to lifelong medical complications. Furthermore, in contexts where stigma surrounding HIV/AIDS is already high, sexual violence survivors who contract HIV face double discrimination. When comparing these findings with global studies, similar trends emerge in conflict zones, such as Syria, Afghanistan, and Myanmar, where sexual violence is used to destabilize communities and enforce control over populations [66, 67]. Studies from Bosnia and Rwanda have highlighted the long-term psychological trauma and intergenerational impact of conflict-related SGBV, drawing parallels with the African cases [68, 69]. This implies that, the inability to fully integrate SGBV survivors into post-conflict reconciliation efforts has prolonged societal divisions.

When compared with global studies, patterns of sexual violence in conflict zones such as Syria, Afghanistan, and Myanmar follow similar trends [70]. In these regions, SGBV has been used to destabilize communities, displace populations, and control marginalized groups. The Syrian civil war, for instance, has seen widespread reports of sexual torture in detention centers, forced marriages, and trafficking of women and girls [71]. In Afghanistan, the Taliban’s rule has systematically restricted women's rights, enabling the institutionalization of gender-based oppression [72]. Similarly, in Myanmar, Rohingya women have faced mass rapes as part of ethnic cleansing campaigns [73]. Studies from Bosnia and Rwanda further illustrate the long-term impact of conflict-related SGBV. Survivors in Bosnia still struggle with psychological trauma, economic hardship, and social ostracization decades after the conflict. Rwanda's genocide left thousands of women raped and impregnated by perpetrators, leading to intergenerational trauma that affects not only survivors but also their children [74]. The inability to fully integrate SGBV survivors into post-conflict reconciliation efforts has prolonged societal divisions.

Despite these similarities, differences exist in how various nations address conflict-related SGBV. In African conflict zones, such as DRC, South Sudan, and Ethiopia, weak judicial systems often fail to hold perpetrators accountable, while post-war tribunals in Bosnia and other global contexts have provided more structured mechanisms for justice [75]. The availability of survivor support services also varies significantly, with global cases generally offering stronger post-war rehabilitation programs, while many African contexts lack adequate mental health, economic, and social reintegration initiatives [5, 66].

Several barriers prevent effective interventions despite international commitments to protect women and girls in conflict zones.

Weak legal and institutional frameworks fail to hold perpetrators accountable, and many survivors lack access to survivor-centeric health care and mental health services in war-torn countries. Deep rooted social stigmas discourage survivors from reporting cases and limited economic and educational opportunities make reintegration difficult. Addressing these challenges requires strengthening legal accountability by increasing support for international tribunals and local justice systems, ensuring access to comprehensive health care, and promoting community-based survivor support programs [76]. Expanding access to mental networks, post-rape care, and economic empowerment initiatives modeled after successful post-genocide recovery programs in Rwanda can help mitigate the long-term consequences of SGBV [77] . Despite the numerous barriers that hinder the effectiveness of interventions for addressing SGBV in conflict zones, several key facilitators have emerged that contribute to more impactful and sustainable solutions.

Community based support mechanisms, such as survivor networks and peer counseling, have played a pivotal role in fostering resilience and empowerment among survivors. These initiatives have been instrumental in facilitating recovery, as evidenced in contexts like Rwanda and South Sudan [67, 78], where survivors have benefitted from strong social networks and community-driven support systems. The establishment of such mechanisms not only provides emotional and psychological healing but also serves to reduce the stigma associated with SGBV, promoting broader societal acceptance and understanding [69, 70].

Moreover, integrating SGBV services into broader health and humanitarian responses has improved access to care in refugee settings. By creating a coordinated, multi-sectoral system, including healthcare, legal aid, and psychosocial support- this approach ensures timely and holistic care, particularly for marginalized populations [64, 79].

Looking to global models, several innovative frameworks have been identified that offer valuable lessons for addressing SGBV in crisis settings. The safe spaces initiative in Syria, for example, has demonstrated the potential of creating dedicated spaces for survivors to access critical support services in a safe and confidential environment [61]. Similarly, the gender-based violence information management system in Central America has proven effective in streamlining data collection and analysis, enhancing the coordination of services and enabling more targeted interventions [80]. These initiatives underscore the importance of context-specific, adaptable solutions that can be integrated into broader emergency response efforts.

International organizations, including the World Health Organizations and the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), have consistently advocated for survivor-centeric approaches that integrate legal, health, and psychosocial support services [78] .This comprehensive model of care is essential to addressing the multifaceted needs of survivors and ensuring that their voices are at the center of response efforts. The success of gender-transformative approaches in post-conflict contexts, such as in Bosnia and East Timor, further highlights the importance of engaging local communities in SGBV prevention and response strategies.

The findings of this review emphasize the urgent need for a multi-sectoral approach to addressing SGBV in conflict zones. This approach should encompass legal, health, social, and economic interventions, recognizing that SGBV is a complex issue that requires coordinated efforts from various sectors. Strengthening legal and policy frameworks to hold perpetrators accountable is a critical step in deterring violence and ensuring that survivors have access to justice. In tandem, increasing funding for survivor-centeric health services is essential to ensure that survivors can receive the comprehensive care they need. Furthermore, the promotion of gender-transformative interventions, particularly those that engage both men and women in challenging harmful gender norms, is key to creating sustainable change.

Equally important is the integration of SGBV response into post-conflict reconstruction efforts. Addressing SGBV in the aftermath of conflict not only provides immediate relief to survivors but also contributes to long-term social stability and peace building. By prioritizing the needs of survivors in reconstruction and development initiatives, communities can rebuild in a way that is inclusive, equitable, and focused on healing. This integration ensures that survivors are not left behind and that their recovery is prioritized as part of the broader effort to rebuild societies affected by conflict.

At the international level, frameworks like the UN Women’s call to action on protection from gender-based violence in emergencies offer strategic guidance that aligns with best practices observed in African contexts and other regions. This global framework emphasizes the importance of coordinated, multi-sectoral action, as well as the need for accountability, funding, and capacity building to address SGBV in humanitarian emergencies [63, 69]. Additionally, international organizations, including the Global Fund for Women, have played a crucial role in funding community-led initiatives that focus on preventing and responding to SGBV [76] By supporting grassroots efforts, these organizations help to amplify the voices of survivors and ensure that interventions are culturally appropriate and contextually relevant. This exemplar revealed that, addressing SGBV in conflict zones requires a comprehensive, survivor-centeric approach that incorporates legal, healthcare, psychosocial, and community-based interventions, and ensuring the integration of SGBV response into post-conflict reconstruction efforts will be critical in creating long-term solutions to this pervasive issue.

This scoping review benefits from a rigorous methodological approach (PRISMA-ScR) and the inclusion of diverse sources, capturing comprehensive perspectives on SGBV in African conflict zones. Its focus on survivor-centered and multi-sectoral strategies enhances practical relevance for policymakers and practitioners. Nonetheless, the review has some limitations, including potential publication bias, exclusion of non-English literature, and heterogeneity in study designs. Data gaps from active conflict zones may underrepresent certain barriers, such as violence against men and boys. Despite these limitations, the findings provide critical insights to guide context-sensitive interventions and inform policy and program development in fragile settings.

Conclusion

Addressing SGBV in African conflict and post-conflict settings requires coordinated, multi-sectoral strategies that tackle structural, social, and economic barriers. Deeply entrenched patriarchal norms, weak legal frameworks, security constraints, and stigma, particularly among marginalized groups such as displaced populations and people with disabilities, hinder survivors’ access to justice and care. Effective interventions include community mobilization and advocacy, survivor-centered and trauma-informed services, multi-sectoral coordination, economic empowerment, and technological innovations to improve access and service delivery.

Strengthening legal systems, promoting women’s participation in governance and peace building, and ensuring sustained investment and political commitment are essential to achieve accountability, resilience, and long-term social inclusion. Integrating these approaches within holistic frameworks can enhance the prevention of SGBV, support survivor recovery, and foster sustainable, inclusive post-conflict reconstruction.

Furthermore, future research is recommended to evaluate the effectiveness and scalability of these interventions across diverse African conflict contexts, generating evidence to inform policy, program design, and implementation strategies.
