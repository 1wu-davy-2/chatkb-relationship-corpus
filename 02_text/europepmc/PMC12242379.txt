ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesAlthough marital status influences cognitive health, limited research has examined the effects of diverse marital histories, particularly regarding remarriage. This study investigates the relationship between remarriage, marital histories, and cognitive impairment with a focus on gender differences. Building on the stress and marital resource models, we examine whether this relationship operates through economic and health-related factors.Research Design and MethodsUsing the Health and Retirement Study (2004–2020; N = 11,406), we constructed 8 mutually exclusive categories based on current marital status and previous transitions, including divorce, widowhood, and multiple disruptions. We fit discrete-time hazard models to assess cognitive impairment risk across these marital trajectories. The bootstrap resampling method was used to conduct a formal mediation test.ResultsRemarriage was associated with higher risks of cognitive impairment for women, but not for men, even after controlling for economic and health-related factors. Specifically, remarried women following widowhood or multiple disruptions had 39% and 32% higher risks of cognitive impairment, respectively, compared to continuously married women. The formal mediation test revealed that remarriage after multiple disruptions has adverse impacts on cognitive impairment, in part, through unhealthy behaviors and poor health status. However, when comparing remarried and unmarried groups, remarriage was associated with lower risks of cognitive impairment for men, whereas remarried women continued to show higher risks of cognitive impairment compared to their unmarried counterparts.Discussion and ImplicationsThis study is among the first to examine gender differences in how lifetime marital histories affect cognitive impairment risk. Remarriage, particularly following widowhood or multiple disruptions may put women at higher risks of cognitive impairment compared to those in their first marriage or those who remain single. These findings underscore the importance of considering detailed marital histories, beyond simple married/unmarried classification, when evaluating the role of social conditions in shaping cognitive impairment risk in practice settings.Keywords: Cognitive impairment, Gender differences, HRS, Marital transitions, Remarriages

BODY
Translational Significance: Cognitive impairment is a significant health concern for older adults. Although research has largely focused on current marital status associated with later-life cognition, this work moves beyond this simple classification and highlights the role of lifetime marital transitions as significant risk factors for cognitive decline. A novel translational finding was that remarriage after widowhood or multiple disruptions increases cognitive impairment risk over time, particularly for older women, but not for men. Identifying these gender-specific vulnerabilities in marital transitions may provide new opportunities to create targeted interventions for older adults at increased cognitive impairment risk.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment and dementia represent significant public health challenges in the United States. Individuals with cognitive impairment face substantially higher risks of developing dementia, which is associated with reduced life expectancy and increased needs for healthcare and personal assistance (Alzheimer’s Association, 2019). Although research on risk factors associated with cognitive impairment has traditionally focused on behavioral and biological factors, emerging evidence highlights marital status as a key social determinant of cognitive health. A growing body of research demonstrates that married individuals have a lower likelihood of developing cognitive impairment compared to their unmarried counterparts, including those who are divorced, widowed, or never married (Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019, 2020; Skirbekk et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021).

However, contemporary older Americans—those in their 60s, 70s, and 80s—exhibit remarkably different marriage trajectories compared to their earlier cohorts. Fewer individuals in this demographic are aging with their first spouses. The divorce rate after age 50 nearly doubled between 1990 and 2010, whereas rates of remarriage and serial partnerships have steadily increased over recent decades (Carr & Utz, 2020). As of 2016, only 40% of adults aged 60 or older remained married to their first spouses, whereas 5%–10% in this age group had married three or more times (U.S. Census Bureau, 2021).

Despite these demographic shifts, previous studies linking marriage to cognition have primarily focused on one’s current marital status without considering the complex marital histories of older adults in the United States. Existing studies have often grouped remarried individuals with the continuously married or treated remarriage merely as a control variable (Liu et al., 2020; Sundström et al., 2016; Håkansson et al., 2009). This limitation is particularly problematic because remarriage, while offering access to economic and health resources, may introduce unique challenges and lingering issues from previous disruptions that may compromise health (McFarland et al., 2013). Although a few recent studies have begun to explore the role of remarriage in later-life cognition (Brown et al., 2021; Hanes & Clouston, 2024; Zhang et al., 2021), little attention has been paid to how remarried individuals differ not only from continuously married but also from unmarried groups. Furthermore, no prior research has investigated the potential pathways—such as access to economic and health resources—that might explain the relationship between lifetime marital trajectories and cognitive impairment.

In this study, we address these limitations by incorporating the concept of marital biography—transitions in and out of marriage over the life course—to examine both current marital status and lifetime marital trajectories, with a particular focus on remarriage effects. Drawing on marital history data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS; 2004–2020), we construct eight mutually exclusive marital history categories through a two-step process. First, we categorize current marital status into four groups: continuously married, never married, currently remarried, and currently unmarried. We then further divide the latter two categories based on prior marital transitions—divorce, widowhood, or multiple disruptions. Through the theoretical frameworks of the stress and marital resource model, we investigate cognitive impairment risks across these diverse marital histories while examining potential economic and health-related pathways. Because the “gendered marriage effect” postulates differential health effects of marital status, with men typically benefiting more from marriage than women, we further examine how the relationship between marital history and cognitive impairment differs by gender.

Background

Theoretical Framework Linking Remarriage and Cognitive Impairment

The marital biography framework, grounded in life course theory, posits that an individual’s health outcomes are shaped by the cumulative nature of their marital history, including both the number and types of past and current unions (Hughes & Waite, 2009). Among other health outcomes, cognitive functioning is particularly well-suited to this framework due to the brain’s age-related patterns of growth and decline (Kuh, 2007). Recently, theoretical interest in marital biographies has expanded across different types of health outcomes (Dupre & Nelson, 2016; Hughes & Waite, 2009; McFarland et al., 2013; Zhang & Hayward, 2006), but empirical studies examining their influence on cognitive impairment remain largely limited.

Research on the stress model has identified marital disruption as a significant stressor that imposes emotional and physiological burdens, potentially leading to long-term health consequences including cognitive decline (Liu et al., 2020; Williams & Umberson, 2004). For instance, the emotional toll of losing a partner and the physiological stress responses triggered by dissolution experiences can all have negative impacts on cognitive health (Chandra et al., 2024). Although some studies have found that marital disruption is associated with poor cognition compared to continuous marriage (Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020), it remains unclear whether these negative effects of marital disruption persist even after remarriage.

Within the marital biography framework, remarriage holds particular interest for researchers as it represents both continuity and change in marital trajectories. Remarriage may act as a critical event that offers opportunities to recover from the stress of prior marital disruptions through the reacquisition of economic, social, and health resources (Brown et al., 2021), the very resources that advantage the married over the unmarried, according to the marital resource model. It thus has the potential to mitigate the adverse effects of marital disruption by restoring these resources lost during the dissolution process. For example, returning to a married state can help individuals to access pooled economic resources, health behavior monitoring, and increased healthcare utilization (McFarland et al., 2013). Moreover, the presence of a spouse may facilitate regular interactions, such as engaging in conversations or attending social events, while also broadening access to larger social networks through one’s spouse, which are known to be beneficial in maintaining cognitive functioning (Liu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021). These protective factors suggest that remarried individuals may fare better than their unmarried peers even though both groups experienced a marital disruption.

At the same time, remarriage may also introduce unique challenges or lingering effects of previous unions (Cherlin, 1978), which can complicate its potential protective effects. Specifically, the complexities of stepfamily dynamics, negotiation of roles within a new partnership, and financial entanglements place remarried individuals in a stressful context that distinguishes remarriage from first marriages (Sweeney, 2010). These challenges may be combined with the lingering stress from prior marital disruptions, which may ultimately contribute to poor cognitive outcomes, hindering remarried individuals from fully matching the cognitive outcomes of the continuously married, even with the regained resources. On the basis of these arguments, we formulated our first and second hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Individuals with the experience of marital disruption—remarried and currently unmarried individuals—will have higher risks of cognitive impairment than the continuously married.Hypothesis 2: Currently unmarried after a disruption will have higher risks of cognitive impairment than those remarried.

Hypothesis 1: Individuals with the experience of marital disruption—remarried and currently unmarried individuals—will have higher risks of cognitive impairment than the continuously married.

Hypothesis 2: Currently unmarried after a disruption will have higher risks of cognitive impairment than those remarried.

These opposing dynamics of remarriage—as both a potential relief and a source of new stress—raise critical questions about its role in cognitive health. Specifically, does remarriage primarily offset the effects of prior disruptions by restoring access to key resources, or does it intensify the stress associated with marital disruption due to its complexities? By addressing these questions, this study aims to shed light on the nuanced pathways through which marital histories, particularly remarriage, influence cognitive outcomes in later life.

Pathways Linking Remarriage to Cognitive Impairment

The vast majority of the literature explaining pathways linking marital status and cognitive health has focused on how health differences between the married and the unmarried are attributable to economic and health-related resources provided by marriage (Liu et al., 2020; Waite & Gallagher, 2000). However, whether and how economic and health-related resources explain the cognitive health differences between remarried and continuously married individuals remains unexplored. Economically, remarried individuals may regain access to pooled resources, such as shared income and assets, which can alleviate financial strain and provide stability (Waite & Gallagher, 2000). However, remarriage does not always equitably resolve financial difficulties. For example, previous financial obligations, such as alimony, child support, or debts from prior unions, can constrain the economic benefits of remarriage (Sweeney, 2010). Furthermore, financial disagreements within remarriages can increase household tension, reduce marital satisfaction, and contribute to chronic stress, all of which are linked to cognitive decline (Dew & Dakin, 2011; Sandberg & Harper, 2000).

Health-related factors may also serve as important pathways through which remarriage and marital histories contribute to cognitive impairment. Although remarriage can encourage healthier behaviors, such as increased physical activity or better diet adherence, evidence suggests that remarried individuals may continue to engage in health-risk behaviors such as smoking and heavy drinking (Nystedt, 2006). Moreover, remarriage can facilitate increased healthcare utilization, allowing for better management of chronic conditions. However, the negative effects of prior marital disruptions on overall well-being may persist even after remarriage (Hughes & Waite, 2009; Zhang & Hayward, 2006), potentially limiting the protective benefits of remarriage. On the basis of these arguments, we put forth our third hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: Differences in risks of cognitive impairment across marital histories will be explained by economic resources and health-related factors.

Gender Differences of Remarriage and Cognitive Impairment

Gender not only shapes marital histories (Carr & Bodnar-Deren, 2009), but it also likely influences the experiences within specific marital statuses, such as remarriage. These differences in remarriage experiences between men and women may lead to distinct pathways through which remarriage affects cognitive outcomes. For men, remarriage is more likely to function as a resource that reduces the negative impacts of marital disruption. Men are more likely to remarry and are shown to be more open about remarriage than women (Brown et al. 2019; Livingston, 2014). According to notions of “swapping families” (Furstenberg & Cherlin, 1991; Furstenberg et al., 1983), remarried men often redirect their time and energy into their new families while potentially withdrawing from the responsibilities to their nonresidential children. Through remarriage, men gain back the health-promoting benefits that come with marriage such as emotional support and health-related social control, which may help buffer the adverse effects of marital disruption and protect cognitive function.

In contrast, women’s experiences with remarriage tend to be more complex as remarriage may heighten the existing stress from marital disruption. Women generally benefit financially from remarriage (Lin & Brown, 2021), but remarried women may also face the burden of increased familial responsibilities. Women tend to take on the role of a “kinkeeper” in family relationships, maintaining family connections (Rosenthal, 1985). Women’s role as the kinkeeper continues even after a marital dissolution (Kalmijn et al., 2019), suggesting that remarried women likely have to balance their past and current family obligations. Remarriage may also be particularly stressful to older women if they have to provide care for their spouse. Research has shown that remarried women were more likely than their male counterparts to experience psychological distress from remarriage because men typically regain a source of support and care, whereas women often assume increased responsibilities for family bonds and household chores for other family members (Williams & Umberson, 2004). Similarly, another study showed that remarried women have a higher likelihood of developing cardiovascular disease compared to continuously married women, whereas remarried men exhibit a similar or even lower risk compared to continuously married men (Zhang & Hayward, 2006).

The literature on the impact of marriage on cognitive health is, however, primarily limited to studies that compare nonmarried to currently married people, and the findings on gender differences are mixed. Little attention has been paid to gender differences in the effects of remarriage on cognitive impairment. For instance, some studies have reported that the impact of marital dissolution on the risk of dementia is higher in men (Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020), whereas others have found limited or no gender effect on the association between marital status and cognitive function (Liu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021). Thus, it remains unclear whether remarriage places remarried women at a higher risk of developing cognitive impairment and whether it provides the same health and financial benefits as continuous marriage. On the basis of these arguments, we formulated our fourth hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4: The difference in the risk of cognitive impairment across marital histories will vary by gender due to gender role expectations within marital unions.

In addition to current marital status, past marital transitions may also play a key role in cognitive function. Previous studies have highlighted the different impacts of divorce and widowhood on cognitive function (Brown et al., 2021), and the detrimental impact of experiencing multiple marital disruptions on health (Hughes & Waite, 2009; McFarland et al., 2013). Building on this research, this study further categorizes past marital transitions into three groups: divorce, widowhood, and multiple disruptions.

Research Design and Methods

Data

Data are drawn from the 2004–2020 HRS, a nationally representative longitudinal survey of U.S. older adults aged 51 and older. As the longest-running aging survey of its kind, the HRS collects more than two decades of follow-up on cognitive functioning in later life, along with comprehensive marital history over one’s entire lifetime. The first core interview was conducted in 1992, with subsequent waves fielded every 2 years. Since 1998, the HRS has replenished the sample every 6 years with younger cohorts (Sonnega et al., 2014). We chose the 2004 survey wave as our baseline because it was the first wave to recruit baby boomers, who are believed to have different marital histories than earlier cohorts (Brown & Lin, 2012). In this study, we included both self- and proxy-interviewed respondents to avoid underestimating cognitive impairment cases, as proxy-interviewed respondents have been found to be at a higher risk of developing cognitive impairment (Crimmins et al., 2011).

We limited the analytic sample to respondents who were aged 51 and older in 2004 (n = 18,931). We then excluded respondents who did not have baseline sampling weight (n = 440) and those who were cognitively impaired or those who had missing data on cognition at baseline to assess the onset of cognitive impairment during the study period (n = 3,886). We also excluded respondents with missing data on marital history (n = 352), years of education (n = 19), smoking (n = 92), and other covariates (n = 17). We further excluded respondents whose spouse is not living with them (n = 672) and those who are cohabiting (n = 965) as the health benefits from a noncohabiting spouse or nonmarital partner may be different from the benefits from a coresident spouse. Additionally, we excluded respondents who only participated in the first wave to construct a lagged data structure (n = 1,101) and those in same-sex relationships (n = 1). This process yielded a final analytic sample of 11,406 (51,669 person-periods observations). A figure showing the process leading to our analytic sample is presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Measures

Cognitive impairment

Following previous work (Crimmins et al., 2011), we measured cognitive impairment differently for self-reports and proxy reports using the HRS modified version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS). A small percentage of respondents in each wave refused to participate in the cognitive tests, and to minimize sample attrition, the HRS has imputed cognitive measures for missing data. We utilized the HRS Cross-Wave Imputation of Cognitive Functioning Measures (V3) for self-interviewed respondents in our analysis. We first assessed global cognitive function based on the three cognitive tests available in the core: total word recall (0–20 points); counting backward from 20 (0–2 points); and serial sevens (0–5 points). For participants who can respond without help from others, the summary score ranged from 0 (severely impaired) to 27 (highly functioning). Consistent with previous work (Crimmins et al., 2011; Langa et al., 2023), we classified respondents with a response score of 11 or less as cognitively impaired. For participants who were unable to answer survey questions, proxy’s responses were used to measure their cognitive scores. Proxy cognitive scores were then calculated as the sum of proxy assessment of memory (0: excellent–4: poor), assessment of limitations in five instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs; 0: no limits–5: five limits), and the interviewer assessment of cognitive impairment (0: none, 1: some, 2: prevents completion). Cognitive scores from proxies of 3 or higher were classified as cognitively impaired.

Marital history

We created eight categories of marital history by taking into account both current marital status and past marital transitions. Given that we are unable to identify the sex of the spouse of the respondent prior to entering HRS, we constructed marital history exclusively from different-sex relationships. As summarized in Supplementary Table S1, these categories include (1) continuously married, (2) currently remarried after divorce, (3) currently remarried after widowhood, (4) currently remarried after two or more divorces or widowhood, (5) currently unmarried after divorce, (6) currently unmarried after widowhood, (7) currently unmarried after two or more divorces or widowhood, and (8) never married. We combined respondents who experienced two marital disruptions with those who experienced more than two because the sample size of the latter group was too small to allow for more detailed categorizations. We included the marital history variable as time-varying to capture lifetime marital transitions. In addition, recognizing that marital history also includes the duration of time spent in a particular marital status (Dupre & Nelson, 2016; Hughes & Waite, 2009), we incorporated the length of the longest marriage as a measure of marital duration. This measure is time-varying, reflecting the number of years spent in the longest marriage at each survey wave. Consistent with prior studies (Dupre & Nelson, 2016), we assigned a value of zero for the never-married group. This method is particularly effective in cases where incomplete data on the start or end dates of marriages complicate the calculation of total marital years. Additionally, it allows us to distinguish between long-term stable marriages and multiple shorter marriages.

Economic resources and health-related factors

Economic resources include income and wealth. Income was a household income variable with natural log. Wealth was measured as a sum of all wealth components except debts. Both income and wealth were adjusted by adding constants to remove zero income and negative wealth. Then, we took the natural log of the values.

Health-related factors include self-reported health, chronic conditions, smoking, drinking, and physical activity. These factors have been shown to link marital status with cognitive health, as the stress from marital disruptions can lead to unhealthy behaviors, the development of chronic diseases, and poor self-reported health (Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020). Self-reported health was measured on a 5-point scale with a higher number indicating better health. Chronic conditions were measured as the total number of chronic conditions, including high blood pressure, diabetes, stroke, lung disease, and heart disease (range: 0–5). Smoking was coded as 1 if the respondent was a smoker at the time of the survey. Drinking was measured by multiplying the number of days per week respondents consumed alcohol and the number of drinks they consumed on the days they drank to calculate average weekly alcohol intake. We followed National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism’s definition of heavy drinking—men consuming more than 4 drinks on a day or more than 14 drinks per week and women consuming more than 3 drinks on a day or more than 7 drinks per week (NIAAA, 2009)—and categorized the respondents into three groups: nondrinkers, light drinkers, and heavy drinkers. Moderate physical activity was a dichotomous variable indicating whether participants take part in moderately energetic activities more than once a month or not (1 = yes; 0 = no). All economic resources and health-related factors were time-varying.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics include age (in years), race/ethnicity (White [reference], Black, other), educational attainment (in years), and proxy (1 = proxy report). Age and proxy report were time-varying measures.

Analytic Strategy

Following Liu et al. (2020), we utilized logistic regression to estimate discrete-time hazard models to investigate the differential risks of cognitive impairment across marital histories. We created person-period files for the 2-year intervals from 2004 to 2020, and, to ensure that our predictors precede the event of cognitive impairment, we lagged marital histories, economic, and health-related factors by one wave (2 years). A respondent could potentially contribute from 1 to 8 person-periods to the analysis. Discrete-time hazard model used in this study included baseline hazard functions that represent the survival time for each individual. To account for the onset of cognitive impairment across waves, we introduced wave-specific dummy variables that denote survival at each respective wave. Standard errors were clustered by each respondent to account for correlations between repeated observations from the same person. The final model can be formally represented by the following equation:

In the discrete-time hazard model, h(t) is the probability of the onset of cognitive impairment at time t. ∑i=18αiDi indicates the baseline hazard functions of each wave from 2006 to 2020 (eight waves). β1marital history(t−1) is for the lagged marital history variable, whereas β2X(t−1) is for the lagged time-varying mediating variables. β3Z is for all other covariates. This final model is stratified by gender and estimated separately for male and female groups.

Our analyses were carried out in several steps. First, we estimated a descriptive analysis at baseline. Then, we fit a series of discrete-time logistic regressions, stratified by gender, to investigate the differentiated risk of cognitive impairment by marital history and the underlying mechanisms through which marital history contributes to cognitive impairment by gender. The first model included marital history and covariates. We then introduced economic resources in Model 2 and health-related factors in Model 3 to explore potential pathways linking marital history to cognitive impairment. Finally, we added both economic resources and health-related factors to our model and conducted mediation analysis using the bootstrap resampling method to examine their mediating effects. Unlike the traditional way of mediation analysis (Baron & Kenny, 1986), which struggles with competing direct and indirect effects, the bootstrapping method separately estimates each effect and offers more reliable results without assuming normality (Hayes, 2022). Additionally, pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine differences in risks of cognitive impairment between remarried and unmarried individuals. All analyses were conducted using STATA 18.0.

Table 1 presents baseline descriptive statistics, which stratify our analytic sample by gender and marital history. Important points illustrated in this table are, first, that a greater proportion of men in our analytic sample were remarried (25.5%) compared to women (16.4%). Second, for men, remarried individuals were comparable to their continuously married counterparts in terms of economic resources (e.g., income and wealth) and years of education. However, remarried men, except those who remarried after widowhood, fared worse in health and health behaviors than the continuously married. Third, although remarried women also tended to have worse health and health behaviors than the continuously married, their patterns were not entirely identical to those of men. Women with multiple marital disruptions exhibited the riskiest health behaviors, including the highest smoking rate and relatively high alcohol consumption. Divorced women also had elevated smoking and drinking rates but showed only a marginal difference in self-reported health compared to the continuously married. In contrast, widowed remarried women had better self-reported health but a higher burden of chronic conditions than continuously married women, highlighting a more complex relationship between marital history and health for women.

Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, to evaluate whether the gender differences in the association between remarriage and cognitive impairment risk observed in the gender-stratified models (Table 2) were statistically significant, we tested a two-way interaction between marital history and gender (Supplementary Table S3). The significant interaction term confirmed that the effect of remarriage on cognitive impairment risk differs by gender.

Second, given concerns in the marriage-health literature about reverse causality, which suggests that health status may influence marital transitions, we addressed this issue in our main analyses using lagged independent variables to establish temporal order. To further explore this, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses to examine whether individuals remarry after developing cognitive impairment (Supplementary Table S4). Comparing remarriage rates over a 14-year period (2006–2020) between individuals who developed cognitive impairment between 2004 and 2006 and those who remained cognitively unimpaired revealed no significant differences for either men or women, suggesting that cognitive impairment does not significantly affect the likelihood of remarriage.

Third, we included cohabiting respondents in the models to test the robustness of our results (Supplementary Table S5). This analysis confirmed that the inclusion of cohabiting respondents did not substantially alter the findings. However, they were excluded from the final analytical sample because the HRS only began collecting cohabitation data for individuals aged 50 or older.

Finally, we compared cognitive impairment risks between individuals with one marital disruption (divorce or widowhood) and those with multiple disruptions based on the fully adjusted models. The results from pairwise comparison indicated no significant differences in risk for men or women, regardless of remarried or unmarried status (results available upon request). This suggests that multiple marital disruptions do not independently impose greater cognitive impairment risk compared to a single disruption.

Discussion

Marital status is widely recognized as a significant social risk factor that can influence various health outcomes, including cognitive function. However, few studies have utilized lifetime marital histories and their impact on cognitive impairment (e.g., Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019, 2020; Zhang et al., 2021). Most of the effects of marriage on cognitive impairment have used a binary variable to indicate simply whether older adults are currently married or not, and failed to investigate the effect of remarriage and multiple marital disruptions on cognitive impairment. Taking advantage of longitudinal data collected over one’s entire lifetime in the HRS (2004–2020), this is one of the first studies to shed light on the role of different types of marital histories on cognitive impairment, looking specifically at remarriage effects between men and women.

We found partial evidence supporting our hypotheses. First, older adults who are continuously married had a lower risk of cognitive impairment compared to remarried or unmarried individuals, with this association being particularly pronounced among women. For men, remarried individuals had a risk of cognitive impairment similar to that of continuously married men, whereas unmarried men exhibited a higher risk. Second, men and women showed opposite patterns when comparing remarried and unmarried groups. Remarried men with multiple disruptions had a lower risk of cognitive impairment than unmarried men, whereas remarried women with widowhood or multiple disruptions had a higher risk than unmarried women. This contradicts Hypothesis 2 but supports Hypothesis 4, emphasizing gender differences in how marital history affects cognitive impairment risk. Third, consistent with Hypothesis 3, economic and health-related factors explained the cognitive impairment risk for unmarried individuals but provided a limited explanation for remarried women’s increased risk.

Our results reveal notable gender differences in the effect of remarriage on cognitive impairment, which may stem from the distinct social, emotional, and economic dynamics that remarriage entails for men and women. Among men, remarriage appeared to mitigate the risks associated with multiple marital disruptions, as remarried men had a similar risk of cognitive impairment as continuously married men, and a lower risk compared to unmarried men with the same marital history. This suggests that remarriage may provide men with protective factors, such as emotional support and caregiving, that buffer against cognitive decline. In contrast, remarriage for women, particularly those with a history of widowhood or multiple disruptions, was associated with higher risks of cognitive impairment compared to both continuously married and unmarried women. This finding may reflect the cumulative stress of navigating multiple marital transitions, which could outweigh the potential benefits of remarriage. Drawing on the perspectives of “linked lives” (Elder & Johnson, 2018), remarried women may maintain “indisposable” ties with children, siblings, parents, and extended families (Hu & To, 2018). Remarried women may therefore adopt traditional roles again in new marital unions, continuing as the primary caregivers and kin connectors (Williams & Umberson, 2004). For older women, readopting these roles, particularly household responsibilities, can be a source of emotional and physical burden. On the other hand, remarried men may shift their focus to their new families, stepping back from previous familial relationships that continue after marital disruptions. This approach may lessen the cognitive load associated with managing complex family structures while allowing men to enjoy the health benefits of remarriage (Furstenberg & Cherlin, 1991; Furstenberg et al., 1983; Williams & Umberson, 2004).

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to investigate the mechanisms linking marital histories, particularly remarriage, to later-life cognitive function in a nationally representative sample. Using the bootstrap resampling method, we found that the effect of remaining unmarried after widowhood or multiple disruptions on cognitive impairment was fully explained by economic resources and health-related factors for both men and women. These findings suggest that the cognitive impairment risk for currently unmarried individuals can largely be attributed to the loss of economic and health-related resources provided through marital relationships. We also found that while the elevated risk for remarried women with multiple disruptions was partially explained by health-related factors, the increased risk for remarried women who experienced widowhood was not significantly mediated by either economic or health-related factors. These findings echo recent studies showing that economic and health-related factors do not fully account for differences in cognition across marital statuses (Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020), which highlights the importance of considering other factors beyond those examined in our study. Future research exploring additional pathways that may link remarriage to cognitive health, such as the quality of relationships with new partners, unresolved psychological stress from prior marital disruptions, or broader contextual factors including societal expectations, could provide deeper insights into the complex, gendered dynamics of remarriage and its impact on cognitive outcomes in later life. Furthermore, incorporating other unobserved factors, such as early-life experiences, personality traits, and preexisting health conditions that not only influence long-term health trajectories but also shape opportunities for marital transitions would provide more accurate insights into the pathways through which remarriage and marital histories influence cognitive health outcomes.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. First, to address the issues of reverse causality and selection, we utilized lagged independent variables to establish temporal order, focusing on a sample that included individuals who were cognitively normal at baseline and accounted for proxy reports. Sensitivity analyses also revealed no significant difference in remarriage likelihood between individuals who experienced cognitive impairment and those who did not (Supplementary Table S4). However, the possibility of reverse causality remains, and caution is still needed when interpreting the results. Second, our categorization of marital histories did not include individuals in cohabiting relationships. Because the HRS only began collecting information on cohabitation after respondents entered the survey at age 50 or older, any cohabitation history before this point is not available. Future research with a comprehensive record of partnership history could better determine how transitions in and out of cohabitation relate to cognitive aging in later life. Third, our analyses cannot account for marital quality, as information on marital quality was not collected until 2006. Future research with detailed information on marital quality could provide a deeper understanding of how marital trajectories and the quality of each relationship interact to shape cognitive functioning over the life course. Lastly, although our study primarily focused on gender differences in the association between marital history and cognitive impairment, future research should explore how this relationship varies across other sociodemographic factors, such as race/ethnicity and education. Marital losses over the life course may be particularly detrimental to the cognitive health of socially vulnerable older adults.

Implications

This study is among the first to distinguish between first marriages and remarriages using marital history data, with a particular focus on gender differences. Most existing evidence on the marriage-cognition link has collapsed remarried individuals with those in their first marriages, underestimating the unique challenges and experiences of remarriage. Contrary to prior studies finding no higher risk of cognitive impairment for remarried older adults compared to continuously married counterparts (Brown et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020), our results reveal that remarried women with a history of widowhood or multiple disruptions may face a greater risk of cognitive impairment compared to both continuously married and unmarried women. For men, however, remarried men showed a similar risk of cognitive impairment as continuously married men, and a lower risk compared to unmarried men with the same marital history. This pattern highlights the importance of understanding the gendered dynamics of remarriage when considering its implications for cognitive health. Furthermore, although our findings suggest a negative association between remarriage and women’s cognitive health, it is also important to consider that having a spouse—even through remarriage—may provide significant resources, such as emotional support and assistance with daily activities, which could help mitigate the challenges associated with cognitive decline. Future research should explore the balance between the benefits and costs of remarriage across cognitive health outcomes to better inform targeted interventions for older adults navigating different marital trajectories.
