ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrevious research on the impact of the Covid‐19 pandemic on romantic relationships has mainly concentrated on short‐term effects and average trends of change. This study aimed to explore different trajectories of relationship and sexual satisfaction from April 2020 to March 2022.MethodIncluding a cross‐national sample of 2859 individuals, a latent class approach was applied to identify subgroups of sexual and relationship satisfaction trajectories. Participants' satisfaction levels, attachment style, psychological symptoms, life satisfaction, and living arrangements were taken into account.ResultsThree latent classes were identified for both relationship and sexual satisfaction: (1) a group with high satisfaction levels but a slightly declining trajectory, (2) a group with low satisfaction levels but an ascending trajectory, and (3) a fluctuating group. Living apart, having children, psychological symptoms, low life satisfaction, and avoidant attachment characterized the two groups with low or fluctuating trajectories.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that there were different trajectories of relationship and sexual satisfaction during the pandemic, which can be predicted by individual dispositions. Even 2 years after the pandemic, its effects had not disappeared.Keywords: attachment style, cohabitation, Covid‐19, latent class analysis, relationship satisfaction, sexual satisfaction

BODY
INTRODUCTION

From the moment the World Health Organization first declared Covid‐19 a global pandemic on March 11, 2020 (World Health Organization, 2020), a large number of studies tried to investigate its consequences on individuals and demonstrated effects on well‐being, psychological symptoms (e.g., anxiety or stress), friendship networks, and the organization of everyday life (Bourmistrova et al., 2022; Dragano et al., 2022; Lippke et al., 2021; Talevi et al., 2020). Similarly, a wide range of evidence is available on how the pandemic has affected couples and families, such as their relationship quality, sexuality, couple daily adjustment, and partner violence (see Estlein et al., 2022, for a review).

However, these studies have yielded mixed results, indicating both positive and negative trends in the change of relationship and sexual satisfaction. Although it has been suggested that demographic, situational, and personal characteristics led to distinct trajectories of relationship satisfaction over the course of the pandemic, previous studies have paid little attention to identifying trajectory subtypes, focusing on overall trends instead. In the present study, we aim to narrow this gap by employing a latent class approach.

Another question that remains to be answered is how lasting the effects of the pandemic on close relationships are. Recent studies on the Covid‐19 pandemic have demonstrated habituation to social restrictions (Reitsema et al., 2022) and recovery from illness (Dahlen et al., 2022). Similarly, pandemic‐related stress and its impact on relationships may have been affected by factors such as milder lockdowns, increased availability of vaccines, and lower mortality rates in the second year of the pandemic (Hale et al., 2021; Worldometer, 2022). By spanning a period of 2 years from the time of the outbreak to the beginning of its decline, the current study will provide new information on a question that is not only of scientific but also societal relevance.

Among the stressors that have been found to negatively affect satisfaction in close relationships are uncertainty about the future, concerns about one's own health and that of loved ones, financial and work‐related worries, and quarantine‐related social isolation (e.g., Cindrich et al., 2021; Hertz‐Palmor et al., 2021; Ornell et al., 2020; Taylor, 2022). According to the stress‐spillover theory, stress can negatively affect couples, even when it originates outside the relationship. External stressors can lead to internal stress between partners through various mechanisms. For instance, they can result in less time spent together, reduced mutual understanding and communication, and an increase in problematic behaviors such as rigidity, anxiety, or hostility (e.g., Randall & Bodenmann, 2009).

According to the vulnerability‐stress‐adaptation model (Karney & Bradbury, 1995), the extent to which stress affects couples is further influenced by personal vulnerabilities like insecure attachment style, neuroticism, or psychological symptoms, which can amplify the effects of external stress. When examining different patterns of change in relationship and sexual satisfaction during the pandemic, incorporating these characteristics has the potential to offer a more precise description of the class and enhance the prediction of class membership.

The different vulnerabilities amplify the effect of stress in various ways. For example, individuals with high levels of neuroticism tend to perceive partner behaviors as more aggressive and conflicts as more relationship threatening, which might result in hostile communication patterns (Carver & Connor‐Smith, 2010). Similarly, those with anxious attachment styles tend to respond to stressful situations with problematic behaviors such as self‐reproach or emotional outbursts (Campbell et al., 2005), while individuals with avoidant attachment styles tend to withdraw during conflicts, potentially leading to emotional disconnection (Kobak & Sceery, 1988). Finally, difficult conditions such as psychological symptoms may lead to ineffective intrarelational problem‐solving and underestimation of the support offered by partners (Braithwaite & Holt‐Lunstad, 2017). As a result, these characteristics are known to lower relationship satisfaction and stability and increase relationship‐endangering tendencies (e.g., Hazan & Shaver, 1987; Karney & Bradbury, 1995; O'Meara & South, 2019).

The relevance of the stress‐spillover theory and the vulnerability‐stress‐adaptation model in the context of the Covid‐19 pandemic has been demonstrated both theoretically and empirically. Pietromonaco and Overall (2020) and Karantzas et al. (2021) have outlined how different Covid‐related stressors may affect coping in close relationships, conceptualizing Covid‐related stressors as loss‐related (e.g., fear of losing loved ones, loss of livelihood, stable housing, connection to the partner). Furthermore, empirical evidence suggests that the negative impact of pandemic stress on romantic relationships is moderated by attachment insecurity (Overall et al., 2022; Vigl et al., 2022), psychological distress (Abira et al., 2020; Dion et al., 2023), anxiety (Panzeri et al., 2020), dyadic coping (Randall et al., 2022), and living circumstances, such as noncohabitating or living with children (Vigl et al., 2023).

One way the pandemic has generated external stress is through changes in daily routines and the balance between closeness and distance. For instance, cohabitating couples had to navigate spending extended periods of time together in a confined space, sometimes while working from home and caring for children (Feeney & Fitzgerald, 2021; Waismel‐Manor et al., 2021), while couples in long‐distance relationships faced mobility restrictions and travel bans that prevented them from meeting for months (Dewitte et al., 2020; Hale et al., 2021). Despite several digital communication options, prolonged separation has been shown to pose a risk to relationships (Haggerty et al., 2022), particularly regarding sexual satisfaction (Vigl et al., 2022, 2023). Because of this large impact of the pandemic on physical closeness and distance, it appears that the pandemic has affected the relationship and sexual satisfaction to different extent, despite the two factors being moderate to strongly correlated according to previous studies (Fallis et al., 2016).

In summary, these findings suggest that relationship and sexual satisfaction are primarily influenced by three primary factors: demographic‐ and relationship‐related factors, personal characteristics, and stress. During times of contact restrictions, demographic and relationship‐related factors, such as living arrangements and parenting, may exert a stronger influence. Characteristics, such as neuroticism, attachment style, and psychological symptoms, have been consistently linked to relationship satisfaction, while stress during the pandemic might add an extra layer of burden to everyday external and internal stress of couples.

Latent class analysis of relationship satisfaction

Another important aspect to consider is that not all couples seem to have been affected by the pandemic to the same extent, and studies have indeed found negative effects (e.g., more conflicts, loneliness, distance), no effects, and even positive effects (e.g., (re)connection, improved communication, teamwork) (Estlein et al., 2022; Günther‐Bel et al., 2020). One way to interpret these findings is that different types of couple populations may have been affected in different ways due to distinct personal dispositions and environmental pressures. For example, evidence suggests that living apart and socioeconomic risk exacerbated the negative effects of the pandemic on relationship satisfaction (e.g., Turliuc & Candel, 2021; Vigl et al., 2022, 2023), whereas the presence of children in the household seems to have mitigated these effects to some extent (e.g., Schmid et al., 2021; Vigl et al., 2023).

The possibility of distinct or even opposing trajectories characterizing different types of couples tends to be occluded by approaches that focus on general or average trends (Collins & Lanza, 2009). In contrast, person‐centered approaches such as latent class analyses can be useful in identifying subgroups, assigning sample proportions to these groups and determining the factors influencing group membership. As summarized in a comprehensive review by Proulx et al. (2017), several longitudinal studies already applied these methods to examine trajectories of relationship satisfaction, some of which are also based on the vulnerability‐stress‐adaptation model (e.g., Anderson & Emmers‐Sommer, 2006; Don & Mickelson, 2014). Contrary to earlier studies that mainly assume a U‐shaped curve of satisfaction (e.g., VanLaningham et al., 2001) or an average decrease in satisfaction (e.g., Umberson et al., 2005), these studies found that the largest proportion of couples experience high, stable or slightly decreasing levels of satisfaction over time, with only a smaller proportion reporting strong declines. A recently published study on trajectories of sexual satisfaction (Leonhardt et al., 2021) painted a similar picture.

Research on trajectories of relationship satisfaction has shown that negative changes or generally low satisfaction are particularly evident in those who, at the beginning of the longitudinal assessment, reported low satisfaction levels or were experiencing a significant life transition, such as the transition to parenthood (see Proulx et al., 2017; for a review). However, these studies have shown that even during stressful periods such as the transition to parenthood, people can exhibit different trajectories of change. A substantial proportion of these individuals show stable trajectories or minor decreases in their relationship satisfaction, with group membership influenced by factors such as partner support, attachment avoidance, or relational self‐expansion (Don & Mickelson, 2014; Leonhardt et al., 2022; Rosen et al., 2021). Although latent class approaches have been used to examine changes in psychological distress, loneliness, depressiveness, and anxiety during the Covid‐19 pandemic (Ahrens et al., 2021; Mayerl et al., 2022; Pierce et al., 2021), they have not yet been adopted in the study of relationship and sexual satisfaction during the Covid‐19 pandemic to the best of our knowledge.

The present study: Aim and research questions

In summary, evidence regarding changes in relationship and sexual satisfaction to date relates primarily to the first year of the pandemic and to average patterns of change, presenting a mixed picture of negative and positive aspects (Estlein et al., 2022). In the present longitudinal study, we consider a broader period, investigating changes in relationship and sexual satisfaction from the beginning of the pandemic in the spring of 2020 to its decline in the spring of 2022. Our focus is on exploring whether different trajectories in satisfaction exist among our sample, identifying participant proportions in each trajectory, and examining predictors of class memberships, including demographic, relationship‐related, and personality factors assessed at the baseline measurement.

Different trajectories are distinguished primarily by baseline levels and patterns of change, but predicting specific combinations of these factors in advance is challenging. While different baseline levels may characterize trajectory types in relationship satisfaction, there is little evidence to predict the direction of change based on initial satisfaction levels. In line with the literature, and in very general terms, we expected that groups exhibiting low and/or strongly declining satisfaction values would be characterized by vulnerabilities such as an anxious and avoidant attachment style, low life satisfaction, and a higher prevalence of psychological symptoms. We also hypothesized that noncohabitants and participants with children would be more prevalent in classes with low and/or declining relationship satisfaction. However, due to unknown trajectory types prior to latent group analysis, more specific hypotheses are challenging at this stage.

METHOD

Participants

Data were collected in the course of a longitudinal study with six measurement time points (Vigl et al., 2022, 2023). 1 In the present analysis, we only include participants who (1) participated in the longitudinal study by giving consent to be contacted for further measurement occasions and (2) did not report a separation from their partner during the course of the study. 2 A total of 2593 individuals (73.6% female, 0.5% non‐binary) with a mean age of 31.51 (SD = 11.46) years participated. Most individuals reported being in a heterosexual relationship (94%), while some participants indicated to be in a homosexual (5.3%) or other (0.7%) relationship, with an average relationship duration of 6.73 (SD = 8.20) years. Slightly more than half of the participants (58.9%) were cohabitating with their partners and 20.8% had children. In total, 63 different countries were represented, with most of the participants living in Italy (33.7%), the GSA region (Germany, Switzerland, Austria; 26.6%), United States (22.2%), and the United Kingdom (9%). Participants had a high educational level, with 57.5% having completed university, 24.7% currently studying, and 15.7% having completed high school. When comparing demographics, relationship, and sexual satisfaction levels of the sample with reference population values of their respective countries (i.e., demographic statistics and satisfaction levels according to the norms of the validation studies available for the scales used), the sample showed similar satisfaction scores at the first measurement point. However, it was characterized by a higher representation of female and noncohabiting individuals, as well as by a comparatively young age and a high level of education (Vigl et al., 2022).

Measures

The survey was provided in English, German, and Italian. When translations were not available, three fluent independent translators provided their translations of the items and jointly decided on the best versions. The questionnaires used in the three language versions had comparable reliabilities. In the following sections, the range of reliabilities across language versions and, for relationship satisfaction, across time points is given in parentheses.

Outcomes

Relationship and sexual satisfaction

We assessed relationship satisfaction with the Relationship Assessment Scale (Hendrick, 1988) and its German version (Hassebrauck, 1991), consisting of seven items (range α = 0.88–0.93). Sexual satisfaction was measured with an single item (“How sexually satisfied are you with your partner?”; Mark et al., 2014). Both measures were administered at each time point, including retrospective assessments before the pandemic at the initial measurement point due to the absence of real‐time data before the Covid‐19 outbreak.

Predictors of latent class memberships assessed at baseline

Insecure attachment

Attachment anxiety and avoidance were measured with a short form of the Experiences in Close Relationships questionnaire (Lafontaine et al., 2016) and the German and Italian translations of items (Brenk‐Franz et al., 2018; Busonera et al., 2014). The scale consists of eight items that are answered on a 5‐point scale (range α = 0.78–0.88 for attachment anxiety, range α = 0.65–0.75 for attachment avoidance).

Psychological symptoms

We administered a short form of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale and its German and Italian versions (Bottesi et al., 2015; Nilges & Essau, 2015; Yusoff, 2013). This scale consists of nine items answered on a 4‐point scale, of which we computed a mean score (range α = 0.82–0.85).

Negative emotionality

Three items of the extra‐short form of the Big Five Inventory and its German translations (Rammstedt et al., 2020; Soto & John, 2017), answered on a 5‐point scale, were administered to assess negative emotionality (range α = 0.71–0.75).

Pandemic‐related worries

We created a scale of six items that investigated worries about the spread of the virus, one's own health and the health of loved ones, work‐related and financial worries, and concerns about personal relationships (family relationships and friendships). Answers were given on a 10‐point scale (1 = not worried at all, 10 = very worried) and an average score of overall worries was calculated (range α = 0.71–0.74).

Demographic characteristics

We assessed gender, age (in years), level of education, relationship duration (in years), whether participants lived with their partner (0 = no, 1 = yes), and whether they had children (0 = no, 1 = yes). Furthermore, we assessed the size of the living area (1 = semi‐rural or rural, 2 = small‐to‐medium city, 3 = big city) and if the occupational situation of the participant changed because of the pandemic (−1 = I work less hours/lost my job, 0 = no changes, 1 = I work more hours/got a job).

Procedure

We conducted the study online using the platform LimeSurvey and recruited participants through the university mailing list, newspapers, flyers, and posts on social networks, the website SurveyCircle, personal contacts, and the crowdsourcing service Clickworker, in which participants were compensated with €12 (~12 USD) for participating in all time points. The study, open to all adults in romantic relationships, was published online on April 8, 2020. The first three surveys took place approximately 10 days apart, whereas the other measurement points were half a year or a year apart. In order to facilitate grouping participants into trajectories of satisfaction with more equally spaced measurement points, only four assessments are reported in this analysis: T1 in April 2020 (n = 2593), T2 in November 2020 (n = 1180), T3 in March 2021 (n = 911), and T4 in March 2022 (n = 811). The local Ethics Committee approved the study and subjects gave their informed consent to participate.

Missing data

Due to the long assessment period, the majority of participants had missing data for at least one measurement point (cf. Figure S1). The greatest attrition occurred from T1 (April 2020) to T2 (November 2020), with approximately 54% of participants missing data at T2, rising to 69% missing data at T4 (March 2022). Data are fully available for 18.4% of participants.

As in previous analyses of the data set (Vigl et al., 2023), missing information was estimated using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). Multiple imputation has repeatedly been found to outperform other methods for dealing with missing data, especially in large samples or repeated measurement designs (Hardt et al., 2012; Soullier et al., 2010). Compared to the common approach of list‐wise deletion, it provides less biased results for both missing not at random (MNAR) and missing at random (MAR) data and higher power for missing completely at random (MCAR) data (van Ginkel et al., 2020). Recent simulation studies have shown that well‐specified imputation models can yield unbiased results even when up to 90% of data are missing (Madley‐Dowd et al., 2019).

In order to improve the estimation of missing values, auxiliary variables were included in the imputation model. These variables are not part of the analysis model but either explain the mechanisms of missingness or are highly correlated with the analysis variables (scale of ±0.40 or greater; Hardt et al., 2012). Using the R‐package mice (van Buuren & Groothuis‐Oudshoorn, 2011), we created 10 imputations as recommended by Raghunathan et al. (2002) and pooled the results according to Rubin's rules (Rubin, 2004). As previously reported in Vigl et al. (2023), relationship and sexual satisfaction scores of participants reporting separation (n = 266) were set to the lowest value (i.e., 1) from the time of separation before imputation, to avoid imputing unrealistically high scores. 3

Data analysis

Longitudinal data

The current data are part of a larger longitudinal study of relationships and sexual satisfaction during the Covid‐19 pandemic. Due to the unpredictable nature of the outbreak of the pandemic, we faced the challenge of establishing a retrospective baseline. Although we recognize the limitations of retrospective assessments, we included this baseline in our analyses for several reasons. Our initial prospective measurement took place one month after the WHO officially declared the pandemic, and it is reasonable to assume that psychological changes had already occurred during this initial month. Indeed, our analysis comparing the first measurement point with the retrospective assessments showed an overall decline in satisfaction in the first month of the pandemic (Vigl et al., 2022). Negative changes in relationship satisfaction have also been documented in other prospective longitudinal studies, either as an average trajectory (e.g., Ahuja & Khurana, 2021; Overall et al., 2022; Pauly et al., 2022) or for sample subgroups (Schmid et al., 2021). Therefore, we believe that an analysis focusing only on prospective measurement occasions would provide an incomplete picture of the impact of the pandemic on relationships and sexual satisfaction. To provide a comprehensive view, we also conducted analyses using only prospective time points, and these results are detailed in the Supplementary Material and the section Analysis of Prospective Data.

Latent class analysis

To identify latent subgroups of trajectories of relationship and sexual satisfaction during 2 years of the pandemic, we performed latent class mixed models using the LCMM package in R (Proust‐Lima et al., 2022). This analytical strategy belongs to the group of parametric finite mixture models that allow for heterogeneity within subgroups and assumes that the population consists of a finite number of latent classes, with a specific trajectory over time (Nguefack et al., 2020). We used models with random intercept and slope per class to estimate linear, quadratic, and cubic trajectories for one to four clusters. 4 This approach provided flexibility for identifying curvilinear change patterns often found in previous studies of trajectories of relationship satisfaction (e.g., Anderson & Emmers‐Sommer, 2006; Don & Mickelson, 2014) and mental health trajectories during the pandemic (Ahrens et al., 2021), without forcing them.

To obtain start values for the other models, we used the one‐class solution. We then added the classes stepwise and compared the model fit based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), the entropy statistic as a measure of sufficient class separation, the sample size of classes, and the average of the maximum posterior probability of assignments (APPA). We selected models with lower BICs, an entropy >0.50, a group size >5% of the sample (~130 individuals), and an APPA value of >70% for all classes (Lennon et al., 2018). Because we are aware of the different challenges faced by cohabitants and noncohabitants (Vigl et al., 2022, 2023), we first modeled the trajectories for the two groups separately. Figure S2 shows that the trajectories of the two groups were similar, particularly for sexual satisfaction. Although four classes were extracted for noncohabitants and only two for cohabitants for relationship satisfaction, the differences were negligible. As a result, we ran the models for the entire sample and included living arrangement as a predictor in the multinomial logistic regressions.

Latent class predictors

After identifying latent classes and assigning participants to classes based on the greatest posterior probability of class membership, predictors of class membership were analyzed using multinomial logistic regressions. The two lower satisfaction classes (low ascending, low fluctuating) for both outcomes were compared to the higher satisfaction class (high descending) as a reference because it was the most common and most robust class in our sample. Predictors included demographic, relationship‐related, and personality‐related characteristics assessed at the first measurement point. To address uncertainty in trajectory assignment, we incorporated posterior probabilities of trajectory membership as weights in the regression model using the nnet package (Ripley et al., 2016). Our data and analysis scripts are available in the Open Science Framework. 5

Table 2 shows predictors of the three relationship satisfaction classes. Compared to the high descending class, the odds of belonging to the low ascending class were higher for individuals with children, avoidant attachment, and low life satisfaction. The probability of being assigned to the high fluctuating class was higher among noncohabitating individuals, those with children, with low life satisfaction, and higher psychological symptoms.

Few predictors with at least small effect sizes were found for sexual satisfaction classes (Table 3). Compared with the high descending class, the variables “noncohabitating,” “having children,” and “psychological symptoms” were moderately strong predictors of assignement to the high fluctuating class. Although effects were slightly below the threshold for small effect sizes, “noncohabitating” and life dissatisfaction appeared to increase the likelihood of belonging to both the low ascending and high fluctuating classes.

Analysis of prospective data

To compensate for the potential bias of retrospectively assessing satisfaction before the pandemic, we conducted the same analyses using only prospective measurement points from April 2020 to March 2022. Figure S3 and Tables S4–S7 for these analyses can be found in the Supplemental Material in the section Analysis of Prospective Measurement Time Points. In this analysis, we observed two distinct classes for both relationship and sexual satisfaction. Specifically, we found a low fluctuating (17%) and a high descending (83%) class for relationship satisfaction and a low fluctuating (92%) and a medium ascending (8%) class for sexual satisfaction. These results are similar to our previously reported findings but with smaller fluctuations due to the exclusion of the retrospectively reported change before April 2020. The predictors of the classes were largely consistent with our previous analyses: For relationship satisfaction, individuals who were noncohabiting, had children, avoidant attachment, psychological symptoms, and life dissatisfaction were more likely to be in the low fluctuating compared with the high descending class. In terms of sexual satisfaction, women and individuals with low life satisfaction were more likely to be in the low fluctuating compared to the medium ascending class.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, the current study is one of the first to have analyzed trajectories of relationship and sexual satisfaction from the outbreak of the Covid‐19 pandemic up to the beginning of its decline in the spring of 2022. To this end, we applied a latent class analysis to a cross‐national sample of over 2000 individuals, about half of whom cohabited with their partners and 20% of whom had children. In what follows, we discuss the different types of trajectories as well as predictors for each class.

Trajectories of relationship and sexual satisfaction

We identified three different trajectories for both relationship and sexual satisfaction in our sample, representing a high descending, high fluctuating, and low ascending class, respectively. Individuals in the high descending classes started with high satisfaction scores, but their satisfaction declined over the course of the study. Those in the high fluctuating class showed marked declines in satisfaction at the beginning of the pandemic (April 2020), from which they recovered slightly, though not completely. Individuals in the low ascending class, started with low satisfaction levels, which increased slightly for relationship satisfaction and more markedly for sexual satisfaction.

Although the three classes emerged regardless of whether relationship or sexual satisfaction was used as a classifying variable, there were some differences in the trajectories of both satisfaction measures. First, the class sizes were different for the two outcomes. Specifically, for relationship satisfaction, 83% of the sample showed a high descending trajectory, while only 8% showed a high fluctuating trajectory. For sexual satisfaction, almost a quarter of the sample (24%) had a high fluctuating trajectory, whereas 59% of the sample had a high descending trajectory. Second, the degree of variation in one class also differed between outcomes. As can be seen from Figure 1 and the coefficients in Table 1, the fluctuations in satisfaction in the high fluctuating class and the decline in satisfaction in the high descending class appear to be stronger for sexual satisfaction than for relationship satisfaction. The greater instability of sexual satisfaction compared with overall relationship satisfaction may be related to the fact that physical closeness was more directly affected by Covid‐related life changes than other relationship aspects. For example, cohabitating couples in an Italian study reported decreased sexual activity due to lack of privacy (spending most of the day together) triggered by national quarantine (Cito et al., 2021), whereas sexual activity of noncohabitating couples was restricted by quarantine and travel bans (Vigl et al., 2022).

Interestingly, in the high fluctuating classes of the two outcomes, both relationship and sexual satisfaction declined the most at the start of the pandemic and then stabilized until November 2020. One may question if this result is due to bias in our retrospective assessment of the prepandemic period, given that only two classes with less pronounced fluctuations were identified when considering just the prospective measurement occasions. Nevertheless, this finding is consistent with other reports from prospective studies indicating substantial declines in relationship and sexual satisfaction during the first weeks of the pandemic, emphasizing the importance of this early period for couples and families (e.g., Ahuja & Khurana, 2021; Overall et al., 2022). This initial decline seems to mirror the strictness of Covid‐related measures enforced during the first few months of the pandemic and their subsequent easing (Hale et al., 2021). 7 Although the initial decline in satisfaction recovered somewhat, it should be emphasized that it did not return to the prepandemic levels.

A less marked, but still statistically significant decline in relationship and especially in sexual satisfaction characterized individuals in the high descending group, which represented the majority of the sample. This trend began in the first weeks of the pandemic and persisted for up to 2 years, suggesting that prepandemic satisfaction levels have not fully recovered. This decline may be due to ongoing pandemic‐related stressors and the global crisis caused by Russia's invasion of Ukraine in March 2022 which may have generated stress and worry independently of the pandemic (Barchielli et al., 2022; Riad et al., 2022). In addition, the decline in relationship satisfaction among initially satisfied couples is consistent with the typical pattern observed at the beginning of relationships among young adults, a substantial portion of our sample, as reported in a recent meta‐analysis (Bühler & Orth, 2022). This finding is also consistent with previous group‐based trajectory modeling analyses, suggesting that improvements in relationship satisfaction are rare, and that stability or, more often, decline is the predominant pattern (Leonhardt et al., 2021; Proulx et al., 2017). Therefore, it is possible that, independent of the pandemic, a negative trend in satisfaction may have occurred among initially highly satisfied individuals. However, normative declines among satisfied couples tend to be rather weak, especially over relatively short periods of time, and are therefore unlikely to explain the strong declines in satisfaction observed in our study.

For a small group of participants, however, relationship and especially sexual satisfaction increased somewhat, although from a low level, especially between April and November 2020. This is notable because one might have expected a poor relationship to be particularly vulnerable to a major stressor and to deteriorate further as a result. Although unexpected, this finding is consistent with positive changes following the early stages of the pandemic reported in other studies, where it has been explained by an increase in opportunities for intimacy among cohabitating couples (Li et al., 2020) or by the incorporation of new sexual activities, such as sexting or sharing sexual fantasies, among noncohabitating couples (Lehmiller et al., 2021). Indeed, the forced closeness created by quarantines may have acted as a corrective to the lack of communication and intimacy that often characterizes poor couple relationships (Doss et al., 2004; Yoo et al., 2014).

Predictors of class memberships

Regarding the high fluctuating classes, which were particularly affected by the pandemic, predictors included noncohabitation, having children, and experiencing more psychological symptoms and lower life satisfaction. Challenges experienced by noncohabitating individuals include factors such as lack of opportunity for physical contact (Dewitte et al., 2020), difficulty in replacing physical aspects of the relationship, notably sexuality, with digital communication (Neustaedter & Greenberg, 2012), and uncertainties regarding the timeline for a return to normalcy. We might also speculate that the challenges at the beginning of the pandemic caused some noncohabiting partners to distance themselves from each other and lose some confidence in the security of their relationship (Dainton & Aylor, 2001), representing a point of psychological rupture from which recovery was not fully possible, even when restrictions were eased. For psychological symptoms, Karantzas et al. (2021) postulated that psychological symptoms may exacerbate the negative effects of the pandemic on couples by interfering with perspective taking and the perceptions of partner support.

Finally, individuals living with children were more likely to belong to both the low ascending and high fluctuating class, consistent with findings showing that couples with children tend to report slightly lower relationship satisfaction (Twenge et al., 2003). During the pandemic, relationship satisfaction of parents may also fluctuate more strongly due to challenges such as reduced availability of childcare. Predictors characterizing the low ascending classes were largely consistent with our expectations based on the vulnerability‐stress‐adaptation model (Karney & Bradbury, 1995), namely avoidant attachment and poor life satisfaction. These findings suggest the applicability of the vulnerability‐stress‐adaptation model during the pandemic period (Karantzas et al., 2021; Karney & Bradbury, 1995; Pietromonaco & Overall, 2020).

An advantage of the current analytical approach is that it allows to determine when predictors are class dependent and outcome dependent. In terms of classes, for example, noncohabiting individuals were more likely to experience fluctuating classes but did not show higher probabilities for classes with consistently low levels of relationship or sexual satisfaction. Regarding relationship satisfaction, we also found that avoidant attachment style was predictive of assignment to classes characterized by low relationship satisfaction levels but not predictive of assignment to classes characterized by fluctuating trajectories. In contrast, psychological symptoms emerged as predictors of assignment to classes with fluctuating trajectories rather than classes characterized by low relationship and sexual satisfaction levels. These results emphasize the benefits associated with subgroup analysis compared with focusing on average change and linear predictors of improvement or decline without considering the underlying patterns of progression.

In terms of outcomes, comparing the two dependent variables, quite similar trends in the predictors emerged, but fewer effects were observed for the sexual satisfaction classes than for the relationship satisfaction classes, and the effects generally appear to be somewhat weaker (with the exception of the effect of cohabitating). There are several possible explanations for this discrepancy: First, although we decided to label the three classes in the same way, it is important to note that their patterns of change differed somewhat. Second, our choice of predictors was primarily based on theories of relationship satisfaction. While our study suggests that these predictors do affect sexual satisfaction, although in an attenuated fashion compared to relationship satisfaction, we did not specifically examine factors that are particularly relevant to sexual satisfaction, such as communication about sexuality, sexual compatibility, or experiences of physical intimacy (e.g., Freihart et al., 2020). Finally, the differences could also be attributed to differences in the magnitude of change between sexual and relationship satisfaction. As we have seen, fluctuations in sexual satisfaction over time were more pronounced than in relationship satisfaction, probably because pandemic‐related restrictions have a more immediate impact on physical aspects of relationships. Since the effect of individual differences becomes less pronounced as normative change increases, this may provide an explanation for the comparatively smaller effect of dispositional predictors on sexual satisfaction compared to their effect on relationship satisfaction.

Strengths and limitations

The study's main strengths include its prospective longitudinal design capturing the onset of the pandemic, periods of pandemic‐related restrictions and the beginning of its decline, the large cross‐national sample, and the presence of an approximately equal number of cohabitating and noncohabitating individuals. In addition, by taking a latent class approach, we were able to identify population subgroups with distinct satisfaction trajectories, as well as group‐specific vulnerability and resilience factors, consistent with predictions from the vulnerability‐stress‐adaptation model (Karney & Bradbury, 1995).

Limitations include the high proportion of women in our sample (>70%), the relatively high level of education, and the inclusion of individuals instead of couples. In particular, the high attrition and dropout rate, especially at later measurement points, warrants some caution in the interpretation of the current results. Replication studies, for example, studying changes in relationship and sexual satisfaction during periods of internal or external stress, would be necessary for validation and further substantiation of the results.

Another limitation is the retrospective assessment of prepandemic satisfaction. This limitation is somewhat mitigated by previous findings about the tendency of retrospective ratings of relationship satisfaction to be either accurate or negatively biased, while our data showed a perceived decline in satisfaction (Karney & Frye, 2002; Zygar‐Hoffmann & Schönbrodt, 2020). Moreover, our results reflect changes also found in prospective studies (e.g., Ahuja & Khurana, 2021; Pauly et al., 2022), and therefore, in our opinion, should not be left out. In addition, our data contradict typical recall bias patterns, as the observed changes were specific to the high fluctuating group and did not affect both outcomes equally, as opposed to being uniform across the sample and outcomes. Recall bias is also unlikely in light of the rebound in satisfaction by November 2020 observed in the group with the greatest change. Nevertheless, retrospective reports are not a true substitute for actual measurements, and we cannot rule out the possibility that the results would have been different if a prospective baseline measurement had been taken prior to the outbreak of the pandemic.

Another limitation arises from the probabilistic nature of group assignments in latent profile analysis, which can lead to misclassification errors, especially in cases of small samples and low entropy (Bakk & Kuha, 2021). Fortunately, neither of these two conditions were present in our study. Still, despite using a bias adjustment method to address this concern, we acknowledge the possibility that correlations between class assignments and external predictors could have been underestimated (Bolck et al., 2004). Finally, although we excluded separated individuals from our analysis, we do not know whether all or most of the separated individuals provided information about their separation. Some individuals may have stopped participating without informing us of a separation, making it more difficult to assign them to an appropriate satisfaction trajectory.

CONCLUSION

Overall, our study shows that the short‐term impacts of the pandemic on relationship and sexual satisfaction still persisted 2 years after its onset. At the same time, we were able to identify subgroups that differed in their trajectories of relationship and sexual satisfaction during the pandemic and that were characterized by individual dispositions and vulnerabilities. Our findings also highlight that general relationship satisfaction and sexual satisfaction do not always follow parallel paths. Apart from adding to our understanding of the dynamics of relationship functioning in response to the considerable stresses and disruptions to daily life caused by the pandemic, we hope that this understanding can be helpful in informing responses and policies to future pandemics or disruptive events of a similar nature.
