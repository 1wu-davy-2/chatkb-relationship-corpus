ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman sexuality and mating behaviors have been linked to the subjective importance of olfaction and body odor in one’s life. This study aimed to investigate the relationships between sociosexual orientation, subjective importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior, and to determine whether there is gender and cultural consistency.Methods1,904 Chinese college students and additional 861 participants from more general population (410 Chinese and 451 Americans) completed three questionnaires measuring sociosexual orientation, subjective importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior, respectively.ResultsThe results of Study 1 indicated a significant association between sociosexual orientation, subjective importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior for both males and females. Besides, the subjective importance of olfaction mediated the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior. Results of Study 2 suggested that the relationships were consistent across cultures.ConclusionsThis study found positive correlations between sociosexual orientation, the subjective importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior. Subjective importance of olfaction played a mediating role in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior, and this relationship was consistent across genders and cultures. These findings provide valuable insights into the role of human mating strategies in olfaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03134-9.Keywords: Sociosexual orientation, Olfaction, Body odor, Cross-culture, Mediation

BODY
Introduction

Sociosexual orientation or sociosexuality refers to an individual’s inclination toward engaging in casual, uncommitted sexual relationships [1–3]. A more “restricted” sociosexual orientation is associated with a long-term mating strategy and a tendency towards being more cautious before sexual activity, having fewer sex partners and prolonged courtship [2, 4]. Conversely, a more “unrestricted” sociosexual orientation is associated with a short-term mating strategy, characterized by earlier initiation of sexual activity, more frequent sexual engagement, and a greater number of sex partners [3, 5, 6]. Individuals with this orientation tend to report higher levels of dyadic sexual desire and stronger motivations for uncommitted sexual behavior [7–10].

Olfaction, especially the sniffing of body odor, plays an essential role in human sexual life and mating behavior [11–16]. When considering sexual activity, females identified olfactory information as the most important factor in mate selection compared to other sensory modalities [17, 18]. Interestingly, beyond the use of artificial fragrances, it was the liking of a partner’s natural body odor that emerged as the most influential olfactory factor in eliciting sexual interest—for both men and women [18]. Researchers have also demonstrated that men’s increased sexual interest and perceived pleasantness were associated with exposure to preovulatory female scents from the axilla and vulva [19, 20]. Together with heightened sexual interest and arousal, elevated testosterone levels were observed [19, 21], a pattern similarly found following exposure to sexually arousing films or interactions with attractive women [21–23]. Furthermore, women who had experienced sexual intercourse with at least one male partner rated the odor of androsterone as more pleasant than those who had never had sex [24]. However, women’s preferences for men’s body odor may be influenced by hormonal fluctuations across the ovulatory cycle, potentially resulting in a more complex relationship between body odor and sexual behavior [21, 25–27]. Specifically, women in the fertile phase of their menstrual cycle tend to show an increased preference for male body odor [26].

It appears that heightened interest and motivation in sexual behavior may be closely associated with the perception of the body odor of the opposite sex. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether individuals’ sociosexual orientation influences their preferences and their behaviors related to body odor sniffing. As mentioned above, individuals with a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation tend to show greater interest in sexual activity, particularly in uncommitted relationships [7–10]. This raises the question: are they also more attentive to body odor and more willing to engage in body odor sniffing as a means of seeking potential sexual partners [16, 28, 29]? Research has shown that individuals with a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation tend to exhibit lower levels of disgust towards body odor [12, 30]. People with higher disgust sensitivity tend to avoid sexual promiscuity to reduce the risk of pathogen exposure, which stands in contrast to an unrestricted sociosexual orientation [12, 31, 32]. In another study, men’s sexual arousal and short-term mating motivation were positively correlated with the perception of women’s body odors [33]. These findings may indicate that those who engage more frequently in casual sexual behaviors may be more inclined to interact with body odor and potentially exhibit increased body odor sniffing behaviors.

Han and his colleagues found that the subjective importance of olfaction—that is, how important individuals perceive olfactory cues to be in daily life—was a robust predictor of odor sniffing behavior [34], including the sniffing of both self and others’ body odor. This could be due to the fact that for those who value olfaction highly, odor cues represent a crucial source of information, potentially leading to an increase in sniffing behavior. Based on this, it is possible that individuals with a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation pay greater attention to olfactory cues, leading to more frequent body odor sniffing. However, heightened awareness of and importance placed on olfaction has also been associated with better olfactory function [35–39], which in turn may be linked to higher disgust sensitivity [12, 40]. This appears to contradict the possibility that individuals with a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation tend to exhibit lower levels of disgust sensitivity [12]. Nevertheless, the desire for sex, sexual function, and the amount of social interactions were positively correlated to individual’s olfactory function [12, 41–44]. A recent study also revealed the positive correlation between sexual desire and importance of olfaction [16]. Therefore, the influence of a preference for casual sexual behavior on the subjective significance of olfaction, as well as the role of olfaction’s importance in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior, remains unknown.

Previous studies have highlighted the influence of various factors, such as gender, religion, laws, and cultural norms, on sociosexual orientation [3, 45, 46]. Across 48 nations, men consistently exhibit more unrestricted sociosexuality than women [46]. According to sexual strategies theory and parental investment theory, men were evolutionarily more inclined toward short-term mating to maximize reproductive success, whereas women preferred long-term partners with resources for offspring care [47, 48]. In terms of cultural differences, China and several other East Asian countries tend to exhibit more restricted sociosexual orientations compared to Western nations [3, 46]. One possible explanation is that East Asians have consistently been found to be more sexually conservative than other ethnic groups [49, 50].

Additionally, studies have reported gender and cross-cultural differences in the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior [14, 51]. Specifically, compared to men, women tend to show greater interest in and attentiveness to olfactory cues [35, 52]. Women are also more likely to utilize chemical sensory information transmitted by body odor and are more sensitive to such cues in partner selection [17, 53]. In a cross-cultural study comparing participants from Mexico, South Korea, Germany, and the Czech Republic, Mexican participants rated olfaction significantly higher than those from the other countries—likely because Mexico (“contact culture”) places greater emphasis on close social interactions involving olfactory cues [51]. However, a more recent cross-cultural study involving participants from China, India, and the USA found that the positive relationships among sexual desire, the importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior were consistent across both gender and cultures [16]. Given these mixed findings regarding gender and cultural variations among human sexuality and olfaction, the present study aims to investigate whether the association between sociosexual orientation, importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior is consistent across genders and cultures.

In Study 1, we investigated the relationships between sociosexual orientation, subjective importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior among Chinese college students. We hypothesized that sociosexual orientation affects individuals’ body odor sniffing behavior through the mediating role of the subjective importance of olfaction among males and females. In Study 2, we aimed to determine whether the findings in Study 1 extend to both Chinese and US participants among general populations, not just college students. According to previous studies and also based on fundamental gender differences in the sociosexual orientation, all the relationships were investigated separately for males and females [3, 46, 48].

Study 1

Methods

Transparency and openness

Data were obtained from online surveys and have not been reported in any other publication. The data that support the findings of this study and produced this article are openly available in the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/r96fy/?view_only=192f8be5bb314c2da50d9fb5e9839980. Our study was not preregistered. We report all details for the sampling strategy and all exclusion criteria.

Participants

A total of 2,513 Chinese college students agreed to participate in the study by completing online surveys through the popular Chinese survey platform Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn). We designed the survey and created a link to distribute. Participants who were interested in the study clicked on the link and completed the survey via the website. To ensure data quality, we excluded incomplete questionnaires and embedded two attention check items (e.g., ‘Please select the second option’). Participants who failed any checks were also excluded (full items in Supplemental Materials). A final sample of 1904 students remained (469 males, 1435 females, aged 18–23 years [mean (M) ± standard deviation (SD) = 19.62 ± 1.06 years]) (More details were provided in Supplemental Materials). Before the beginning of the survey, all participants provided informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (No. 2022-004).

Measures

The Importance of Olfaction Questionnaire (IOQ)

The Importance of Olfaction Questionnaire [35] is a 20-item questionnaire with response options on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = I totally disagree, 3 = I totally agree). It consists of four subscales: (i) Association (the unconscious processes triggered by olfactory sensation, e.g., “When I smell delicious food, I am getting hungry”), (ii) Application (the intentional application of olfactory sensation, e.g., “Before drinking coffee/tea, I intentionally smell it”), (iii) Consequence (the tendency to draw conclusions from olfactory impression, e.g., When I do not like the smell of a shampoo, I do not buy it”), (iv) Aggravation (the propensity to overestimate the importance of olfactory loss, e.g., “Without my sense of smell, life would be worthless”). Scores range from 0 to 60, where higher values reflect a greater perceived importance of the sense of smell in one’s daily life. The IOQ has a good internal consistency (With Cronbach’s alpha = 0.86; McDonald’s omega = 0.855, see more details in Supplemental Materials).

Body Odor Sniffing Questionnaire (BOSQ)

The Body Odor Sniffing Questionnaire [14] measures an individual’s frequency of sniffing their own and others’ body odor. It is a 17-item questionnaire composed of three factors: (i) Self-private body odor (smelling one’s own private parts, e.g., “Do you ever sniff/smell your own underarm?”), (ii) Other’s body odor (smelling other’s body odor, e.g., “Do you ever sniff/smell your friends?”), (iii) Self-common body odor (smelling one’s own common body odor, e.g., “Do you ever sniff/smell your own hands?”). Participants were asked to rate their body odor sniffing behavior on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“Never”) to 3 (“Often”). The score ranges from 0 to 51, with a higher score indicating a higher frequency of body odor sniffing behavior. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha of the BOSQ was 0.92 (See more details of BOSQ in the Supplemental Materials).

Revised Sociosexual Orientation nventory (SOI-R)

The Revised Sociosexual Orientation Inventory [6, 54] is a 9-item questionnaire composed of three subscales (3 items per subscale): (i) Behavior (past behavior in terms of the number of casual and changing sex partners, e.g., “With how many different partners have you had sex within the past 12 months”), (ii) Attitude (attitudes toward uncommitted sex, e.g., “Sex without love is OK”), (iii) Desire (sexual desire for uncommitted sexual relationships, e.g., “How often do you have fantasies about having sex with someone you are not in a committed romantic relationship with?”). Responses were scored on a 9-point scale, with the overall SOI-R score ranging from 9 to 81, with higher scores indicating a greater preference for an unrestricted sociosexual orientation and a greater willingness for uncommitted sexual relationships. The SOI-R has a good internal consistency, and Cronbach’s alpha of the current sample was 0.74 (See more details of SOI-R in the Supplemental Materials).

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA), and PROCESS Macro for SPSS 3.4.1 (Andrew F. Hayes, Calgary, Canada) were utilized for data analyses. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with a significance level of α = 0.05. Parametric tests were applied to normally distributed data, and non-parametric tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Due to the highly skewed distribution of the SOI-R scores, a Mann-Whitney U-test was used to examine the sex differences in sociosexual orientation. T-tests were used to examine the sex differences in the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior as the data was normally distributed. In addition to the association between the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior, which was examined using Pearson’s correlation, the associations between sociosexual orientation and other variables were examined using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Mediation analyses were conducted to explore the role of the importance of olfaction in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior. The analysis of the mediation model was conducted using Model 4 in the PROCESS Macro, with 95% confidence intervals of the bias-corrected bootstrapped method based on 5000 samples. The effect size of the mediating effect was calculated as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect (i.e., indirect effect / total effect). To evaluate the inferential robustness and risks associated with our sample size, we conducted a retrospective analysis using the smallest sample size from the current study 1 (N = 469) [55]. The analysis indicated that our study’s sample size demonstrates robust inferential validity and minimal risk of statistical errors when detecting an effect size of 0.25 (Statistical power: 1; Type M error: 0.997; Type S error: 0).

The mediating effect of the importance of olfaction on sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior

Based on the correlation analyses, mediation analyses were conducted to further investigate the relationship between sociosexual orientation, the importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior in Chinese college students. We treated the total SOI-R score as the independent variable, the total BOSQ score as the dependent variable, and the total IOQ score as the mediating variable (see Fig. 3). Results showed that in both males and females, sociosexual orientation was positively associated with body odor sniffing behavior (males: β = 0.21, p <.001, 95% CI [0.13, 0.28]; females: β = 0.29, p <.001, 95% CI = [0.21, 0.36]) and the importance of olfaction (males: β = 0.15, p <.01, 95% CI [0.04, 0.18]; females: β = 0.14, p <.001, 95% CI [0.10, 0.22]). The importance of olfaction was positively associated with body odor sniffing behavior (males: β = 0.24, p <.001, 95% CI [0.19, 0.40]; females: β = 0.37, p <.001, 95% CI [0.40, 0.51]).

Mediation of sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior by the importance of olfaction. Note. Boot SE, bootstrapped standard error. *p <.05, **p <.01, ***p <.001

The indirect effect of the importance of olfaction on the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior was significant in both males and females (males: β = 0.04, boot SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.01, 0.06]; females: β = 0.05, boot SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.03, 0.07]). Study 2

A total of 410 Chinese participants (218 males, 192 females, aged 20–55 years [Mean ± SD = 27.29 ± 6.35 years]), and 451 American participants (213 males and 238 females, aged 20–55 years [Mean ± SD = 36.81 ± 8.80 years]) were recruited to study 2 (More details were provided in Supplemental Materials). Chinese residents with Chinese nationality were included in the Chinese sample, and residents of the United States with American nationality were included in the American sample. The Chinese sample was recruited through Wenjuanxing, the same platform used in Study 1. The American sample was collected via an online workers’ platform for survey research (Amazon Mechanical Turk). Before the beginning of the survey, all participants provided informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (No. 2022-004).

The same questionnaires were used in Study 2 as in Study 1, including the Importance of Olfaction Questionnaire (IOQ) [35], the Body Odor Sniffing Questionnaire (BOSQ) [14], and the Revised Sociosexual Orientation Inventory (SOI-R) [6, 54].

Parametric tests were applied to normally distributed data, and non-parametric tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Mann-Whitney U-test was used to examine the country differences in sociosexual orientation, as the distribution of the SOI-R scores were also highly skewed. T-tests were used to examine the country differences in the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior because of the normally distributed data. Partial correlation analyses were conducted separately in the Chinese and American samples to explore the relationships between variables after controlling for age. Furthermore, mediating analysis was conducted to explore the role of the importance of olfaction in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior for each country. The analysis of the mediation model was conducted using Model 4 in the PROCESS Macro, with 95% confidence intervals of the bias-corrected bootstrapped method based on 5000 samples. Same as study 1, we conducted a retrospective analysis using the smallest sample size from the current study 2 (N = 410) [55]. The analysis indicated that the sample size in our study provides robust inferential validity and a minimal risk of statistical errors when detecting an effect size of 0.25 (statistical power: 1; Type M error: 0.998; Type S error: 0).

Discussion

This study explored the relationships between sociosexual orientation, the importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior. The results indicated that, compared to males, females tend to have a more restricted sociosexual orientation, place higher importance on olfaction, and engage in a higher frequency of body odor sniffing behavior. Furthermore, when comparing Chinese and American participants, American participants displayed a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation, lower olfactory importance, and lower frequency of body odor sniffing behavior. Nevertheless, the relationships between sociosexual orientation, the importance of olfaction, body odor sniffing behavior, and the mediating role of the importance of olfaction persisted across genders and cultures.

The current study has found that males tend to have a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation than females among both Chinese and American participants. This finding is consistent with a cross-cultural study of 48 countries and regions [46] and the study conducted in mainland China [3]. Two theoretical frameworks from evolutionary psychology provide plausible explanations for gender differences in sociosexual behavior [46, 47, 56, 57]. First, the Parental Investment Theory posits that the substantial biological costs associated with female reproduction—such as pregnancy and lactation—drive women to adopt long-term mating strategies to secure resource investment and paternal care [56, 57]. In contrast, males, facing lower reproductive costs, exhibit greater openness to short-term mating opportunities and less restricted sociosexual attitudes. ​Second, the Sexual Strategies Theory suggests that males, aiming to maximize reproductive success, adopt short-term mating strategies and exhibit more unrestricted sociosexual behaviors [47, 58]. Conversely, females are more inclined toward long-term mating strategies to secure sustained resource provision, resulting in a more restricted sociosexual orientation.

Among the general sample of Chinese and American populations, it was found that, compared to Chinese participants, American participants had a greater tendency towards an unrestricted sociosexual orientation. Again, this finding is consistent with previous studies employing the sociosexual orientation in Chinese samples as the results reveal that, compared to Western countries, the sociosexual orientation of Chinese people is relatively low [3, 46], indicating Chinese people tend to have a more restricted sociosexual orientation. Compared to Western societies, Asian cultures generally hold stricter gender-role expectations for women, emphasizing female chastity and modesty [49, 59]. This socialization process contributes to particularly conservative sexual behaviors among Asian women, and these strict norms indirectly affect the overall level of sexual openness within Asian populations [59]. Additionally, relative to Western societies, Asian cultures impose stronger social stigma and moral judgments on casual sexual behaviors, such as premarital sex or having multiple sexual partners, which discourages individuals from engaging in openly promiscuous behaviors to avoid social exclusion or negative evaluations [59, 60]. Furthermore, some researchers propose that differences in sexual openness may be related to pathogen prevalence. In regions with higher disease prevalence, people tend to exhibit more restricted sociosexual orientations, reducing exploration and interactions with novel environments and thereby mitigating the risk of pathogen exposure [61, 62]. Although contemporary China does not have a high prevalence of infectious diseases, prior to the mid-20th century, China experienced high rates of infectious diseases such as malaria, schistosomiasis, and tuberculosis, which might have historically influenced people’s level of sexual openness [63, 64].

In addition, sex and cultural differences were found in the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior. Our findings were consistent with previous studies, which have shown that women placed a higher value on olfaction and odors compared to men, as well as being more influenced by and utilizing chemosensory information of body odor in mate selection and sexual behavior [14, 35, 53, 65]. General research findings indicate that women exhibit higher olfactory sensitivity compared to men, potentially leading to greater attention toward odor cues and thereby obtaining more information from them [14, 66]. Moreover, women demonstrate heightened sensitivity to socially and emotionally relevant odors (e.g., stress-related odors, infant body odors), as evidenced by stronger neural responses (such as larger amplitudes in chemosensory event-related potentials, CSERP) and faster processing speeds [67]. This enhanced sensitivity may have evolved due to the greater reproductive investment required of women, necessitating increased reliance on olfactory cues for identifying high-quality mates [14, 68].

Our results also showed that American participants displayed lower olfactory importance than Chinese participants. Similarly, recent cross-cultural research revealed that Indian participants attributed more significance to olfaction than American participants [16]. Since both China and India are Eastern cultures, future studies could further explore the consistency of the importance of olfaction across a broader range of Eastern countries. Additionally, in our study, the American participants displayed a lower frequency of body odor sniffing behavior than the Chinese participants. This finding differed from a previous study, which indicated that the US sample reported a higher prevalence of sniffing body odor than the Chinese sample [14]. It is important to note that our study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, when people might have placed greater importance on their sense of smell, which could have influenced their body odor sniffing behavior. Therefore, due to the inconsistency of the findings, it is worth exploring the cross-country differences in the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior across a broader range of cultures in future studies.

Despite the findings regarding sex and cultural differences in sociosexual orientation, the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior, the positive relationships between sociosexual orientation and the importance of olfaction and body odor sniffing behavior were consistent. Previous research has shown that an individual’s subjective importance on olfaction is related to better olfactory function (e.g., olfactory sensitivity) [16, 35, 38, 51]. Olfactory function is closely related to sexual desire and the quantity and quality of sexual activity. Specifically, better olfactory function is associated with stronger sexual desire, greater enjoyment of sexual activity, and more frequent sexual activity [11, 44, 69]. However, better olfactory function has been found to be associated with higher disgust sensitivity [12, 40]. These findings suggest that while individuals’ sociosexual orientation demonstrates a positive association with the perceived significance of olfactory cues, this relationship may not necessarily correspond to superior olfactory performance. Studies have shown that individuals who frequently engage in sexual activities with various partners may exhibit diminished olfactory function, which could be associated with a reduced sensitivity to body odor disgust [31, 32, 70]. Blomkvist also noted that a decline in olfactory function might lower alertness to pathogen cues, thereby potentially increasing sexual promiscuity [12]. Nevertheless, research has found that the intensity of odor perception is not directly linked to levels of body odor disgust [71]. It is recommended that future research should consider olfactory function and provide a clearer and more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between sociosexual orientation and the individual’s valuation of olfaction. In addition, body odor serves as a critical chemosensory signal that conveys multiple biologically and socially relevant cues, including genetic compatibility, reproductive fitness, health status, and psychosocial states [41, 53, 72–74]. It plays an essential role in sexual activity by helping to trigger sexual desire and signal attractiveness to potential mates [75]. Previous research has found that individuals with higher sexual motivation tend to be more sensitive to body odor perception [43, 44]. In the current study, we suggest that individuals with a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation may be more attentive to body odor and more willing to engage in body odor sniffing due to their desire to seek potential sexual partners. Therefore, a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation may indicate more frequent engagement in body odor sniffing behaviors.

The mediation analyses indicated that the importance of olfaction serves as an intervening factor in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior. Thus, individuals with a more unrestricted sociosexual orientation tend to place a higher value on their sense of smell, increasing their frequency of sniffing body odors. One possible explanation for this finding is that the importance of olfaction represents a type of “olfactory cognition”, while body odor sniffing behavior represents a type of “olfactory behavior”. Therefore, an individual’s sociosexual orientation can influence their olfactory behavior by affecting their olfactory cognition. Furthermore, the mediating effect of the importance of olfaction is consistent across sex and cultures, indicating that the mediating effect of the importance of olfaction between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior is a consistent phenomenon that transcends gender and cultural boundaries. Nevertheless, the extent to which sociosexual orientation influences the perceived importance of olfaction—and how this perception affects body odor sniffing behavior—may vary across gender and cultural contexts. In Study 2, the association between sociosexual orientation and the importance of olfaction was stronger among Chinese women than Chinese men, suggesting a closer link between olfaction and sexuality in women. This finding indicates that sociosexual orientation may exert a greater influence on “olfactory cognition” among Chinese women. However, such gender differences were not observed in the American sample. Instead, among American participants, the perceived importance of olfaction was more strongly associated with body odor sniffing behavior in men than in women, implying that olfactory behavior in American men may be more strongly influenced by their “olfactory cognition”. It should be noted that these findings are based solely on self-report measures, limiting the interpretation of behavioral outcomes. Future research should further explore the neural mechanisms underlying sociosexual orientation–related changes in olfactory cognition across genders and cultures [76].

The current study provides a new perspective for interdisciplinary research on olfaction and relationships. The current study explored the relationship between olfaction and sexuality by examining the relationship between sociosexual orientation and olfaction and identifying the mediating role of the subjective importance of olfaction in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior. This study also explored whether there is cross-cultural consistency in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and olfaction, enriching the findings of cross-cultural research on olfaction and sexuality.

This study has several limitations. First, the relationships between sociosexual orientation, the importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior were assessed solely through self-report questionnaires. Future research is encouraged to incorporate psychophysiological measures of olfaction (e.g. Sniffin’ Sticks Test [77]) to enhance the robustness of the findings. Moreover, future research should consider additional individual-level factors when exploring the interaction between olfaction and sociosexuality. Prior studies have shown that olfactory impairments may be present in individuals with mental illnesses such as schizophrenia [78, 79], depression [80, 81] and bipolar disorder [82, 83]. However, it remains unclear whether such impairments influence the relationship between olfaction and sociosexual orientation. Other factors—including the interaction between gender and age [42, 84, 85], sexual orientation [86], and the menstrual cycle phase in women [87]—may also affect relationships between olfaction and sexual life and should be taken into account in future studies. To address these complexities, future research should adopt more nuanced designs incorporating hormonal assessments, validated clinical screenings, and objective olfactory measures. This would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how biological, psychological, and social factors interact to shape the relationship between olfaction and human mating strategies. Second, although the results of our study showed significant correlations in the analysis, the relatively small magnitude of the correlation coefficients indicates the need for replication in future research to ensure the reproducibility of these findings. Third, the current study was based on a cross-sectional design, which restricts the ability to draw definitive causal inferences from the mediation analyses. While the mediation model provides a useful framework for exploring potential mechanisms underlying the observed relationships, longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to confirm the directionality and causality of these effects. Nonetheless, given the exploratory nature of the research question, the use of mediation analysis offers valuable preliminary insights and serves as a foundation for future research in this area. Fourth, we recruited the American sample via Amazon’s M-Turk platform, which includes global members. To ensure the sample consisted of American nationals, we limited participants by nationality and residence. Future studies may benefit from using a platform more specifically focused on American participants.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found positive correlations between sociosexual orientation, the subjective importance of olfaction, and body odor sniffing behavior. Subjective importance of olfaction played a mediating role in the relationship between sociosexual orientation and body odor sniffing behavior, and this relationship was consistent across genders and cultures.
