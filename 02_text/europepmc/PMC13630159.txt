ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBacterial sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in the USA rose substantially in the 2010s and incidence remains high, despite reported declines after the COVID-19 pandemic. We aimed to better understand potential changes that may be contributing to these epidemic trends, particularly new behavioural and demographic patterns in the online dating era since ~2010.MethodsWe used joinpoint models to examine trends in reported incidence for three notifiable bacterial STIs (ie, chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis) in the USA during 2010–2024, and analysed concurrent changes in sexual behaviour, online dating and sexual orientation demographics using three national datasets (ie, the National Survey of Family Growth, General Social Survey and How Couples Meet and Stay Together).ResultsReported incidence rates of the three STIs increased during 2010–2019 and declined after circa 2020. Common STI behaviour indicators (eg, partner change rate) did not show changes consistent with the STI trends. In contrast, online dating partnership data suggested higher shares of partnerships formed within the same age group among online daters than offline daters. Analyses of sexual orientation demographics revealed marked increases in the population share self-reported as bisexual, particularly among young adults and females, and identified two candidate groups for further investigation (same-sex males and bisexual females) with group-level partner change metrics correlated with STI incidence rates.ConclusionsNew partnership patterns among online daters and changing demographics of sexual orientation may have influenced STI trends in the USA during 2010–2024. Research is needed to quantify their impacts on STI epidemics.Keywords: Public Health, Sexual Health, Sexually Transmitted Diseases

BODY
WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Reported incidence rates of three common bacterial sexually transmitted infections (STIs), namely, chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, increased substantially in the USA in the 2010s.

Sexual behaviours may have changed considerably since ~2010 due to widespread use of online dating and demographic shifts in gender identity and sexual orientation, which could in turn affect multiple STIs.

The impact of demographic shifts and partnership patterns in the online dating era remains understudied in many settings including the USA.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We identified consistent increases in all three STIs during 2010–2019 and decreases after 2020.

We found potential differences between online and offline daters (eg, a marginally higher tendency of online daters to form intimate partnerships with those of the same age), and identified marked increases in the population share self-identified as bisexual, particularly among young adults and females.

Exploratory analysis further identified two groups, namely, same-sex males and bisexual females, whose aggregate partner-change metrics were correlated with reported STI case counts.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our analyses suggest several new partnership patterns among online daters and changing demographics of sexual orientation that may have influenced STI trends during recent years.

Study findings represent a first step to generate new hypotheses and inform new lines of inquiry for future work on STI epidemiology and control.

Introduction

Chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis are the three most common notifiable bacterial sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in the USA and many other places around the world. These infections can cause short-term illnesses and long-term health complications, particularly in the reproductive systems (eg, infertility and birth defects)1–3 and central nervous systems (eg, meningitis).4–6 In the USA, reported incidence rates rose by 30% for chlamydia, 87% for gonorrhoea and 169% for primary and secondary syphilis from 2010 to 2019.7The rates however appeared to decline around 2020–2021, particularly for chlamydia and gonorrhoea.7,8 It remains unclear what has driven the rapid STI increases during the 2010s or the declines after the COVID-19 pandemic in the USA.

In addition to the characteristics of the aetiological agent (eg, different transmissibility, infection timeline, clinical presentation and disease severity), multiple factors including sexual behaviour, access to testing, treatment and prevention (eg, doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP))9–13 and antibiotic resistance (particularly for gonorrhoea) can all affect epidemic dynamics. The impact of these factors could vary over time and by aetiological agent (eg, differential effectiveness of doxy-PEP against the three bacterial STIs), leading to differences in the temporal trend across STIs. Nonetheless, given the shared transmission mechanisms, sexual behaviour is an essential factor affecting multiple STIs. Indeed, several key sexual behaviour indicators (eg, partner change rate, condomless sex and partner concurrency) have been shown to affect the risk of STI transmission.14–16 Analysing recent trends in these sexual behaviour indicators thus may help understand the recent STI incidence trends in the USA.

More recently, use of online dating has increased substantially following widespread use of smartphones and apps in the late 2000s/early 2010s.17–20 The US national survey data show that the share of partnerships initiated through online venues increased from ~0% before 2000 to 30–40% in 2018–2019.19,21,22 Such widespread use of online dating may have not only increased sexual contact overall but also facilitated casual (often overlapping/concurrent) contact and, in turn, increased STI spread. However, to date few studies have examined partnership patterns among online daters in comparison with offline daters and the potential impacts on STI trends.

Another potential key factor related to the recent STI trends is the sizeable demographic shift in gender identity and sexual orientation in recent years. For example, in a 2020 report, the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine estimated that the number of self-identified lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender (LGBT) people in the USA increased substantially to >11 millions during 2008–2017.23 In addition, a person’s sexual orientation could change with age and over time in response to situational, interpersonal and societal factors, a phenomenon known as sexual fluidity.24–27 Such sexual orientation demographic and life-course shifts are crucial in the context of STIs, as previous studies15,28,29 have reported a larger number of partners in people (particularly males) who engage in same-sex/bisexual interactions, leading to higher STI rates in these populations. Nonetheless, the demographic shifts in sexual orientation and the relevance to STI trends remain understudied in many settings including the USA.

Here, we first examine trends in reported incidence for the three notifiable bacterial STIs (ie, chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis) in the USA during 2010–2024. We then analyse concurrent changes in sexual behaviour, online dating and sexual orientation demographics using three US national social and behavioural surveys (online supplemental tables S1-S3) to better understand factors influencing the STI trends.

Materials and methods

STI incidence data and time trend analyses

Annual reported incidence rates of chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis (primary and secondary; same hereafter) in the USA during 2010–2024 were sourced from Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).8 To examine the incidence trends, we performed two analyses for each STI: (1) trends during the entire study period (2010–2024); and (2) trends before the COVID-19 pandemic (2010–2019). Specifically, we fit joinpoint models,30 that is, segmented linear regression models fitting a series of line segments connecting consecutive rate change points (ie, ‘joinpoints’).30 Here, we used the year of diagnosis as the independent variable and fit reported incidence rate using joined log-linear segments to identify rate change points and estimated the annual percent change (APC) for each time segment, using the joinpoint software (V.5.4.0; released April 2025).31 We used the software’s default settings: allowing a maximum of 1 joinpoint for 7–11 data points (for trends during 2010–2019) and a maximum of 2 joinpoints for 12–16 data points (for trends during 2010–2024); having at least 2 data points between consecutive joinpoints; determining the number of joinpoints using weighted Bayesian information criterion32; and computing the 95% CIs using the empirical quantile method (ie, 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of resampled estimates).32,33

Examining STI-related sexual behaviour indicators

We examined three STI-related sexual behaviour indicators (partner change rate, frequency of condomless vaginal sex and partner concurrency) using measurements from the National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG).34 The NSFG is conducted by the CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and gathers information on multiple sexual behaviour measures of the US population. Here, we included NSFG data from 2011–2013, 2013–2015, 2015–2017, 2017–2019 and 2022–2023. Importantly, the NSFG used audio computer-assisted self-interviewing (ACASI) to administer the sexual behaviour questions during our study period (2011–2023), which have been shown to improve reporting of private and sensitive outcomes. The NSFG surveyed individuals aged 15–44 years through the 2013–2015 survey period and those aged 15–49 years from 2015 to 2017 onward. Given the behavioural changes by age, we restricted the samples for all survey periods to those aged 15–44 years to ensure comparability over time (n=48 314 surveyees; online supplemental table S1).

Using the NSFG data, we computed the partner change rate (ie, number of partners in the past year) for each survey cycle, by sex (male and female), age group (15–24, 25–34 and 35–44 years old), type of partnership (same-sex, opposite-sex, all types) and self-reported sexual orientation (heterosexual, same-sex and bisexual; recorded for n=46 821, weighted percentage: 96.8%; online supplemental table S1). Previous studies have analysed the data by behaviourally defined sexuality group according to partner type in the past year (eg, see previous work15). However, ~20% of the surveyees (n=10 631, weighted percentage: 20.1%) did not report any partners in the past year and thus cannot be defined accordingly, even though this group (ie, sexually inactive) also affects STI trends. Thus, to include all surveyees, we did not analyse the data by behaviourally defined sexuality group. In addition, we harmonised the data for female surveyees across survey cycles, by capping the maximum number (Nmax) at 7 for opposite-sex partners and 3 for same-sex partners (vs NSFG set Nmax=20 during 2011–2013, 2013–2015 and 2015–2017 and 7 during 2017–2019 and 2022–2023 for opposite-sex partners, and Nmax=3 during the last two cycles and no capping otherwise); for male surveyees, NSFG used consistent caps for the five cycles (Nmax=20 for opposite-sex partners and 6 for same-sex partners). Overall, small percentages were at the cap (for females: none for same-sex partners and 0.03% for opposite-sex partners in the original NSFG data vs 0.5% and 0.77%, respectively, after the harmonisation; for males: 0.49% for same-sex partners and 0.26% for opposite-sex partners). As a sensitivity analysis, we also computed the corresponding statistics using the original data.

To examine the frequency of condomless vaginal sex, we computed the number of condomless sex acts in the last 4 weeks based on the difference in two variables (‘sexfreq’=times had sex, and ‘confreq’=times used a condom, both in the last 4 weeks). To examine partner concurrency, we computed the proportion of surveyees having more than 1 ‘current’ sex partner at the time of interview. Of note, for the 2022–2023 survey cycle, the NSFG used a revised set of marital and cohabiting status questions to be more inclusive of same-sex spouses and partners, which could affect the concurrency estimates. To examine the impact of this change, we also computed the partner concurrency stratified by marital status.

In addition, we used zero-inflated Poisson models35 to estimate the changes in the partner change rate over time (ie, including survey cycle as a categorical variable in the model), adjusting for key covariates and complex survey design (see details on analysis software below). Zero-inflated models allow mixing of two data distributions (here, 0 for sexually inactive persons and >0 for sexually active persons). We performed this analysis combining all surveyees (full model) and stratified by self-reported sexual orientation. The full model adjusted for sex, race/ethnicity, age group, self-reported sexual orientation, marital status and educational attainment and used the same covariates for the conditional-inflated and the zero-inflated part of the model (see categories for each variable in online supplemental table S4). The group-stratified models accounted for the same covariates except for sexual orientation. To examine the time trend, we computed the average marginal effect of the survey cycle variable (ie, averaging over all other variables).

Examining trends in online dating use

We examined trends in online dating use and sexual partnership patterns among online and offline daters using data from the How Couples Meet and Stay Together (HCMST) study.36 As detailed in previous work,19 HCMST is a nationally representative study of how Americans meet their spouses and romantic partners, using open-ended survey questionnaires. Here, we used data collected in 2017, 2020 and 2022, and restricted the age range to 15–44 years to be consistent with other analyses (n=3007 surveyees included; online supplemental table S2). The HCMST queried past relationships, which could be dated to decades before the time of survey. Here, we computed the proportion of couples met via internet venues based on the variable ‘q24_met_online’, which broadly included any internet-related venues (eg, social networking sites) and were thus not limited to dating sites.37 Given the relatively small sample size (n=3007), we aggregated the estimates by decade (1980–1989, 1990–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2019 and 2020–2022).

We also compared the percentage of partnerships formed within the same age group (here, defined as partner age difference <5 years) and race group (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, others), separately. We focused on age and race here, because STI rates often differ substantially by age (higher in youths in the USA)38 and race (eg, twofold to eightfold higher rates in Blacks than Whites in the USA).39 As sexual behaviour could differ by sexual orientation and change as people age, we performed the analysis for same-sex and opposite-sex couples separately, by age group (15–24, 25–34 and 35–44). Further, to focus on patterns in more recent years, we restricted the analysis to couples met in 2000 or after (n=1461 with the restriction). We computed the stratum-specific estimates, accounting for survey sample weights. In addition, we used generalised logistic regression models to estimate the odds of partnering with someone from the same age group or the same race group separately, comparing online versus offline daters, accounting for (1) age (continuous variable), (2) sex (male, female), race (White, other), partnership type (opposite-sex, same-sex), time period (2000–2011, 2012–2022) and interaction terms between online dating use and these four variables.

Examining demographics of sexual orientation and changes over time

We examined demographics of sexual orientation and changes over time, using data from two surveys: the NSFG34 and the General Social Survey (GSS).40,41 The GSS is a nationally representative survey of adults in the USA to study the trends in opinions, attitudes and behaviours.40,41 Questionnaires on sexual orientation were included in the NSFG from 2011 onward, and in the GSS from 2008 onward. Here, we computed the proportion of population self-reported as heterosexual, same-sex or bisexual separately, by sex and age group (15–24, 25–34 and 35–44) during each survey cycle of the NSFG from 2011 to 2023 (n=48 314; online supplemental table S1). For comparison, we computed the same estimates using GSS data from 2008, 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2021, 2022 and 2024 (n=7694; online supplemental table S3).

Further, using NSFG data, we computed a group-level partner change metric for each self-reported sexual orientation group (heterosexual, same-sex, bisexual). Specifically, we computed the number of partners in the past year (same-sex, opposite-sex, all types, separately) reported for each surveyee and then tallied these numbers by group, accounting for survey weights. That is, this group-level partner change total represents a composite measure of group-level STI transmission potential, combining the changes in per-capita partner change rate and sexual orientation demographics. To explore the combined impact, we computed the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the group-level partner change total and concurrent reported incidence of the three bacterial STIs (ie, using the mean during each survey cycle to match time periods).

Software used in the survey data analyses

We performed the survey data analyses in R (V.4.4.3; https://www.r-project.org) and adjusted for relevant survey design for all summary statistics and models. We used the ‘survey’ package42 to compute the summary statistics: mean, population total, SE and 95% CIs; specifically, we used (1) the svymean function for non-proportional measures (95% CIs computed as mean±1.96 SE); (2) svyciprop function for proportional measures (95% CIs computed with a logit transformation by setting method to ‘logit’); and (3) svytotal function for the group-level partner change total. For the partner change rate trend analysis, we used the svyglm.zip function from the ‘sjstats’ package43 to fit zero-inflated Poisson models (variance estimated using the standard sandwich method from the svyglm function of the ‘survey’ package), and used the avg_slopes function from the ‘marginaleffects’ package44 to compute average marginal effects (variance estimated using the Delta method; see R code in the Supplemental Material for reformatting svyglm.zip outputs). To model HCMST data, we used the svyglm function (‘survey’ package) to fit the generalised logistic regression models and computed Nagelkerke pseudo-R2 using the psrsq function (‘survey’ package).45 We examined variable multicollinearity based on the generalised variance inflation factor46 using the vif function from the ‘car’ package47 to ensure no substantial correlations among model covariates. We used the predict function to generate model fits for visual inspection.

Ethics statement

No human participants included. This study used public, deidentified data for analyses; thus, no Ethics Committee or Institutional Board approval is required.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in this study; all data were collected independent of this study.

Results

Trends in STI incidence rates in the USA, 2010–2024

Figure 1 shows a comparison of reported incidence rates of the three bacterial STIs in the USA during 2010–2024. The rates differed substantially by STI (eg, mean rates per 100 000 population were 10 for syphilis, 152 for gonorrhoea and 479 for chlamydia; figure 1A) and sex (eg, approximately twofold fold difference for chlamydia; figure 1B vs figure 1C). These differences were likely due to multiple factors (see the Discussion section). Estimated trends also differed across STIs and sexes (see APC estimates in figure 1). Nonetheless, the trend analysis identified consistent increases in reported incidence through circa 2020 (the overall reported incidence peaked in 2019 for chlamydia, 2021 for gonorrhoea and 2022 for syphilis) and declines afterwards, even though the rate change points differed slightly by STI and sex (figure 1). Estimated trends during 2010–2019 using 2010–2019 data alone (ie, restricting to pre-COVID data) were similar to the full analysis using data through 2024 (online supplemental figure S1). These similar temporal trends suggest common underlying risk factors (eg, changes in sexual behaviour) leading to the common trends, despite the STI-specific factors contributing to more nuanced differences by STI.

Changes in key STI-related behaviour indicators

We examined three key STI-related behaviour indicators as measured in the NSFG, namely, partner change rate, frequency of condomless sex and concurrent partnership, as increases in these indicators could lead to increased STI transmission. During 2011–2023, these indicators in general did not increase (figure 2; online supplemental table S1). Of note, concurrency among males appeared to be much higher in 2022–2023. Further analysis stratifying by marital status showed the higher concurrencies were largely among unmarried males (online supplemental figure S2), suggesting this finding was likely not due to changes in the 2022–2023 survey questionnaire to include same-sex spouses and partners; nonetheless, further investigation is warranted.

Key sexually transmitted infection–related sexual behaviour indicators in the USA during 2011–2023. Estimates are based on data from the National Survey of Family Growth. Dots and error bars show mean estimate and 95% CIs, adjusting for complex survey design. Note in (A), for females, we harmonised the partner change rate data using the same maximum number of same-sex and opposite-sex partners for all survey cycles (see the Methods section), and computed the estimates using the harmonised data (main analysis; solid lines) and the original data (sensitivity analysis; dashed lines), separately.

Combining all sexual orientation groups, modelling adjusting for key covariates estimated similar partner change rate during 2011–2017 and lower rates during 2017–2019 and 2022–2023 than 2011–2013 (on average, 0.09 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.16) and 0.3 (95% CI 0.24 to 0.36) lower respectively; online supplemental tables S4, S5 and figure S3). For self-reported heterosexual people, the group-stratified model estimated similar time trend as the full model combining all groups (online supplemental tables S4, S5). For self-reported same-sex people, the group-stratified model did not estimate significant changes during the five survey cycles (95% CIs spanned the null; online supplemental table S5). For self-reported bisexual people, the group-stratified model estimated similar partner change rate during 2011–2019 and substantially lower partner change rate during 2022–2023 (online supplemental table S5). Taken together, analysis of the three STI-related behaviour indicators did not identify a consistent trend that could explain the trends in reported STI incidence.

Changing intimate partnership landscape in the online dating era

We examined the trends in use of online dating and partnership patterns using data from the HCMST study. As shown in figure 3A, the share of couples met through online venues increased steadily over time, and more so for same-sex couples. Among 15–44 years old, the share of same-sex couples who met online reached 64% (95% CI 15% to 94%) in 2020–2022, largely surpassed that of opposite-sex couples (28%, 95% CI 15% to 47%). This is not unexpected, as online venues facilitate the search of partners, particularly for people who traditionally face ‘thin dating markets’ (eg, same-sex partner seekers)19,20,48; similar differential use of online dating has been observed using other data sources such as Pew Surveys.22

Online dating and changes in partnership patterns. Estimates are based on data from the How Couples Meet and Stay Together (HCMST) study (2017–2022) for those aged 15–44 years. Dots/bars show mean estimates, and error bars show 95% CIs, adjusting for survey sample weights. Note in the HCMST study, online/internet venues included any internet-related venues (eg, social networking sites).

Next, we examined differences by age and race between online and offline daters, given reported differential STI rates across age and race groups and impacts on STI epidemic dynamics.38,39 As shown in figure 3B, the percentage of partnership formed within the same age group and race group during 2000–2022 was higher among online daters than offline daters, particularly among the two youngest age groups (15–24 and 25–34 years old) who tend to report the highest STI rates.49 Such differences are evident in most population strata, except for racial difference among opposite-sex couples (figure 3B). The logistic regression models estimated marginally higher odds of forming partnership with someone from the same age group among online daters than offline daters (odds ratio (95% CI): 1.86 (0.91 to 3.78); online supplemental table S6), but were not informative for the odds of forming partnership with someone from the same race group due to inferior model goodness-of-fit (Nagelkerke pseudo-R2=0.16; online supplemental table S6 and figure S4). These estimates suggest there may be a higher tendency of online daters than offline daters to form intimate partnership with those sharing key demographic characteristics, which could have ramifications on STI epidemic dynamics50,51 (see the Discussion section).

Changing demographics of sexual orientation

Previous studies15,28,29 have repeatedly observed larger number of partners among people who engage in same-sex and/or bisexual partnership. As such, sexual orientation and changes in the population composition are essential to STI epidemiology. Here, we examined such changes using two U.S. national datasets, namely, the NSFG34 and the GSS.40 Both data sources indicate striking increases in recent years in the population share of bisexual people, particularly among young adults and females (figure 4 and online supplemental table S7). The largest increases occurred in 15–24 and 25–34 years old, the two youngest age groups who also tend to report the highest STI incidence.49 For example, according to the GSS, among 15–24 years old, the share of people who self-reported as bisexual increased from 1.5% in 2008 to 12.4% in 2024, and to 22.1% in females in 2024 (figure 4E, dashed lines). Similar increases were documented by the NSFG (figure 4E, solid lines).

Stratified by self-reported sexual orientation, per-capita partner change rate showed general decreases similar to the overall analysis (online supplemental figure S5 vs figure 2A). However, due to the large increases in population share self-reported as same-sex and bisexual, the group-level partner change total (ie, total partner change rate for each sexual orientation group) generally increased among self-reported same-sex and bisexual groups (figure 5; online supplemental table S8).

Estimates are based on data from the National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG). In exploratory ecological analyses, group-level partner-change metrics for same-sex males and bisexual females were correlated with reported STI measures, suggesting these groups as candidates for further investigation. First, consistent with previous work on men who have sex with men (MSM),28,52 the group-level partner change total for same-sex males closely tracked reported incidence of gonorrhoea (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r=0.92 (0.18, 0.99), p=0.03), syphilis (r=0.97 (0.62, 1), p=0.006) and all three STIs combined (r=0.85 (−0.14, 0.99), p=0.07; figure 5E). Second, for bisexual females, the group-level partner change total closely tracked reported incidence of chlamydia, gonorrhoea and all three STIs combined (figure 5C, r=0.91 (0.16, 0.99), 0.92 (0.22, 1) and 0.94 (0.32, 1), respectively; p<0.05 for all three).

Discussion

To better understand recent trends in STIs and influencing factors in the USA, we have analysed trends in reported incidence for chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis during 2010–2024 and concurrent measures of STI-related sexual behaviours using multiple US national datasets. Our time-trend analysis identified consistent increases in all three STIs during 2010–2019 and declines after circa 2020. Further, our analyses suggest new behavioural patterns among online daters and demographic shifts in sexual orientation that may affect STI trends in the USA during 2010–2024.

The estimated declines in reported STI incidence around 2020 coincided with the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. Previous studies have reported COVID-related disruptions, broadly in three categories: (1) reduced incidence reporting (ie, surveillance artefacts),53 (2) reduced STI control efforts (eg, reduced testing and treatment)54,55 and (3) reduced partnership formation and contact (eg, increased singleness).56 The first two types of disruption were interrelated and both were most severe during early months of the pandemic (roughly, March–December 2020).53–55 Here, our trend analysis used annual reported incidence and thus was unable to capture intra-annual changes. For the third type, here we examined key behavioural indicators such as partner change rate and partnership concurrency including during the COVID-19 pandemic. Consistent with the literature, our model estimated lower per-capita partner change rate during 2022–2023. However, the decline in partner change rate appeared to start from 2017 to 2019, particularly for those self-reported as heterosexual, and as such, may not be due solely to COVID-19-related disruptions. Further, as shown in our other analyses and discussed below, continuous social and demographic changes since the 2010s appeared to better track the STI trends over the 15-year study period including after the COVID pandemic.

Online dating has been cited as a potential contributor to the recent STI increases.17 Here, our analyses suggest there may be a higher tendency of online daters than their offline peers to form partnership with those of the same age. The tendency to interact with those with similar characteristics—namely, assortativity—is a key network characteristic. Assortativity leads to high-risk individuals forming dense, highly connected sexual networks, which could facilitate faster infection spread as shown in modelling studies including for STIs.50,51 Given the importance of assortativity and relevance of age and race/ethnicity,38,39 previous studies20,28,57 have examined age and race/ethnicity partnership assortativity. Using 2009 and 2017 HCMST data, Thomas20 found online daters are more likely to be interracial (ie, lower racial assortativity). Here, analysing partnership formations since 2000 by age group, we were unable to discern a difference in racial assortativity comparing online and offline daters. This inconsistency may be due to Thomas20 pooling all ages, as noted in an analysis by the Pew Research Centre.58 As in this study, Thomas20 found higher age assortativity among online daters. Thus, potential differences in partnership assortativity between online and offline daters, which could affect STI spread in the online dating era warrant further research to examine related impact on STI trends.

The 2010s also saw large increases in population self-identified as non-heterosexual in the USA,23 which could affect STI trends given the reported higher partner change rates and higher STI risks for certain groups (eg, MSM)15,28,29 and intergroup STI transmission. Using two national datasets (the NSFG and the GSS), we consistently identified marked increases in the population share self-reported as bisexual in the USA, particularly among young adults and females. In addition, analysis of group-level partner change metrics identified two candidate groups for further investigation, namely, same-sex males and bisexual females. Same-sex males and more generally MSM have been recognised as a core STI group in previous studies.49 Here, our finding highlights bisexual females as another potential bridge population connecting heterosexual, female same-sex and male same-sex networks, which could be key to STI transmission. Supportively, previous studies have reported higher rates of STIs among bisexual females.59–61 Nonetheless, the role of bisexual females in STI epidemiology remains understudied. Further investigation into bisexual networks, particularly those with bisexual females, may help reveal connections across different sexual networks (MSM, bisexual males, bisexual females and heterosexual networks) and identify links that may have been previously missed.

Importantly, demographic and behavioural factors do not act in isolation, and the processes are likely nonlinear. Likewise, the COVID-19 pandemic disruptions, although most severe around 2020–2021, may have long-lasting ramifications. Mathematical models (eg, compartmental epidemic models and agent-based models) have been used to capture the connections across risk groups and/or sexual networks as well as nonlinear STI transmission dynamics, while accounting for underdetection of infection. Future modelling could incorporate the demographics of sexual orientation, group-specific behaviours and changes over time—including potential COVID-19 disruptions—to test different hypotheses against STI incidence and prevalence data. Such efforts are essential to help tease out the impact of individual factors and in combination.

We recognise several study limitations. First, the large variations in reported incidence may be due to a number of factors in addition to innate differences in transmissibility by STI and sex. These include sex-specific differences in disease presentation/severity (eg, men are more likely to have symptomatic gonorrhoea infection than women)62, sexual behaviour differences (eg, MSM being a core group with higher rates of STIs)28,52 and screening practices that affect case ascertainment and reported incidence (eg, US Preventive Services Task Force recommend screening of chlamydia and gonorrhoea for sexually active females under age 25, but not males of the same age group except for those taking HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)).63 In addition, as noted above, disruptions to STI surveillance could artificially reduce reported incidence during the COVID-19 pandemic.53 Thus, we caution observational biases of reported incidence and comparing the estimates across STIs and between sexes.

Second, our analyses on behaviour and demographic trends are based on survey data, which are subject to measurement errors, recall biases, and other survey limitations. For example, the NSFG capped the maximum number of partners, which could lead to underestimation of the partner change rate. The surveys also did not distinguish long-term vs casual partnership and may miss casual partnership, which is important to STI epidemiology. Changes in survey methodology across cycles could also affect certain measurements; for instance, as noted in the Methods, the revised NSFG questionnaires on marital and cohabiting status in 2022–2023 could affect the concurrency estimates. In addition, survey sample size could be small, especially when stratified by subpopulation, which limits the precision of subgroup analyses as indicated by the wide CIs. Further, most sexual behavioural datasets included only younger ages. Here, for consistency, we restricted our analyses to those aged 15–44 years for whom data on key variables were collected for all study years. While 15–44 years old accounted for a large majority of reported incidence of the three bacterial STIs (eg, ~half of cases aged <25 years),49 recent studies have reported increased infections in older adults (eg, ≥45 years).64 Additional data collection and examination in those aged ≥45 years are thus warranted.

Third, sexual orientation, sexual attraction and actual sexual behaviour may not be consistent and these terms are not interchangeable.15 The inconsistency is evident in our analysis (figure 5, eg, individuals self-identified as same-sex may also engage in hetero-sex acts). To address this, previous studies (eg, see previous work15) have used partner type in the past year to define sexual behaviour group. However, as a substantial percentage of NSFG surveyees (~20% during 2011–2023) did not report any partners, such analysis is essentially restricted to the sexually active and does not reflect the overall changes including the sexually inactive. Further, it is not straightforward as to which measure is more relevant to STI transmission, as sexual orientation, attraction, and behaviour could all change over a person’s life course.23 Many current data collection instruments lack detailed measures of such nuances,23 making it difficult to study these changes and impacts. Thus, better data collection (both cross-sectional population surveys and longitudinal life-course studies) and further studies are warranted.

Lastly, our analyses were primarily descriptive, did not examine the complex interactions and nonlinear impacts, and estimated correlation between group-level partner change total and reported STI incidence is ecological with limited time points. Further, we focused on a limited number of variables to help surface demographic and behavioural factors potentially related to recent STI trends. The behaviour indicators examined here do not capture underlying sexual network characteristics and as such, related null findings do not rule out network-level behavioural mechanisms. We also did not examine potential risk compensation due to the use of HIV PrEP, which was recommended by the CDC starting 2014. It is thus not a systematic study; more comprehensive modelling and analyses are needed to pinpoint the key STI drivers.

Conclusion

In summary, we have examined recent trends of three notifiable bacterial STIs in the USA (chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis) and several nationally representative population and behavioural datasets. Our analyses highlight new partnership patterns among online daters and changing demographics of sexual orientation, which may be important to STI trends in the USA during 2010–2024. These preliminary findings represent a first step to generate new hypotheses and inform new lines of inquiry for future work on STI epidemiology and control.
