ABSTRACT
BackgroundBalancing work and family demands is a significant challenge for dual-earner couples, often undermining relationship satisfaction. While core self-evaluation is a well-established predictor of work-related well-being, its interpersonal effects on romantic relationships remain underexplored. Guided by conservation of resources and spillover-crossover theories, this study investigates the dyadic association between core self-evaluation and relationship satisfaction and examines the parallel mediating roles of work-to-family and family-to-work conflict.MethodsSurvey data from 271 Croatian dual-earner couples were analysed using an actor-partner interdependence mediation model. Both partners self-reported on the Core Self-Evaluations Scale, the Work-Family Conflict Scale, and the Perceived Relationship Quality Components Questionnaire.ResultsThe analysis indicated significant positive actor effects: individuals' core self-evaluation was associated with their own higher relationship satisfaction. For women, this association was fully mediated by their own lower work-to-family conflict. A significant indirect partner effect also emerged: men’s core self-evaluation was associated with higher relationship satisfaction in their partners via the men’s lower work-to-family conflict. Family-to-work conflict was not a significant mediator, and no significant gender differences were found in the overall strength of the actor or partner effects.ConclusionsThe study underscores core self-evaluation may function as a psychological resource for relationship satisfaction in dual-earner couples, operating through the mitigation of work-family conflict. The findings highlight distinct gendered pathways, suggesting processes of spillover for women and crossover from men to women. Interventions aimed at strengthening personal resources and managing work-domain stressors may enhance both individual and relational well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04014-6.

BODY
Introduction

How individuals perceive their own worth and effectiveness shapes not only their work experiences but also the quality of their intimate relationships. Core self-evaluations (CSE), a broad personality construct integrating self-esteem, self-efficacy, emotional stability, and locus of control, reflects a fundamental appraisal of self-worth and competence [1, 2]. Individuals with positive CSE tend to appraise challenges as manageable, rely on problem-focused coping, and maintain well-being across life domains [3, 4]. CSE reliably predicts desirable outcomes such as career satisfaction, work engagement, and leadership success [5–8], highlighting CSE as a key psychological resource in the working context.

Despite this evidence, the role of CSE in romantic relationships remains underexplored. Relationship satisfaction (RS) is a key indicator of relational quality, long-term stability, and individual well-being, and it evolves in response to both partners’ characteristics [9]. Specifically, CSE has been linked to lower depression in couples, partly via marital satisfaction, suggesting that more positive self-evaluations may contribute to more supportive relationship dynamics [10]. Research on the components of CSE indicates consistent patterns: high self-esteem and lower neuroticism are consistently linked to stronger commitment, responsiveness, and satisfaction for individuals and their partners [11, 12]. However, the pathways through which core self-evaluation relates to relationship quality in dual-earner couples navigating work and family demands remain insufficiently understood.

The work–family interface offers a promising explanation. Work-to-family conflict (WFC) and family-to-work conflict (FWC) reflect inter-role tensions where demands from one domain hinder performance in the other [13]. Family-originating role conflicts have been associated with reduced family well-being [14, 15], and meta-analytic evidence shows that one partner’s WFC can contribute to the other partner’s lower family satisfaction [16].

Personality differences also shape how individuals experience role conflicts. Traits including emotional stability [17, 18] and higher CSE [19, 20] generally buffer against both directions of conflict. Individuals with more positive self-evaluations perceive role overload as more controllable [21], experiencing reduced WFC and burnout [22]. These findings imply that CSE may foster RS by protecting both individuals and their partners from role conflicts. To date, Boyar and Mosley [23] conducted the only study that directly examined this pathway, finding that the association between CSE and family satisfaction was partially mediated by WFC. The present study expands this evidence by examining RS, a more specific relational outcome, and by modelling both partners’ CSE, work-family conflicts, and RS simultaneously. Such a dyadic perspective is warranted, as partners share variance in family-domain well-being, and work-family dynamics often extend beyond the individual level [9].

Additionally, some theoretical and empirical work suggests that individuals with high-CSE may be more motivated to take career initiative and maintain high performance, potentially prioritizing work over family responsibilities [24]. In such cases, FWC could increase even as WFC decreases. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the parallel mediating effects of both WFC and FWC in this dyadic model. In the following section, we outline the theoretical foundations of our dyadic path model and derive the hypotheses tested in this study.

Theoretical framework of the study

Research on individual differences and work–family dynamics has been guided by several theoretical perspectives. While a full review is beyond the scope of this paper, here we highlight the most influential frameworks that informed our hypotheses. First, social interdependence theory [25] postulates that relationship outcomes depend on both partners’ dispositions and their interplay. This perspective supports examining CSE in dyads, as one partner’s personality may influence not only their own but also their partner’s RS. Second, the spillover–crossover model [26] provides a framework for understanding how stress at work and home transfers within and across employed partners. At the individual level, spillover occurs when experiences from one domain (e.g. work) transfer into personal well-being in the other (i.e. family). At the dyadic level, crossover occurs when one partner’s stress or role conflicts affect the other partner’s well-being, often through mechanisms such as empathic distress, reduced social support, or shared exposure to stressors [16, 27, 28]. Meta-analytic evidence [16, 29, 30] confirms negative associations between WFC and family-related well-being (r = –0.19, k = 49) and between employees’ WFC and their partners’ outcomes (r = –0.17, k = 26). Dyadic studies also show that work-originating role conflicts are negatively associated with both individuals’ (r = –0.26) and partners’ (r = –0.14) well-being, although partner effects often weaken once individuals’ own role conflicts are controlled [30]. Domain-specificity also emerges: WFC relates more strongly to work-related well-being, while FWC is more closely tied to relationship quality and family outcomes [18, 30, 31].

Finally, the conservation of resources theory [32] emphasizes the role of resource depletion in shaping well-being outcomes. Accordingly, individuals strive to acquire and protect valuable resources, and when confronted with incompatible role demands, they are at risk of resource loss. Resources are defined as “those entities that either are centrally valued in their own right (e.g., self-esteem, close attachments, health, and inner peace) or act as a means to obtain centrally valued ends (e.g., money, social support, and credit)” ([32], p. 307). Personality traits like the positive CSE are psychological resources that enable individuals to manage competing demands and prevent WFC [18, 33], as well as to acquire further resource gains through processes such as work-family enrichment [34]. Research building on the conservation of resources perspective indicates that CSE helps individuals preserve resources by reducing negative appraisals of stress, enhancing coping, and preventing role overload [19, 35]. At the dyadic level, individuals with higher CSE may not only experience fewer role conflicts themselves but also help their partners manage competing demands more effectively, for example by offering greater support, which has been shown to reduce the recipient’s role conflicts (both WFC and FWC) [14, 36]. Building on these theoretical perspectives and empirical findings, we now turn to the aims of the present study.

Current study

Few studies have linked CSE to RS [10] and to lower WFC [19, 23, 35]. However, evidence is lacking on whether WFC and FWC jointly explain how CSE contributes to relationship quality, and whether these mechanisms operate both within and across partners in dual-earner couples. By adopting a dyadic perspective and modeling both mediators simultaneously, the present study addresses this gap in three ways: (1) it tests indirect pathways from CSE to RS via WFC and FWC, (2) it incorporates both intra-individual and crossover effects, and (3) it compares the relative importance of work- versus family-originating conflicts.

Guided by the theoretical frameworks outlined above, we examine how CSE contributes to RS in dual-earner heterosexual couples. Although job and family demands, role-specific stressors, and social support are well-established antecedents of role conflicts [14, 18, 26, 36], this study focuses on personality resources. At the direct level, we expect CSE to predict higher RS for oneself, consistent with conservation of resources theory, and for one’s partner, consistent with social interdependence theory, as well as prior findings that personality dispositions shape both intra-individual and interpersonal outcomes [9, 17, 23, 37]. We predicted the following actor and partner effects:

H1a (total actor effect): Individuals’ CSE will be positively associated with their own RS.H1b (total partner effect): Individuals’ CSE will be positively associated with their partners’ RS.

H1a (total actor effect): Individuals’ CSE will be positively associated with their own RS.

H1b (total partner effect): Individuals’ CSE will be positively associated with their partners’ RS.

Beyond direct effects, we expect indirect effects: CSE should reduce exposure to role conflicts, consistent with conservation of resources theory, and the spillover–crossover evidence linking personality resources to lower WFC and FWC [18, 19]. In turn, evidence supports that salient role conflicts relate to lower RS for oneself and one’s partner [16, 30].

H2 (actor effects): Individuals’ CSE will be negatively associated with their own WFC and FWC.H3a (indirect actor effect): The association between individuals’ CSE and their own RS will be mediated by their own WFC and FWC.H3b (indirect partner effect): The association between individuals’ CSE and their partners’ RS will be mediated by their own WFC and FWC.

H2 (actor effects): Individuals’ CSE will be negatively associated with their own WFC and FWC.

H3a (indirect actor effect): The association between individuals’ CSE and their own RS will be mediated by their own WFC and FWC.

H3b (indirect partner effect): The association between individuals’ CSE and their partners’ RS will be mediated by their own WFC and FWC.

Finally, although meta-analytic evidence suggests that FWC may be more strongly linked to RS than WFC [31], evidence is still missing regarding whether CSE predicts one type of conflict more strongly. Therefore, we test WFC and FWC simultaneously in a parallel mediation model, without positing differential hypotheses, but allowing exploratory comparison of their relative importance.

Evaluating gender differences in the partner effects of CSE

Previous research highlights the importance of considering gender differences in relationship processes. Women are often socialized toward greater emotional responsiveness and relational interdependence [38], which may increase their sensitivity to both their own and their partner’s stressors. Although a meta-analysis reported no consistent gender differences in crossover effects [16], a systematic review by Steiner and Krings [39] found stronger crossover from men to women in most studies (29 of 38), suggesting that women’s relationship satisfaction may be more strongly influenced by their partner’s experiences. Beyond the still inconsistent evidence regarding gender differences in the crossover of WFC [16, 39, 40] women’s RS appears generally more volatile and more affected by relational stressors. Recent meta-analytic evidence indicates greater variability in women’s than men’s relationship satisfaction, particularly at the lower end of the distribution [41], consistent with the notion that women are more susceptible to extreme declines in relationship quality due to their heightened relational interdependence. In this study, we focus on the gender differences in the effects of CSE on RS, testing whether:

H4: Partner effects of CSE on RS are greater for women than men.

Method

Participants and procedure

During the summer of 2022, 278 heterosexual urban couples from Croatia participated in the study. Recruitment occurred via advertisements, social media (e.g., Facebook), and referrals from psychology students at the University of Rijeka, who received course credit for referring eligible participants. Inclusion criteria required participants to be in a relationship for at least six months, aged 18–51 years, and employed at least part-time. Couples did not receive financial compensation.

Eligible couples provided informed consent and completed a paper-and-pencil survey at home. Psychology students, trained in data collection procedures, oversaw the process to ensure protocol compliance, support independent responding, and answer procedural questions if needed. Partners were seated apart and completed the questionnaires independently, preventing collaboration or commenting. Upon completion, each participant sealed their questionnaire in an envelope, linked to their partner’s with randomly generated couple codes to preserve anonymity and confidentiality. Participants were informed about the study procedures, expected duration, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

After excluding seven couples with incomplete data, the final sample comprised 271 dyads, married (51%), cohabiting (23.7%), and dating (25.2%). The average relationship length was 11.9 years (SD = 9.44; range = 6 months–33 years). Among these couples, 48.71% had children. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 50 years (M = 35.58, SD = 10.07 for men; M = 33.74, SD = 9.82 for women; t = 8.51, p < 0.001, d = 0.52). Most men (90.8%) and women (87.1%) were employed full-time, while the remainder worked part-time. Additional measures not reported here have been analysed in other publications [42]. The study procedure and materials were approved by the authors’ institutional ethics review committee.

Measures

Among the sociodemographic variables, participants reported their gender, age, sexual orientation, relationship duration, number of children, occupation, and highest level of education.

The Croatian translation of the Core Self-Evaluations Scale [1], previously applied in research and demonstrating good psychometric properties [43], was used. This 12-item measure (e.g. “I am capable of coping with most of my problems”), has demonstrated incremental validity, predicting job and life satisfaction better than a composite of the four individual traits that constitute the broader CSE. A mean score was computed, with higher scores indicating positive self-evaluations. Both the abovementioned scales used a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

We applied the Croatian translation and adaptation [40] of the two scales of the multidimensional Work-Family Conflict Scale [44] to measure both directions of conflict (work-to-family and family-to-work). Participants had to evaluate six items assessing their time- and strain-based work-originating role conflicts (e.g., “I have to miss family activities due to the amount of time I must spend on work responsibilities”) and six items assessing their time- and strain-based family-originating role conflicts (e.g., “I have to miss work activities due to the amount of time I must spend on family responsibilities”).

RS was assessed with the Perceived Relationship Quality Components Questionnaire [45], a six-item scale in which participants rate different aspects of their relationship: general satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, and love. The Likert-type scale ranges from 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely). Research using the Croatian version of this questionnaire has shown satisfactory psychometric properties [37].

The Omega and alpha reliability coefficients demonstrated good internal consistency across all scales, with the measures exhibiting acceptable comparability between genders. Further details, including the results of the confirmatory factor analysis, are provided in the Supplementary materials.

Data analysis

The application of the actor partner interdependence model (APIM; [46]) accounts for the nonindependence of dyadic data and allows the evaluation of intraindividual (actor) effects as well as interindividual or partner (i.e., crossover) effects that can explain the shared variance between two partners’ outcomes. The hypothesized parallel multiple mediation model where CSE predicts RS through WFC and FWC in a dyadic context was specified as the actor-partner interdependence mediation model (APIMeM) using the MEDYAD, regression-based procedure for R [47].

Relationship length (measured in months) was entered as a covariate. The additional role of couple’s number of children was considered in separate, supplementary analyses to assess the robustness of the findings. Predictor variables, the mediators and covariates were all mean-centered to facilitate interpretation and reduce multicollinearity. All effects were tested using a bootstrapping procedure with 5000 replications and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. Currently, no tools for power computation of APIMeM are available, therefore we computed the power for a simple APIM model. Given our sample size (N = 271), a correlation of predictor variables between partners 0.20, and the correlation between errors 0.50, the power of detecting actor effects of 0.25 would be 0.99, and the power of detecting partner effects of 0.15 would be 0.70 [48]. For a nondyadic parallel mediation model with two mediators, both correlated with the predictor [19] and mutually at 0.40, and associated to the outcome at −0.20 [29], and assuming a correlation between CSE and RS of 0.20, the power of detecting indirect effects would be 0.65 [49].

In the Supplementary materials, we provide evidence of the metric invariance and satisfactory psychometric properties of our measures. Additionally, we present the (mostly nonsignificant) results pertaining to hypothetical positive spillover and crossover through experiences of work-family enrichment.

A significant direct actor effect was found for men, indicating that their CSE were positively associated with their own RS (b = 0.13, SE = 0.05; p 0.05, CI: −0.03, 0.17). Furthermore, the direct partner effects of CSE were non-significant for both men's CSE predicting women's RS (b = 0.08, SE = 0.05; p > 0.05, CI: −0.03, 0.19) and women's CSE predicting men's RS (b = 0.01, SE = 0.05; p > 0.05, CI: −0.09, 0.11).

Hypothesis 2 was supported. For both women and men, their own CSE predicted their own WFC (women: b = −0.31, SE = 0.05; p < 0.001, CI: −0.41, −0.21; men: b = −0.19, SE = 0.06; p < 0.01, CI: −0.30, −0.07) and their own FWC (women: b = −0.16, SE = 0.04; p < 0.001, CI: −0.24, −0.08; men: b = −0.08, SE = 0.04; p < 0.05, CI: −0.16, −0.01). No partner effects were found: individual's CSE was not related to their partner’s WFC or FWC, indicating that role conflicts appear independent of a partner's self-evaluations.

H3a, which proposed that an individual's own WFC and FWC would mediate the link between their CSE and their own RS (indirect actor-mediating paths), was partially supported. While most paths were non-significant, one specific indirect effect was significant: women’s CSE was positively associated with their own RS through its effect on lowering their own WFC (b = 0.04, SE = 0.02, CI: 0.001, 0.07). However, this effect should be interpreted with caution as the lower bound of the confidence interval barely excludes zero, indicating a small and potentially unstable effect.

H3b, which proposed that an individual's own WFC and FWC would mediate the link between their CSE and their partner’s RS (indirect partner-mediating paths), was partially supported. Among all tested indirect pathways involving crossover effects, only one was significant: men's WFC mediated the association between men's CSE and women's RS (b = 0.03, SE = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.01, 0.06). No other indirect pathways were significant.

The total actor effect of men’s CSE on their own RS was significant (b = 0.16, SE = 0.05; p 0.05, CI: −0.06, 0.13). Relationship duration was a significant negative covariate for women’s, but not for men’s RS.

To provide the most direct test of H4 regarding gender differences in the total partner effects, we examined a simplified APIM without mediators. This approach offers greater statistical power and interpretational clarity for testing path constraints compared to the full mediation model. A likelihood ratio test comparing a model with constrained partner effects to an unconstrained model was not significant, χ2 (1) = 1.85, p = 0.174. This indicates that the total partner effect of CSE was not significantly different for women (b = 0.14) and men (b = 0.03). The overall partner effect, estimated by constraining the paths to be equal, was significant (b = 0.09, CI: 0.02, 0.15, p < 0.01).

For completeness, we also tested for gender differences in the total actor effects. The difference between the actor effect for women (b = 0.13) and men (b = 0.16) was not significant, χ2 (1) = 0.75, p = 0.386. The overall actor effect was significant (b = 0.14, CI: 0.08, 0.21, p < 0.001). These results from the total effects model converge with the findings from the full mediation model, consistently indicating an absence of significant gender differences in the strength of both actor and partner effects of CSE on RS.

Discussion

This study suggests that CSE represents a significant psychological resource for RS in dual-earner couples, operating through both direct and indirect pathways shaped by WFC. While recent research has established the dyadic role of CSE in relation to depression via marital satisfaction [10], the present findings point to WFC as a plausible explanatory pathway for RS. This extends prior individual-level and dyadic research on personality [23, 51] and meta-analytic evidence on marital satisfaction [52]. Importantly, the dyadic design shows that the broad construct of CSE predicts RS not only at the individual level but also across partners, largely through lower experiences of work-originating role conflicts.

Our findings provide support for the actor effects derived by conservation of resources theory [8, 32]. In line with the notion of “resource caravans”, which suggests that resources tend to co-occur and mutually reinforce one another, individuals with higher CSE, encompassing high self-esteem and low neuroticism, both established predictors of RS [11, 12], perceived their relationship as more satisfying. Our results expand previous evidence on the predictive validity of CSE components, like emotional stability for higher marital satisfaction [52, 53], and confirms CSE as a crucial personal resource.

We further extended prior research by examining the parallel mediating roles of both work-to-family and family-to-work interference, previously identified as antecedents of RS [22, 29]. Examining the mediating pathways, a possible, gender specific effect emerged. Among women, the association between their CSE and their RS was fully mediated by reduced WFC, indicating that effective management of work-related demands may be a pathway through which women’s personal resources contribute to relational well-being. In contrast, for men, the positive association between their CSE and their own perceived relationship quality was not accounted for by either mediator, suggesting other unmeasured mechanisms (e.g., the provision of partner social support [36, 39]) are at play. This pattern points to a potentially stronger spillover pathway for women, whereby managing work stress is relevant for relational well-being.

Beyond actor effects, our results align with social interdependence theory [25] and the spillover-crossover model [26] by revealing a significant indirect partner effect. Men’s higher CSE was associated with lower WFC, which in turn related to higher RS in their partners. This crossover pathway suggests that men’s personal resources may buffer their partners from the relational consequences of men’s work-originating role conflict. Our results align with previous findings that positive self-evaluations [10, 11] and internal locus of control [54] benefit intimate partners. They are also consistent with evidence linking incumbents’ WFC with detrimental effects on spouses’ general well-being and family-related outcomes across various studies and cultures [16, 39].

This study also expands the limited Croatian research on WFC, mostly centered on occupational stressors [40], by underscoring the importance of dispositional antecedents and their crossover effects. When comparing work-life quality internationally, social relations at work emerge as a particularly important resource in Croatia, while meaningfulness of work and autonomy appear comparatively less central to work-life quality [55]. Thus, our study highlights the value of dyadic research for understanding the role of dispositions in intimate relationship quality and crossover processes in cultural contexts that prioritize meaningful social relationships.

Another specificity of our sample might provide some explanation for the null findings appeared for the mediating effects of FWC. While the principle of “domain specificity” suggests FWC compared to WFC should be more strongly tied to family outcomes [17, 31], our study represents a conservative test, as nearly half of our participants were not parents and thus may have faced lower family demands. Although in our study the number of children did not result to be a significant mediator, future research focusing explicitly on dual-earner parents might reveal stronger effects of FWC. Furthermore, the fact that both mediators were included simultaneously controlled for spurious s associations. Supplementary analyses (available in the Supplementary materials) indicated that the strain-based component of WFC was the primary driver of the mediating effects, suggesting that the psychological experience of conflict is more impactful than mere time constraints.

Contrary to theoretical expectations and some empirical findings [9, 41], formal tests revealed no significant gender differences in the strength of actor or partner effects of CSE on RS. This suggests that the protective dyadic dynamics of CSE may function as a universal process within heterosexual couples, rather than being specific to one gender. This finding aligns with meta-analytic evidence showing no consistent gender differences in the partner effects of personality traits, including neuroticism, on relationship outcomes [53]. The sole significant crossover effect, from men’s CSE to women’s RS via men’s lower WFC, indicates that the mediating process through which effects emerge may vary by gender, even if the overall effect strength is similar. Replication in larger samples is needed to clarify these patterns.

Limitations and practical implications

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data prevents causal inference. Although guided by conservation of resources theory [7, 11, 32], the temporal sequence between FWC or WFC and RS could be reversed or bidirectional [15, 16, 27], as evidenced in recent longitudinal and diary studies [36]. Future research employing such designs is needed to confirm the directionality of these effects. Second, the use of self-report measures from a community sample may introduce common-method bias and limits the objectivity of the constructs assessed. Third, the observed mediation effects were modest, suggesting limited statistical power to detect all dyadic pathways. Larger samples would allow for more precise estimation of complex effects, particularly those involving partner mediation. The generalizability of the findings is limited to heterosexual couples within the Croatian context. Replication in more diverse samples, including couples from different cultural settings is necessary to test the universality of these associations. Also, recruitment through students could limit the diversity of the sample and potentially introduce self-selection effects. Finally, although we controlled for relationship length and confirmed that number of children did not alter the main results, other contextual factors were not assessed. Prior research highlights the role of work demands, resources, and career centrality in shaping spillover and crossover effects on family outcomes [27, 33, 35, 36]; these factors may moderate the protective role of CSE on RS.

Despite these limitations, our study advances research on dispositional predictors of relationship quality and emphasizes the value of a dyadic approach to understanding the work–family interface. A recent review [8] charted the evolution of CSE research, noting a growing interest in its non-work outcomes. Our study directly responds to this research gap by providing dyadic evidence for CSE's role in couple dynamics. Practically, the findings suggest that interventions targeting dual-earner couples should focus not only at work- rather than family- originating experiences, and not only on behavioural strategies of boundary management, but also on phenomenological experiences of role conflicts, particularly among individuals with lower personal resources and more negative self-appraisals. WFC is a subjective experience [40], reflecting the perceived incompatibility of work and family demands, rather than an objective measure of resource depletion. As such, interventions that target appraisal processes and locus of control, elements of CSE [1, 2], may be especially effective. Organizational initiatives, including family-supportive supervisor training, have been shown to mitigate WFC more effectively than formal policies such as flexible work arrangements alone [14, 27]. Extending this perspective, our dyadic analysis highlights the role of romantic partners as an additional resource. Psychoeducating couples about spillover–crossover dynamics, encouraging reflection on self-appraisal, and exploring the subjective experience of WFC may reduce blame and strengthen relationship resilience.

Conclusions

In today’s context of blurred work–family boundaries, rising work expectations, and pervasive technology, it is crucial to understand how dispositional characteristics relate to experiences of role conflict and evaluations of close relationships. The present findings suggest that individuals with greater personal resources, namely more positive self-evaluations, report higher satisfaction in their own romantic relationships and are also partnered with individuals who evaluate their relationships more positively. These associations were partially accounted for by lower reported WFC. Viewed through the conservation of resources framework, which our data partially supports, the results also offer a constructive perspective for individuals with lower CSE: they, and their partners may experience benefits from organizational interventions and family counselling aimed at enhancing work–family balance. Moreover, our research indicates an efficacious and well-adjusted partner can provide additional resources, fostering gain spirals. These insights inform organizational and counselling practices, underscoring the need for holistic approaches that target stressors originating in work roles, support effective segmentation between domains, and improve access to mental health resources for employees with fewer personal resources.
