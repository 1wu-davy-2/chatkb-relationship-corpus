ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to explore dysfunctional romantic beliefs among women undergraduate students and the meanings they attribute to romantic messages received from traditional media and social media. Using a qualitative research design, twenty heterosexual undergraduate women participated in in-depth semi-structured interviews. Data were analyzed using content analysis, and findings were supported with participants’ direct quotations to enhance interpretive depth.The findings indicated that participants perceived both traditional and social media as influential sources of messages about romance. While they emphasized that the content and presentation of these messages differed across media channels, they reported being consistently exposed to romantic narratives through both. At the same time, participants generally did not describe themselves as directly internalizing these messages; rather, they viewed many of the romantic relationships portrayed on social media as artificial, idealized, and inconsistent with real-life. Nevertheless, the results suggested that repeated exposure to media messages contributes to constructing romanticism as a relational necessity, potentially shaping romantic expectations in dysfunctional ways. Among the romantic beliefs identified, the strongest endorsement was for the belief in a "one and only" partner, followed by the “idealization” and the belief that "love conquers all." In contrast, the belief in "love at first sight" received the lowest level of endorsement. The findings are discussed within the broader context of gender roles and Turkish cultural norms, highlighting how media representations intersect with culturally embedded expectations surrounding romantic relationships.Keywords: Romantic beliefs, Romanticism, Traditional media, Social media, Women undergraduate students

BODY
Introduction

Romanticism remains a salient theme in modern life. Romantic relationships are recognized as one of the fundamental psychosocial needs across the lifespan [1, 2]. Romantic love is not only an individual phenomenon but also one that is shaped by the historical period and sociocultural context [3–5]. Therefore, the meaning attributed to romantic relationships may vary in accordance with individuals’ social and cultural setting. Within traditional gender role socialization, women are more likely to construct romantic relationships as central to their sense of self and life satisfaction, reflecting broader cultural expectations that equate femininity with relational orientation and emotional investment [6]. Gender differences in romantic beliefs are complex; however, cultural expectations regarding gender roles continue to shape how women and men differ in their perceptions of love and relationship expectations.

Research on gender differences in romantic beliefs has primarily focused on heterosexual relationships and, findings have been mixed. Some studies suggest that men may generally exhibit more romantic tendencies than women [7, 8], while simultaneously holding more pragmatic views on relationships. In line with this perspective, Singh [9] highlights gender-based differences in romantic ideation, noting that women are more likely to idealize love and romantic experiences, whereas men tend to adopt more emotionally detached or pragmatic perspectives regarding future relationships. Supporting this view, cross-cultural evidence suggests that women report stronger romantic tendencies compared to men [10]. Similarly, Sprecher & Hatfield [11] suggested that women were more likely than men to endorse love in marriage decisions.

In the line of heterosexual relationship context, the observed gender differences in romantic beliefs may be understood through gender socialization processes and broader patriarchal social structures [12]. According to Social Role Theory, women and men internalize different expectations regarding intimacy, emotional expression, and commitment through socialization [4]. Moreover, patriarchal social norms may reinforce traditional relationship scripts by encouraging women to invest emotionally more strongly than men. Gender differences are seen in relational priorities in romantic relationships [13]. These differences reflect patriarchal social norms operating through ambivalent sexism in romantic relationships [14]. Consequently, gender differences in romantic beliefs may reflect socially constructed relationship expectations rather than individual psychological differences. Especially, communication technologies have a close relationship with the production and maintenance of heteronormativity [15].

Both traditional media and social media platforms are saturated with narratives pertaining to romantic relationships. Building on this perspective, Clyde et al. [16] highlight the profound social transformations over the past five decades, noting that the relational needs and expectations of contemporary young adults differ markedly from those of previous generations. Whereas individuals in earlier periods primarily formed their understanding of romantic relationships through personal experiences, observations of close others, and artistic expressions such as literature and music, today’s young people are exposed to a broader array of sources. Among these, visual and narrative media such as films play a particularly influential role in constructing romantic ideals [17]. Moreover, in the digital age, the proliferation of “romanticized” portrayals of real individuals on social media raises questions regarding the authenticity and realism of such depictions. Social networking platforms, such as dating apps, are spaces that reflect the heteronormativity, patriarchal structures, and power relations embedded in society [15].

Why research romantic beliefs

Unrealistic romantic beliefs have the potential to harm both the individual and the relationship they are in [7]. Sprecher and Metts [7] define romantic relationship beliefs as dysfunctional beliefs that shape unrealistic expectations about relationships. According to their classification, four core romantic relationship beliefs can be identified: “love will find a way,” “one and only love,” “true love will last forever,” and “idealization”.

Dysfunctional beliefs on relationship are defined as cognitively rigid, unrealistic, and irrational assumptions regarding the nature, development, and functioning of romantic relationships, which systematically distort individuals’ expectations and contribute to maladaptive emotional and behavioral outcomes within intimate partnerships [18]. Consistent with this definition, these beliefs have been associated with variables such as low relationship satisfaction [19–21], poor conflict resolution skills [22], and higher psychological dating violence victimization [23, 24]. Taken together, these findings highlight the detrimental implications of dysfunctional romantic beliefs.

Why heterosexual women?

The present study focused specifically on heterosexual undergraduate women because the theoretical framework and much of the empirical literature on dysfunctional romantic beliefs, media influences, and romantic relationship expectations have primarily examined heterosexual relationships. More recently, scholars have emphasized that sexual and gender minority individuals have been underrepresented in relationship research and have called for greater inclusivity to better understand the diversity of romantic experiences [25]. Feiring et al. [26] has indicated that LGB + emerging adults encounter relationship experiences that differ in important ways from those of heterosexual individuals, including challenges related to coming out, varying levels of outness, and family acceptance. Research conducted in Türkiye indicates that romantic relationship processes and dynamics differ according to sexual orientation [27]. Recent research conducted in Türkiye suggests that romantic relationships among LGB individuals are shaped by contextual dynamics [28]. Similarly, Ege [29] argues that queer identities in Turkish cinema are frequently portrayed within a patriarchal cultural framework as sources of comic relief, stereotypes, or objects of violence. This suggests that, within the Turkish context, differences between heterosexual and LGBTQ + romantic relationships extend beyond the relationships themselves to the ways in which these relationships are represented in media. Thus, the media representations of romantic relationships, which constitute a central focus of the present study, may also differ according to the sexual and relational identities they portray. Accordingly, this study focuses on heterosexual undergraduate women, and its findings should be interpreted within this context. Unless otherwise stated, “romantic relationships” refers to heterosexual relationships, in line with the study sample and the literature reviewed.

Research context: romantic media in Türkiye

Türkiye represents a unique sociocultural context in which traditional values and modern influences coexist, shaping the ways romantic relationships are understood and experienced. The patriarchal social norms in Türkiye play an important role in shaping women’s life choices [30]. In addition, media plays a central role in this process, particularly through widely consumed television series, films, and digital platforms that frequently portray idealized and emotionally intense romantic narratives. Turkish television dramas, in particular, have gained both national and international prominence for their dramatized depictions of love, sacrifice, and relational conflict, often reinforcing themes such as destiny, enduring love, and emotional devotion. In Türkiye, alongside the widespread use of traditional media, social media use was also highly prevalent. Approximately 82.4% of internet users report primary purpose for internet use as accessing social media, while 90.8% of social media users are between the ages of 18 and 25 [31]. Unlike traditional media, social media allows users not only to consume but also to create and share publicly their relationship-related content [32]. Therefore, romantic beliefs among young women in Türkiye are likely to be influenced not only by traditional media but also by the interactive and highly visual nature of social media, which increasingly shape perceptions of romantic relationships.

The rapid expansion of social media has influenced the mental health of individuals [33] and also introduced new forms of romantic representation, where individuals curate and share idealized versions of their relationships, further blurring the boundaries between reality and fiction. These mediated portrayals not only reflect existing cultural norms but also actively contribute to the construction and reinforcement of romantic beliefs, particularly among young adults navigating their early relational experiences within a rapidly changing social landscape.

Theoretical foundation

Romantic relationships are influenced by media channels [34]. In this context, Fox and Frampton [35] propose a deeper understanding of the traditional media’s role in romantic relationships. Hefner and Wilson [36] argue that media often promote idealized portrayals of sex, romance, and relationships. This mechanism can be further explained through Cultivation Theory posits that prolonged exposure to recurring media narratives shapes individuals’ perceptions of social reality by normalizing specific themes and representations [37, 38]. From a broader perspective, the influence of cultivation theory persists despite the diversification and evolution of media platforms [39]. In this sense, the theory highlights how mass media contribute to the construction of social reality [38].

Building on this theoretical foundation, the influence of media on romantic beliefs is consistent with cultivation theory, which suggests that media consumption plays a significant role in shaping viewers’ perceptions of reality [36, 40, 41]. Romantic beliefs are therefore not solely shaped by formal and systematic education; rather, they are largely influenced by observation and exposure to media messages such as advertisements, films, and other cultural representations [42]. Accordingly, individuals who consume romantic movies tend to report higher unrealistic expectations on love and marriage [43]. Moreover, popular media continues to reinforce romantic beliefs [36, 40], and mediated relational scripts may guide unrealistic relationship expectations and destiny beliefs [44]. In particular, the belief that “love conquers all” and “soul mate” have been associated with a particular type of higher media consumption [45]. Furthermore, watching romantic media such as television s hows or romantic comedies can influence romantic beliefs [17, 41]. It is crucial to consider not only traditional media but also social media within the context of cultivation theory.

Unlike traditional media, digital media platforms provide interactive, personalized, and algorithm-driven content, allowing users to create and disseminate media messages. A meta-analytic study indicated that social media use is associated with a range of attitudes and beliefs within the framework of Cultivation Theory and also younger samples may be more susceptible to cultivation effects [46]. Social networking platforms, particularly Instagram, may function as cultivation environments with repeated exposure to idealized contents [47]. Given the cultivation role of social media in emerging adulthood, social networking sites reinforce social comparison and online aggression victimization [48] and also contribute to digital dating abuse [49]. Collectively, these findings highlight the necessity of integrating media exposure and romantic beliefs to better understand the sociocultural mechanisms underlying contemporary romantic relationships.

The present study

Considering sociocultural mechanisms, media representations of romantic relationships have historically been gendered in ways that position women [50, 51]. Feminist media scholars argue that popular romantic genres reinforce gendered expectations of emotional labor, relational investment, and idealized intimacy [52]. Although the media’s influence on romantic relationships is well documented [41, 53], research on romantic beliefs of women remains limited [54]. The existing literature has predominantly relied on quantitative methodologies and Western samples, thereby limiting culturally contextualized and gender sensitive understandings of romantic belief formation [7, 36]. Culture plays a central role in romantic relationships [55, 56], and research further underlines the importance of cultural factors in romantic relationship formation within Turkish society [57].

Culturally grounded and gender sensitive understandings of romantic belief formation is important. As a result, the lived experiences and meaning-making processes of Turkish heterosexual women university students remain underexplored, highlighting a significant gap in the literature. The research question of the study is “What is the role of traditional media and social media in shaping romantic beliefs of undergraduate heterosexual women in Türkiye?”.

Method

This study was guided by a qualitative research framework, which assumes that reality is socially constructed and that individuals assign subjective meanings to their experiences [58]. Within this methodological framework, participants’ perceptions of romantic beliefs were explored through semi-structured interviews.

Participants

Twenty heterosexual self-identified women participated in the study. Purposive sampling, which involves intentionally selecting participants who are relevant to the research questions and meet predefined eligibility criteria, was used in this study. This sampling method facilitates the selection of participants who can provide relevant insights into the research questions [59]. The participants were undergraduate students from a small city (Yozgat, Türkiye), which may reflect a more limited urban context compared to large metropolitan cities. Given that romantic beliefs are embedded within culturally dominant heterosexual relationship scripts, focusing on a single relational framework was used for thematic exploration. In qualitative research, sample specificity is crucial for information power [60]. Therefore, this study focused on heterosexual women not because heterosexuality is assumed to represent all romantic experiences, but because heterosexual relationship scripts remain culturally dominant in Turkish media, making them an appropriate context for examining the cultivation of romantic beliefs. Nevertheless, this sampling strategy limits the transferability of the findings to individuals with LGBTQ + individuals.

The ages of participants ranged from 21 to 35 years, with a mean age of 22.2 years (SD = 3.11). Six participants (30%) reported having never had a committed romantic relationship, at the time of the interview, six (30%) were currently in a romantic relationship, and fourteen (70%) had previously at least one committed romantic relationship. The number of relationship experiences reported by participants ranged from 1 to 6. Of the 20 participants, eight were enrolled in the Guidance and Psychological Counseling program, and twelve were enrolled in teacher education programs. Participants subjectively reported their socioeconomic status as middle class. All participants indicated that they actively used social media and traditional media platforms.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling and provided written informed consent prior to the interview. A study invitation outlining the research objectives, inclusion criteria, and ethical considerations was distributed through university communication channels, including social media platforms. Participants were informed that the study concerned romantic relationship perceptions and media. Individuals who expressed interest contacted the researcher and scheduled for interviews. Participation was entirely voluntary. Considering the sensitive nature of romantic beliefs, some ethical precautions were implemented throughout the study. Prior to each interview, participants were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary and they could decline to answer any question or withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason. None of the participants disclosed experiences requiring immediate intervention during the interviews. Nevertheless, the interviewer was prepared to pause or terminate the interview if any participant experienced emotional distress. In such cases, participants would have been provided with information about the psychological support services available at the university, if needed. The sample size was determined in line with qualitative research principles of data saturation, defined as the point at which additional interviews yield no substantially new themes or insights [61]. Moreover, guided by the principle of “information power” [60], 20 interviews provided sufficient informational depth to support analytic rigor.

Data collection process

The interview form was developed based on a review of relevant literature, which informed the selection of items related to romanticism and romantic beliefs. A draft form was created, and after writing the interview questions, the researcher sought feedback from two academicians specializing in the psychological counseling field. To assess the clarity of the questions, five pilot interviews were conducted. After this process, it was observed that participants had difficulty fully understanding the concept of romantic beliefs when no prior definition was provided. Therefore, a brief and clear definition of romantic beliefs was added to the interview protocol. In addition, the draft form included separate questions regarding traditional media and social media influences. However, pilot interviews revealed that participants tended to discuss these media forms in an interconnected manner, therefore, questions regarding media were combined into a single item. The final interview form was then revised based on the feedback received. The final version included four personal and background information questions (age, socioeconomic level, relationship experience, relationship status) and eleven questions on romantic beliefs. These questions focused on perceptions of romantic beliefs, their necessity in relationships, their impact on romantic relationship expectations, the four main romantic beliefs (including "love conquers all," "one and only," idealization”, and “love at first sight”), and the influence of traditional media and social media on these beliefs. The full interview guide was provided in Appendix A.

Procedure

Ethical approval was obtained from the university's board of human subjects of the Ethics Committee of Yozgat Bozok University. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of this committee. The interviews were conducted individually and face-to-face in the office of the researcher at the university. The interviews were recorded and later transcribed for content analysis. Following transcription, the data were coded by two coders: the researcher and an expert with an academician in psychological counseling, specializing in close relationships.

Analysis

The interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis following the procedures outlined by Braun and Clarke [62, 63]. The interview texts were coded by two independent coders to enhance the trustworthiness of the study. The analytic process proceeded in six phases (familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report) [62, 63]. The coders held regular meetings to discuss the emerging codes and themes, jointly reviewed the coding process, and developed a shared understanding of the scope and meaning of the codes. Also, interrater reliability was found as 0.95 for coders.

In qualitative research, presenting data with direct quotes without excessive interpretation is recommended to improve internal reliability [64]. Yıldırım and Şimşek [65] also highlight the importance of using direct quotes for the validity of qualitative studies. In this study, direct quotes were utilized to enhance both internal reliability and validity, and to ensure participant anonymity, expressions such as “P” followed by numbers were used in presenting the findings.

Trustworthiness

To enhance the trustworthiness of the study, different strategies were used. First to provide credibility of the study, opinions of two experts specialized in romantic relationships were taken into consideration for the interview questions. Second, five pilot interviews were conducted to assess the understandability of the questions. Third, intercoder reliability was provided to empower the trustworthiness. To provide referential adequacy in documenting the findings of the study, sample quotations were presented. In addition, member checking was conducted after the preliminary analysis by sharing a summary of the emerging themes with a subset of participants. These participants indicated that the findings accurately reflected their experiences and interpretations. Lastly, peer debriefing was also integrated throughout the analytic process.

Researcher positionalities and reflexivity

Reflexivity is at the center of qualitative research as a result of its nature. The researcher, who conducted the interviews, held a doctoral degree in the psychological counseling field, was a Turkish woman, married and 40 years old, and had 19 years of experience in the field of psychological counseling, as well as experience in qualitative research and romantic beliefs. The researcher has personal familiarity with romantic media which provides both insight into participants’ experiences. Cultural background, gendered expectations, and personal experiences of romance were considered carefully during interviews, data coding, and thematic interpretation with the meetings with another coder. By acknowledging these positionalities, the researcher aimed to maintain credibility, and develop a deeper understanding of participants' experiences on romantic relationship beliefs and media. Additionally, the researcher had no experience using certain social media platforms (e.g., TikTok), which may have limited her understanding of the phenomenon. During data analysis, reflexive memo writing was employed to document emerging interpretations and personal assumptions. Codes and themes were continuously reviewed against the original transcripts to ensure that findings were grounded in participants' narratives rather than the researcher's perception or expectations.

Findings

Perception of Romanticism

The first three interview questions focused on understanding participants' perceptions of romanticism, the extent to which they prioritize it, and the impact of these beliefs on their relationship expectations. When asked about the importance of romanticism in relationships, most participants described it as necessary, and none stated that it is unimportant. Some participants emphasized the need for balance in romanticism and highlighted its logical aspects. Table 1 further illustrates participants’ perceptions of romanticism, showing that it is predominantly associated with love, understanding, and commitment. Despite participants' perceived significance of romanticism, most participants focused on its negative effects on expectations. Overall, these results indicate that romanticism is valued, its effects on relationships perceived in a complex way.

Perceptions of romanticism, the need for romanticism, and expectations

Participants predominantly conceptualized romanticism in terms of emotional and relational qualities, including love, care, passion, respect, and fidelity. Romanticism was also associated with relational characteristics such as understanding, acceptance, and harmony, which were viewed as essential for maintaining a healthy relationship. Many emphasized a desire to be shown love, with some explaining this need through material or verbal expressions, such as gifts or compliments. These expressions were viewed as indicators of care, effort, and emotional investment rather than solely material acts. One participant expressed this sentiment as follows: “I truly like being thought about me, for example, when he sees something and should think 'I will buy this for her.'” P3. Another participant explained the need to hear romantic expressions as follows: “I want my partner to constantly compliment me, for example. Because unless these things happen, I think there is no difference between a normal friend and a romantic partner.” P14.

In the interviews, gender role expectations were frequently mentioned, either explicitly or indirectly, and expectations regarding gifts and surprises were often cited from a romantic perspective; however, participants did not mention these as ways of expressing their own romanticism toward their partners. Romanticism was frequently associated with behaviors through which women felt valued and emotionally special. Regarding the gender role, one participant emphasized the role of gender differences in romanticism by describing romanticism as a type of effort characteristic of men, stating that: “I await romanticism from man. The things that he did can be described as romantic. I do not think I can do something romantic for a man. I know my point is not true but that is the situation.” P16.

When asked about the importance of romanticism in relationships, most participants described it as necessary, and none of them underestimated romanticism in relationships. However, dependency-based conceptualizations of romanticism, such as feeling incomplete without a partner or defining romanticism through emotional dependence, were mentioned only rarely. Additionally, some emphasized the need for balance in romanticism and highlighted its logical aspects suggesting that unrealistic romantic expectations could negatively influence relationship functioning. One participant expressed this viewpoint with the following statement: “I think the polarities in the relationship are destructive for partners. High emotions and high expectations can be romantic but also detrimental in the long term. In terms of romanticism, it should be balanced.” P7.

When exploring the relationship between romantic beliefs and expectations, most participants stated that their romantic beliefs significantly influenced their relationship expectations. They primarily highlighted the negative effects of these beliefs. Common issues included doubts about whether a relationship was the right one, disappointment due to high expectations, feeling undervalued because of romantic standards, conflicts with their partner, and either not starting or delaying a romantic relationship. Notably, participants who had never been in a romantic relationship emphasized that romanticism contributes to establishing relationship standards and encourages them to be more selective in partner choice. One participant described this challenge as follows: “Because of my high standards, I never feel close to trying a relationship, and that makes me feel lonely in terms of romantic relationships.” P2. Another participant focused on her despair because of her romantic beliefs: “I am not sure if I can have a romantic relationship one day. I sometimes give up hope.” P12. Similarly, other participants emphasized postponing or avoiding relationships due to their expectations: “I have too many criteria, that is why I cannot have a relationship.” P6. One participant specifically highlighted the role of social media in shaping her expectations, stating: “I do not make choices or postpone them because of my expectations.” P4. One other participant explained the negative effects of romantic beliefs on her relationship expectations explicitly: “I am always looking for the most ideal one, and that makes me selective. As I see the relationships around me, my criteria increase, such as being respectful, showing love, etc. It is also getting harder to find someone like that.” P15.

Conversely, participants with current or previous romantic relationship experience emphasized that romanticism fosters expectations within relationships, which may subsequently lead to conflict and disappointment. Some participants focused on conflicts in their romantic relationships because of their romantic expectations and articulated these: “I expect some gifts and flowers to see my value in him. When he does not show me this in a romantic way, I argue with him, and I compare my relationship with the relationships on social media.” P11. “I usually have problems with very high expectations. For example, I want to hear that I am loved all the time. I always expect him to prepare gifts and surprises for me; they may not be financial.” P17.

Participants further reported that these expectations were often shaped by comparisons between their own relationships and the idealized portrayals of romantic relationships on social media. The majority of participants recognized that the romantic relationships depicted on social media were unrealistic. Nevertheless, they indicated that exposure to these portrayals continued to shape their romantic expectations. One participant elaborated that romantic expectations led her to question not only her relationship and her partner but also herself: “Relationships are constantly on display, but I believe that those relationships are not healthy either. For example, people feel that they have to prove themselves and their relationships with others. I think this is a result of pressure. When people do not know anything about relationships, they think that is what relationships are like. They try to be like others, which prevents them from being natural. Even if I know this, I am very affected by it. I am affected by it in this way, for example, when we see young couples doing events. Her lover buys her a gift. Inevitably, one asks the following question: 'Is there something wrong with me?' P14. On the other hand, some of the participants underlined the importance of balance in romanticism, and one of them explained this: “It should be balanced and not at extremes. Partners should make each other feel special, though.” P18.

Endorsement of four main romantic beliefs

The study found that the most commonly endorsed beliefs were: "one and only," “idealization”, "love at first sight," and "love conquers all" (Table 2). Table 2 shows different levels of agreement with romantic beliefs among participants. The belief “One and Only” is the most supported, suggesting that many people believe in the idea of a single ideal romantic partner. “Idealization” is also widely accepted, indicating that people often tend to view their romantic partners in a very positive or idealized way. In contrast, “Love Conquers All” is less supported, as half of the participants disagree with this idea, showing a more realistic approach to relationships. Opinions about “Love at First Sight” are more divided, with similar proportions of agreement and disagreement. Overall, the results suggest that participants tend to support some traditional romantic beliefs, especially those related to exclusivity and idealization, while being more skeptical about extreme or absolute views of love.

Romantic belief endorsement of participants

Although the study was not designed to compare participants according to relationship history, some noteworthy patterns emerged across the interviews. Participants who had never experienced a romantic relationship more frequently endorsed idealized beliefs, particularly the belief in finding "the one and only." They often explained their lack of relationship experience by referring to waiting for the "right person," having perfectionistic expectations, or becoming increasingly selective when evaluating potential partners. Interestingly, these participants were less likely to endorse the belief that love alone could overcome all obstacles. Instead, they emphasized the importance of family compatibility, receiving parental approval, and finding a partner who was loving, understanding, and compatible with their family's expectations. In contrast, participants with current or previous relationship experience tended to describe romantic relationships in more experience-based and flexible terms. No clear differences related to relationship history were observed across the remaining themes.

Perceived cultivation effects in romantic beliefs

The findings revealed several notable differences in the influence of traditional and social media on romantic beliefs (Table 3). Although both media types appeared to cultivate romantic beliefs, they did so through different mechanisms. Traditional media primarily reinforced romantic myths such as soulmates, destiny, and the belief that love conquers all, whereas social media promoted idealized portrayals of romantic relationships through carefully curated images and seemingly perfect couples. At the same time, participants emphasized that both media types also encouraged critical reflection by exposing them to alternative relationship narratives, unhealthy relationship dynamics, and relationship advice.

Perceived differences on traditional media messages and social media messages

After identifying their specific romantic beliefs, participants were asked about traditional media and social media messages related to these beliefs. Regarding media messages, participants reported receiving the most messages related to “idealization”, "one and only", “love at first sight”, and, lastly, "love conquers all." The sources of these messages included TV series, reality programs, social media, films, news, and novels. The influence of social media was particularly emphasized in relation to idealization. Films were most often mentioned for reinforcing messages about "one and only" and “love at first sight”. Additionally, Turkish TV series were specifically noted for promoting the "love conquers all" message.

As shown in Table 4, participants’ endorsement of certain romantic beliefs differed from their perceptions of media messages. While “One and Only” and “Idealization” were the most strongly endorsed beliefs, all themes were perceived as being highly represented in media content.

Perceived media messages of participants

Love conquers all

One of the central romantic relationship beliefs explored in this study was the idea that “love conquers all”. Participants’ responses reveal a divided stance toward this belief. Half of the participants explicitly rejected the notion that love alone is sufficient to overcome all challenges. This distribution suggests that although the belief remains culturally visible, it is not internalized by the majority of participants.

Importantly, agreement with this belief often co-occurred with references to media portrayals of enduring love, particularly films and social media content that emphasize perseverance. In contrast, those who rejected the belief tended to draw from lived relational experiences (e.g. past relationships, parents’ relationships) rather than media narratives. From an interpretative standpoint, this pattern may reflect a tension between culturally idealized depictions of love and participants’ experiential realities. They explained these differing views with the following statements: “I do not believe it. Love does not forgive everything. There are things love will forgive and things it will not forgive.” P13. “There were times when I thought like that and had a hard time breaking away, but when I look back on my later life, I face the fact that love is not enough.” P14.

In addition, participants emphasized that this idealized message conveyed in Turkish television series and films was contradicted by the relationship conflicts and interpersonal problems frequently portrayed in daytime television and reality programs targeting women. They argued that these programs presented a more conflict-oriented view of romantic relationships, challenging the fairy-tale narratives commonly depicted in films and TV series. In contrast to the idealized portrayal of women as “princesses” or “fortunate partners” on social media, participants noted that television news and daytime programs targeting women predominantly represented women as victims, particularly in the context of relationship problems and interpersonal conflict. Some participants focused especially on love never conquering all, particularly if it was about violence. In this regard, one participant expressed: “I am not a person who is affected by social media. I think social media supports the fairytale part of relationships. For example, their dating stories seem beautiful and impressive. When I see them, I admire them. On the other hand, when I think about the violence news, I want it to never happen because of violence in relationships. Violence against women distances me from relationships. It is terrifying.” P4. “I believe love endures, but I do not think it should endure all kinds of negative circumstances. It can turn into emotional violence, which does not bring happiness.” P15.

Participants emphasized that Turkish television series and films, particularly television dramas, frequently portray the idea that true love conquers all. They described these narratives as depicting romantic relationships that ultimately overcome a wide range of obstacles, including socioeconomic differences (e.g., poor woman–wealthy man or vice versa), sociocultural differences (e.g., ethnic backgrounds), and marital status (e.g., extramarital relationships). Participants further noted that these narratives often position women as the primary source of sacrifice and compromise, reinforcing the expectation that women should bear greater responsibility for sustaining romantic relationships. They provided examples of their views with the following statements: “Especially in the Turkish series, it is emphasized that love will forgive everything. For example, Ferit (a very famous main character in a Turkish series), the man does everything but somehow the woman continues to love.” P13. “There is a lot of romance in the media, but I think it is wrong. My parents also got married with great love, but then they got divorced. This is glorified on social media. If two people are hurting each other, no matter how much love they have, they should break up.” P17. While most participants reported agreeing that they receive messages from traditional media and social media supporting the "love conquers all" belief, some stated that they partially agreed with this message. Conversely, a few participants mentioned that they do not receive or endorse messages related to this belief underlying the positive and negative examples in the media.

Participants perceived social media as conveying similar messages through different mechanisms. Because social media is based on users’ self-presentation, participants suggested that couples often communicate the idea that love overcomes difficulties by selectively sharing positive photographs, and happy moments, even when experiencing significant challenges in their relationships. In addition, although most participants reported being exposed to both explicit and implicit messages suggesting that love can overcome all obstacles, they stated that they did not believe these portrayals accurately reflected real life relationships. “I do not think it is shown much in the media. Relationships are divided into two types of social media. First, they are shown as a perfect couple, but 3–5 days later, the news of their separation comes. On the other hand, there are those who constantly break up and make up. They are either with the same partner or with different partners. Movies, TV series and news are worse than social media, and there are intrigues, deceptions and such things. There is no picture that reflects real love.” P18.

One and only

Another prominent romantic belief explored in this study was the “One and Only” or soulmate belief. A majority of participants expressed agreement with this belief, while some partially agreed and some disagreed with it. Notably, participants who endorsed this belief frequently referenced romantic films and social media content that portray relationships as fated and uniquely predetermined. In contrast, those who rejected the belief often emphasized personal experiences and the possibility of multiple compatible partners. From an interpretative perspective, this finding may reflect the strong cultural circulation of soulmate narratives, which are repeatedly reinforced through mediated romantic scripts.

Participants emphasized that traditional media, particularly Turkish television series and films, reinforce the belief that there is one destined or “right” person for everyone. In addition, one participant noted that this belief is further idealized in romance novels, where the notion of finding one’s soulmate is frequently glorified. Some of the participants agreed with this belief and mentioned their religious belief and value system. A participant referred to a verse from the Qur’an as an example of how this belief is also supported through religious discourse as follows: “I believe too. There is a verse that says that people are created with their spouses. That is why I believe that there is someone who will complete each other and that we came as spouses.” P1. “If it is the right person, you feel complete. It is an incarnation of you in another body. For instance, Adam and Eve are examples for completion.” P3. On the other hand, some participants expressed disagreement with this belief: “I disagree. My previous relationships were like my soul mate. When I look at my current relationship, they are nothing. Everyone continues their lives again.” P16.

In terms of media messages, just one of the participants mentioned that she did not see any messages on media or social media about “one and only” belief. On the other hand, all of the other participants reported that they took messages from media and social media about “one and only” belief and explained their beliefs with such words; “Especially when I look at the books and literature I read, I wish love was like this. The world that books offer is a different world for me. In particular, Korean youth literature. There are a lot of romantic messages in these books, for example, you have a soul mate and you somehow meet him.” P18.

Regarding social media, participants explained that this belief is primarily conveyed through users’ tendency to present their partners as “the right person” by sharing carefully curated photographs, stories, and relationship-related posts. Furthermore, several participants highlighted that relationship-focused influencers reinforce this belief by sharing personal narratives suggesting that they found the “right person” through specific thoughts, behaviors, or manifestation practices. According to participants, influencers frequently conveyed messages such as “I changed my mindset, practiced manifestation, and then the right person entered my life,” thereby strengthening the belief that everyone has a destined partner. One participant reflected: “There is a lot of messaging on social media that we can find our soul mate if we manifest. Sometimes I think, maybe I do not know how to want it.” P9.

Idealization

The belief in the idealization of romantic relationships constituted another central theme. More than half of the participants agreed that relationships should be ideal or highly fulfilling, while a small number of participants partially agreed and some disagreed with it. These findings suggest that although idealization remains influential, a significant proportion of participants adopt a more realistic stance toward romantic relationships.

Importantly, those who endorsed idealized expectations frequently referenced romantic films and influencer content portraying “perfect couples” and aesthetically curated relationship narratives. In contrast, participants who rejected idealization emphasized lived experience, highlighting the inevitability of conflict and imperfection in real relationships. From an interpretative phenomenological perspective, this pattern suggests contradiction between mediated romantic scripts and experiential knowledge. While media narratives appear to normalize perfection as relationship standards, participants’ lived experiences often introduce realism and critical view. Participants illustrated this belief with the following examples: “For example, in a Turkish series, the man does everything for the woman. I follow these types of stories, especially on reels on Instagram. I am affected because everything is shown better on Instagram. When I realize that my expectations are affected by what I watch, I remind myself that this is the makeup of relationships. I remind myself to adopt a more rational perspective.” P1.

Although participants acknowledged the influence of both traditional media and social media on the “Idealization” belief, they perceived social media as having a substantially greater impact. Participants emphasized that social media promotes the idealization of romantic relationships through carefully curated portrayals of couples, as well as through relationship influencers who provide advice on achieving the “ideal” relationship. They also noted that social media reinforces traditional gender expectations by portraying women as passive partners who should expect grand romantic gestures and expensive gifts from their partners. In contrast, participants indicated that traditional media, particularly daytime television programs aimed at women (e.g., bride programs), communicates messages suggesting that women should meet ideal standards in terms of their physical appearance, personal qualities, and relationship behaviors.

“There are so many messages on social media. The ideas are presented visually. When I see them, I also think yes, a relationship should be like this. For example, if a video comes up saying that a relationship should always be happy, my thoughts; yes, it should be like this. It is also supported in the media, for example, women's programs criticize women for being the ideal bride for men.” P13. Similarly, one participant described how social media shapes specific relational expectations in everyday practices: “I think the perceptions about relationships have changed a lot in recent years with social media. We know which month to send flowers with which note. In addition, I expect flowers at those special times. I have expectations, but when they are not met, I start arguing with my partner.” P17.

Love at first sight

The belief in “love at first sight” represents another prominent romantic narrative explored in this study. Participants were notably divided. While some agreed with the belief and very small number of participants partially agreed, half of the participants rejected the idea that love at first sight. This distribution indicates a contradiction between immediate emotional attraction and the view that love develops gradually over time. While mediated narratives frequently celebrate immediacy and emotional intensity, participants’ accounts may reveal a more complex understanding of love as either an instantaneous feeling or a process unfolding over time. This duality highlights how individuals both internalize and critically evaluate dominant romantic ideologies.

Most participants indicated that media and social media promote the idea of "love at first sight," particularly through movies and TV series where love occurs unexpectedly. In this respect, participants frequently referred to recurring portrayals in visual and digital media: “This idea is heavily promoted, especially in movies and TV shows—people fall in love right after a random encounter. I think love at first sight is strongly emphasized in social media videos as well.” P14. “It’s always about love at first sight. They bump into each other, drop their books, and fall in love. In all my years as a student, I’ve never witnessed anything like that in real life.” P16.

Participants emphasized that both traditional media and social media conveyed messages supporting the belief in love at first sight to a similar extent; however, they perceived the modes of presentation to differ. In traditional media, such as television series, films, and entertainment shows, this belief was portrayed explicitly through fictional narratives. In contrast, social media was perceived as conveying the same belief more indirectly through video comments, as well as through influencers’ personal stories and self-disclosure videos. At the same time, participants noted that some influencers shared experiences and opinions that challenged the belief in love at first sight. According to participants, exposure to these alternative perspectives also provided opportunities for indirect learning and encouraged individuals to consider different interpretations of romantic relationships.

“Many people claim they fell in love at first sight in the comment sections under social media posts. I think this concept is very common in books. In TV series, relationships often start with love at first sight but then move into more realistic dynamics—usually involving different partners later on.” P18. Similarly, some participants emphasized that exposure to diverse relational experiences on social media weakened their belief in the idea of instant and lasting romantic connection: “The variety of experiences shared on social media suggests that love at first sight is not always the case.” P4. On the other hand, a smaller group of participants expressed partial belief in the concept, while still emphasizing the importance of time and mutual understanding: “I believe in love at first sight to some extent, but I think it takes time to understand better. If these are mutual, it is not enough for one party to feel like they have the opportunity to live.” P15.

The findings suggest that although both traditional and social media contribute to the cultivation of romantic beliefs, participants perceive meaningful differences in the ways these media shape relationship expectations. While both media were described as reinforcing beliefs such as idealization and love at first sight, social media was perceived as exerting a stronger influence, particularly with regard to idealized relationship standards. Participants attributed this influence to the platform's emphasis on carefully curated relationship portrayals, influencers' relationship advice, and personal narratives, all of which present ideal relationships as realistic and attainable. Social media was also perceived as reinforcing traditional gender roles by portraying women as passive recipients of romantic gestures and material expressions of love. Importantly, participants also reported encountering influencers who challenged the notion of love at first sight by sharing alternative relationship experiences. This finding suggests that, unlike traditional media, social media exposes individuals to both reinforcing and counter-narratives, allowing users to negotiate competing perspectives on romantic relationships. Taken together, these findings indicate that media cultivation in contemporary digital environments is more complex than the transmission of a single, dominant romantic narrative. Rather, romantic beliefs appear to be shaped through repeated exposure to both idealized and contradictory relationship messages that are embedded within broader gendered and cultural contexts.

Discussion

This study suggests that participants predominantly viewed romanticism as an important aspect of romantic relationships but also emphasized the negative consequences of unrealistic romantic expectations, recognizing that media portrayals of romance can contribute to the development of unrealistic relationship expectations. In this study, participants underlined the influence of media on their expectations and romantic ideals, which may lead to conflict or dissatisfaction in relationships. In this study, most participants reported that their romantic beliefs negatively shaped their relationship expectations, often resulting in dissatisfaction [19, 21]. Liebers [66] suggests that unmet romantic needs contribute to stronger romantic parasocial interactions with media characters. In line with cultivation theory, continuous exposure to romantic media can influence how individuals perceive real-life relationships [38]. For example, studies find that consumption of romantic films and television programming correlates with stronger endorsement of idealistic beliefs such as “love conquers all” and elevated intimacy expectations [41, 43].

Rios et al. [44] pointed out the influence of popular media on cultivating relationship ideals and destiny beliefs in romantic relationships. The most frequently endorsed belief in the current study was the “one and only” belief, with some participants linking it to religious and spiritual values. Previous studies likewise emphasize the association between attachment processes, religiosity, and romantic love [67] Glick et al. [68] highlights the potential role of religious beliefs as related with honor in shaping social attitudes and gender roles in Turkish women. Mahoney et al. [69] argued that religion and spirituality may exert complex influences on the formation, maintenance, and dissolution of romantic relationships, and that perceiving a relationship as sacred may strengthen partners' commitment to the relationship. Additionally, some participants endorsed the belief that love would “conquer all,” although some rejected this notion—particularly when referencing violence. This tension reflects findings from violence awareness research in Türkiye, where romantic love myths are identified as potential risk factors for tolerating abusive dynamics [22, 24].

Findings indicate that participants’ perceptions of romanticism are shaped by their own experiences but also their gender role expectations, and social influences. Some participants viewed perceived material romantic behaviors such as gifts and surprises primarily as a male responsibility as reflecting traditional gender norms. Similarly, Wu et al. [70] underlined traditional gender norms in college students, such as men paying the full bill on first dates. In a similar vein, Sakallı Uğurlu et al. [71] indicated that, within the Turkish cultural context, undergraduate students expect men to display assertive behaviors in romantic relationships. They also reported that these expectations can also be understood in light of benevolent sexism, which portrays unmarried women as deserving of protection, care, and affection because of gender stereotypes regarding the "fragility" of women in Turkish culture. In addition, a study conducted across nine countries, including Türkiye, found that women who endorse traditional gender ideologies tend to prefer men with greater financial resources when choosing a partner [72].

Turkish sociocultural context may have additional contributions to participants’ perceptions on romantic intimacy [57, 73]. In collectivist cultures, individuals cannot completely detach themselves from their collective identity. In this context, social media can become a space where women both create their own spheres of privacy and resist prevailing societal expectations [15]. Hjorth and Lim [74] argue that mobile media reconfigure romantic and social intimacy, blur the boundaries between the public and private spheres, and that this transformation is not independent of cultural norms and gender relations. Taken together, these findings highlight that romantic beliefs are both theoretically grounded and practically significant in terms of media influence and cultural norms in shaping individuals’ relational experiences.

In the present study, participants reported lower levels of endorsement for the romantic beliefs that love at first sight exists and love can overcome all obstacles compared with other romantic beliefs. This finding may be interpreted in relation to the normative expectations surrounding romantic relationships in collectivist cultures. Feybesse et al. [75] argue that collectivist or family-oriented societies tend to disapprove of Ludus (playful love) and the emotionally intense attitudes associated with Mania, which may contribute to inconsistencies and variations in participants' responses to measures of romantic beliefs. Similarly, Labor [76] demonstrated that Filipino young adults strategically construct their self-presentations on dating applications not only according to individual preferences but also in response to social expectations and cultural norms.

In their comparative study, Liu et al. [77] found that Chinese and Chilean participants, representing more collectivist cultural contexts, were more likely to understand and experience love within the framework of social relationships and interpersonal connectedness rather than as an individual experience. As consistent with this study, Jiao et al. [78] reported that romantic attitudes among Asian emerging adults are shaped by the interplay of parental influence, family expectations, culture-specific values and psychological factors. Cross and Joo [55] argue that cultural models are constructed from childhood through stories, traditions, and media. These findings suggest that women's romantic beliefs in collectivist societies are shaped not only by their own experiences but also by the interaction of family-oriented values, social-cultural factors and media.

Social media and traditional media influence emerged as central mechanisms in the formation and reinforcement of romantic beliefs of women in this study. Consistently, Pezoldt et al. [50] underlined gendered narrative presentations in romantic media. Romantic love is widely cultivated in television as the most culturally dominant form of love [34]. Participants reported that Turkish television series frequently promote narratives such as “love at first sight” and “love conquers all,” while social media intensifies idealization through curated portrayals of intimacy. This aligns with evidence suggesting that media has a strong influence on romantic belief formation, Kracht and Powell’s [20] emphasis on the persuasive power of romantic media. Osborn [34] further notes that romantic media consumption is associated with lower marital commitment and more favorable perceptions of alternatives. Thus, dysfunctional romantic beliefs may reflect cultivated perceptions of relational “reality” rather than purely individual distortions.

These findings can be theoretically situated within cultivation theory [38]. From a cultivation perspective, repeated exposure to dramatized romantic portrayals may normalize idealized relational expectations and integrate them into viewers’ cognitive schemas. Contemporary cultivation research suggests that such processes now operate within fragmented digital ecosystems as well [46, 47]. Mechanisms such as mainstreaming may homogenize relational expectations across individuals, while resonance may amplify effects when media portrayals align with pre-existing cultural norms in Türkiye. Turkish young adults who do not receive sufficient support from their families may be more likely than their Western counterparts to rely on traditional cultural norms and societal expectations (such as traditional gender roles) to make sense of their romantic relationships [79]. Although Turkish emerging adults show similarities to their Western counterparts in romantic intimacy, romantic relationships in Türkiye are shaped by family values, limited openness about sexuality, and traditional cultural values [79].

The present findings suggest that media-promoted romantic beliefs were not passively accepted by Turkish women. Rather, participants appeared to actively interpret, negotiate, and critically evaluate these messages. The gendered dimensions of these beliefs are particularly salient. Feminist relationship theory conceptualizes intimate relationships as embedded within patriarchal and gendered social structures [80]. Participants’ narratives reflected internalized gendered expectations. Moreover, media representations frequently reinforce heteronormative scripts [81]. Rather than presenting a universal understanding of romance, social media integrates each culture's patriarchal, religious, and collectivist values into its digital infrastructure [15]. The media can shape individuals' expectations of romantic relationships by reproducing idealized representations of femininity and masculinity. Yang [82] examined Chinese chick flicks targeting middle-class women and demonstrated that Western postfeminist discourses are reinterpreted through local cultural values and represented in line with emerging gender norms. Digital representations on social media do not portray genuine empowerment for women,rather, they reinforce existing gender inequalities [83]. On the other hand, Zimmermann et al. [84] suggest that female influencers sometimes intentionally share their negative experiences as a strategy for followers. From a feminist perspective, Scharff [85] argues that the distinction between social media influencers and activists is shaped by feminized and exclusionary cultures of perfection. The diversity of women-centered trends on social media is particularly noteworthy. For instance, the tradwife movement illustrates that social media is not merely a platform for promoting aesthetic or lifestyle trends but also a medium through which ideological messages with power dynamics in romantic relationships [86]. The Turkish sociocultural context further intensifies these dynamics, as Turkish media often portray romantic narratives through traditional gender roles [87].

Consequently, romantic belief formation among women cannot be fully understood without examining the interplay between media exposure and culturally specific gender norms [51, 81]. idealized relationship portrayals on social networking sites reinforce individuals’ relational standards, and participants reported lower relationship satisfaction due to comparisons with others [48]. Overall, these findings highlight the potential role of media exposure in shaping individuals’ romantic relationship beliefs and suggest that idealized portrayals of romance may contribute to the development and reinforcement of unrealistic expectations about romantic relationships.

Conclusions

As highlighted in this study, dysfunctional romantic beliefs are prevalent among women undergraduate students and are heavily promoted by traditional media and social media. Gender roles played a significant role in shaping romantic expectations, and the influence of spiritual and religious belief systems (particularly concerning the "one and only" belief) was evident in the findings. Future research could explore romantic beliefs more explicitly through the lens of gender roles.

The findings further indicate the importance of preventive and protective mental health interventions. The findings of this study have practical implications for several groups. Media literacy programs could incorporate content that fosters critical thinking on romantic narratives in universities. As suggested, offering relationship education in academic settings could help guide young people in cultivating healthy romantic relationships [88, 89]. Such interventions should go beyond general digital literacy skills by supporting young adults in critically evaluating algorithmically curated content, recognizing unrealistic portrayals of romantic relationships and gender stereotypes, and incorporating empowering components that help them identify psychological violence against women reproduced through the media and develop resilience against it. Mental health practitioners may use the insights or media literacy programs to guide young adults’ romantic beliefs. Policymakers may consider regulations for responsible media representations of romantic relationships. Early media literacy education could play a crucial role in this regard.

Some limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the study relied on a small and homogeneous sample of heterosexual Turkish women undergraduates. Romantic relationship experiences, gender norms, and the meaning attributed to love may differ across sexual orientations and gender identities. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting heterosexual women's experiences rather than romantic belief formation more broadly. Future research should examine how media contributes to romantic belief formation across LGBTQ + individuals. Future research should include male and more diverse samples considering different relationship contexts. Second, this study did not systematically compare perceptions across different media genres and media consumption patterns. The interview protocol invited broad reflections rather than genre-based evaluations and examined the internalization of media-constructed romantic ideals rather than differential exposure levels. Future research could employ comparative designs to examine whether distinct media formats—such as television dramas, romantic comedies, or influencer-based social media content— and media consumption patterns differentially cultivate dysfunctional romantic beliefs. An important limitation of this study is that participants’ relationship histories (never, current, past) were not examined as a potential moderator. Although preliminary patterns indicated that participants with no prior romantic relationship experience may exhibit higher levels of romantic idealization, future studies with larger samples should examine whether relationship history moderates romantic beliefs. In future studies, as Cultivation Theory emphasizes the cumulative effects of long-term media exposure, longitudinal studies could provide stronger evidence regarding the development of romantic beliefs considering the digital media. Additionally, cross-cultural comparisons may further illuminate how media effects interact with culturally specific gender norms.
