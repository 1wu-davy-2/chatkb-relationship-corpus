ABSTRACT
AbstractNot surprisingly, incarceration's extreme separation and stress have significant negative effects on romantic relationships. Unfortunately, few programs have been developed to improve jailed individuals' romantic relationship with their non‐incarcerated partner. The present study investigated the effectiveness of the individual version of the digital OurRelationship program for incarcerated individuals. The current study is a program evaluation of services provided by PayTel Inc. (a provider of eLearning and communication devices in US jails) between June 2020 and November 2021. Of the 5411 individuals in a romantic relationship who started the program, 3034 completed it. Following completion of the program, 78% reported feeling “Mostly” or “Very Satisfied” with the program, and 77% reported “Slight” to “Strong” agreement that their relationship benefited from the program. Individuals' relationship confidence (d = 0.36) and relationship knowledge (d = 0.33) significantly improved during the program. Women and Latino/Hispanic and Asian/PI individuals experienced the largest pre‐post gains in relationship functioning. Furthermore, racial/ethnic minority groups tended to report higher satisfaction with the program. Overall, the high rates of program satisfaction and significant pre‐post changes—combined with their reduced barriers to dissemination—support the delivery of digital relationship programs for jailed individuals interested in strengthening their relationship.

BODY
Approximately one in three couples experiences relationship distress at any given time (Whisman et al., 2008), suggesting the importance of addressing relationship discord and dissolution on a population level. Healthy romantic relationships have important positive effects on individual mental health, increased work fulfillment, sexual satisfaction, and overall life satisfaction (Glenn & Weaver, 1981; Heller et al., 2004). A meta‐analysis examining the link between relationship quality and individual well‐being found that those in healthy, happy relationships tend to experience better and more consistent levels of individual well‐being (Proulx et al., 2007). Conversely, the impact that relationship distress can have on individuals or partners in romantic relationships is far‐reaching and often a primary reason for seeking mental health services (Foran et al., 2015). Relationship distress can significantly affect individuals' mental and physical well‐being (Whisman, 2007). Furthermore, results from randomized controlled trials of relationship interventions (e.g., Doss et al., 2016) have demonstrated the causal effects of relationship functioning on mental and physical health.

Relationship distress can be caused by a plethora of external and contextual factors, ranging from work and financial stress to nationwide pandemics and incarceration (Nguyen et al., 2017). The high rate of incarceration and innumerable romantic relationships that are affected by the challenges of jail have contributed to the increase in the number of relationship distress and dissolution in the United States. Although incarceration rates decreased during COVID, 358 per 100,000 United States residents were sentenced to over 1 year in state or federal prison in 2020, and over 1.2 million individuals were in state and federal prison (Carson, 2021). Roughly 1% of the male population will likely become incarcerated at some point during their lifetime, with substantially higher rates among minority and low‐income populations. These high rates of incarceration create sizable impacts on health, parenting, and romantic relationships.

Effects of incarceration

A recent nationwide sample found that high rates of incarceration are associated with higher rates of morbidity and mortality (Weidner & Schultz, 2019). Both the initial acute stress of incarceration, as well as the chronic stress of continued imprisonment, lead to subsequent negative mental health outcomes (McEwen & Stellar, 1993). Furthermore, incarceration negatively impacts numerous social and human capital factors, such as work experience, job opportunity, and relationships with family and peers (Huebner, 2005). These consequences trickle down to their larger family units, creating disruptions in their romantic relationships and children.

Incarceration, regardless of duration, is a major stressor that can destabilize a family unit and damage ongoing family dynamics (Comfort et al., 2016; DeHart et al., 2018; Turney, 2015). Between 50% and 60% of incarcerated individuals have children under the age of 18 (Glaze, 2010), and roughly 7% of US children experience the incarceration of at least one of their parents (Muentner et al., 2021). These negative outcomes compound the effects on the incarcerated individual and further strain both the co‐parenting and romantic relationships of parents. Indeed, fathers' incarceration increases a mother's odds of developing depression and experiencing lower life satisfaction (Wildeman et al., 2012; Wildeman et al., 2019). Given the far‐reaching consequences associated with incarceration—to the incarcerated individual, their family members, and society as a whole—incarceration is a major and pressing public health concern.

Unsurprisingly, incarceration also has long‐lasting and significant negative effects on romantic relationships (Turney & Wildeman, 2012) and increases their risk for divorce (Apel et al., 2010). Positive romantic relationships, on the other hand, can help inmates feel hope and improve their own emotional state during imprisonment (Granot & Einat, 2022). Barriers such as physical distance, inability to work through problems, and difficulty in developing shared behaviors challenge the success of the relationship (De Claire et al., 2020). During most incarcerations, couples are only permitted to have minimal physical contact with each other. Because physical contact is a significant element of most romantic relationships (Butzer & Campbell, 2008; Fisher & McNulty, 2008), not having the ability to be physically and sexually intimate creates additional hardships within the relationship (Harman et al., 2007). The inflexibility of visitation and overall minimal communication between partners further strains the ability to maintain the relationship (Comfort, 2008). Unsurprisingly, inmates with a partner inside the prison have higher levels of global, environmental, and psychological quality of life (Carcedo et al., 2011).

In addition to physical barriers, incarceration can create impenetrable emotional barriers. Incarcerated individuals tend to resort to avoidant communication strategies and become emotionally withdrawn from their partners on the outside (Harman et al., 2007). Moreover, some inmates believe that their romantic relationships are a weakness that can be exploited, and thus distance themselves from their partner (Haney, 2001). These emotional and physical barriers put great strain on the relationship.

The effects of incarceration on romantic relationships persist long after the period of incarceration. Indeed, incarceration greatly increases the odds of separation and divorce (Siennick et al., 2014), with differences inconsistently found across gender and racial groups. While some studies have not found any effect of gender and race on incarceration on relationship dissolution, others indicate that these demographic characteristics may in fact be quite meaningful (Widdowson et al., 2019; Wildeman & Turney, 2014). Race and gender, however, do seem to impact future relationships. Prior literature demonstrates differences by race and gender in how incarcerated men are viewed by non‐incarcerated women (Webster et al., 1995). However, given the varying research regarding demographic impact on incarceration and relationship dissolution, future research is needed to better understand the specific role that gender and race have on relationship quality, marriage, and dissolution.

Relationship interventions during incarceration

The majority of interpersonal interventions designed to be delivered during or following incarceration solely focus on parenting and family functioning. To date, only a handful of studies have examined interventions for romantic relationships, with even fewer reporting on individual and relationship program effects post completion of the program.

There have been two main relationship education programs that have been implemented and researched in jail settings: the Relationship Enhancement Program and the Prevention and Relationship Enhancement program. The Relationship Enhancement Program, one of the first programs implemented in prisons, resulted in significant pre‐post (but not long‐term) effects on relationship quality (Accordino & Guerney, 1998). The effective Prevention and Relationship Enhancement Program (PREP) was adapted to “PREP Inside and Out” and implemented in jails across Oklahoma in two formats: (1) a couple's class where both partners (including the non‐incarcerated partner) learned couples' skills together, (2) and an individual class for those who either could not meet during visitation, or for individuals who were not in a serious relationship but wanted to learn tools to better their current or future relationships (Einhorn et al., 2008). Results using a self‐report assessment completed prior and immediately after the program indicated that incarcerated individuals reported increased relationship satisfaction, communication skills, and confidence about their relationship regardless of gender and/or race.

The Creating Lasting Family Connections Marriage Enhancement Program is delivered after individuals are released from incarceration and was assessed using structured surveys given pretest, posttest, and 3 months later. It focuses on improving effective communication, conflict resolution, positive parenting techniques, and education on marriage enrichment (Shamblen et al., 2012). Compared to a control condition, couples in the intervention group experienced significant benefits in communication skills, conflict resolution, emotional awareness, and inter‐personal skills; however, there were no differences reported in relationship commitment between the two groups. These findings not only exemplify the many benefits of relationship programs but also highlight the unique challenges of improving relationship commitment and long‐term satisfaction within relationship help programs. Furthermore, this program relies on a group format to implement the skills, as well as the availability of trained leaders to deliver the intervention.

Unfortunately, there are several challenges to delivery or implementation with these interventions. First, jails and prisons are often understaffed and do not have educators available to deliver the interventions. Second, the nature of incarceration itself makes program delivery difficult. Incarcerated individuals are only permitted limited and sporadic contact with their partners. Thus, previous programs have been designed to be administered during limited visiting hours (while one partner is in jail) or designed for the couple once the jailed partner is released. Given that incarcerated individuals typically have copious free time—and that individual instruction is common—the addition of an individual, digital, relationship help program may reduce many barriers to delivery.

The OurRelationship program is a web‐based adaptation of Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy that helps couples identify, understand, and solve a relationship issue (Doss et al., 2013). In previous studies, it has increased relationship satisfaction (d = 0.69), relationship confidence (d = 0.47), and overall individual functioning (Doss et al., 2016). In addition to the ease of delivery as reflected by its entirely online and self‐guided structure, the OurRelationship program is also unique in that it can be adapted for different populations, types of relationships, and specific relationship issues. Notably, the OurRelationship program has been shown to be effective in two separate samples of low‐income couples (Doss et al., 2020; Hatch et al., 2021) and tailored for delivery to these populations.

The OurRelationship program has also been adapted to enable an individual (rather than a couple) to complete the program (Nowlan et al., 2017), further increasing its applicability for jails and prisons. The individual version (OR‐I) has a similar structure and content as the couples' version of the program with two notable differences. First, rather than having structured conversations with the partner, the individual is supported in writing a letter to the partner to share their DEEP understandings and desired changes. Second, rather than providing feedback based on the partner's actual responses, feedback is based on the user's perceptions of how their partner would respond. The individual version is designed for couples in which one partner is unable or unwilling to complete the program; however, it has been used in some settings for individuals not currently in a relationship. Individuals who completed the individual version, relative to a waitlist control group, reported significant benefits in quality of life (d = 0.69), perceived health (d = 0.49), and work functioning (d = 0.44); the groups did not significantly differ in their gains in relationship satisfaction or confidence. However, all participants in this study were first encouraged to complete the couples' version of the program; thus, it is possible that participants completed the individual version because their partners refused to participate, thus skewing the results. Therefore, the program may be more effective in improving relationship functioning when delivered to individuals who have more committed partners.

The current study sought to examine the effects of the standalone individual version of the OurRelationship program for individuals currently in jails. In the individual program, participants report on how they would expect their partner to respond on measures of personality, stress, and other constructs (rather than seeing their partner's responses, as in the couples' version). Additionally, in the current study, the individual program was delivered without supplemental coach calls to reduce barriers to, and cost of, program delivery. Most, but not all (Rothman et al., 2019) studies of the couples version have included a coach.

Given the physical separation between inmates and their partners during this period, we did not expect the program to have immediate effects on relationship satisfaction; however, this intervention can provide individuals with tools and skills to better understand their relationship. Furthermore, if this brief intervention is successful in that goal, it could make them more confident about their ability to solve important problems in their relationship.

Specifically, the study examined (1) satisfaction with the OurRelationship program, (2) perceived relationship improvements following completion of the program, (3) changes in relationship confidence and relationship knowledge during the program, and (4) and the impact of demographic characteristics on the outcomes of any of the previous aims.

METHOD

This study was conducted as part of a program evaluation of the OurRelationship program conducted by PayTel Inc.—a provider of eLearning and communication devices in US jails. The program evaluation was conducted from June 2020 to November 2021. The use of data from this program evaluation was evaluated by the (redacted) IRB and determined not to be human subjects research because the data were deidentified by PayTel before being provided to the researchers. Additionally, the current study and its methods were preregistered and accepted with the Open Science Framework (osf.io/gp7bd/).

Participants

There were no inclusion or exclusion criteria for participants to receive the program. All participants were incarcerated in jails across the United States. The current study focuses on a total of 3034 individuals who at the time of assessment were (1) currently in a romantic relationship, (2) completed the entirety of the program, and (3) were given access to complete the pre‐ and post‐program assessments. Individuals who started the program, but did not complete it, were unable to complete the post‐program assessment. Participants were predominantly male (58%), White, non‐Hispanic (45%), and in their mid‐30s (M = 36). At the start of the program, 21% of participants were married, 20% were engaged, 26% were living with their partner (prior to being incarcerated), and 32% were dating their partner but not living together.

Some of the demographic characteristics reported in this study are atypical for the general population demographics reported on in jail settings. Specifically, the Federal Bureau of Prisons reports that 93% of incarcerated individuals are men, with women accounting for only 6.9% of the population (Federal Bureau of Prisons‐Gender). The greater percentage of women in this study (33%) compared to the general population may reflect differences in gender for help‐seeking behaviors. Indeed, research on the OurRelationship program (Barton et al., 2020), as well on help‐seeking behaviors for individuals in couple therapy (Doss et al., 2003), shows that women are more likely to find and start a relationship intervention program than men. Generally, race and ethnicity were comparable to general demographics for this population; however, fewer Black individuals participated in the study—25.4% versus a general population of 38.5% (Federal Bureau of Prisons‐Race)—implying that Black individuals may be less likely to enroll in the program or that there were institutional‐based differences that limited their opportunity to use the program.

Missing data

While 5411 individuals in a romantic relationship initially signed up and started the program, 3034 individuals (56%) completed the entirety of the program. The remainder did not complete the program for a variety of reasons (i.e., release, transfer to a prison, or disinterest in the program). Procedure

The OurRelationship program for individuals was offered to provide jailed individuals education on romantic relationships. All participants had access to tablets in which they were able to access and complete an adapted version of the individual version of the OurRelationship program designed to function without an internet connection. PayTel was able to track participants across sections using identification numbers that participants had to enter each time they logged into their allotted tablet; the results were then de‐identified by PayTel. Individuals were not compensated or rewarded for completing the program.

Description of the OurRelationship—individual intervention

The OurRelationship individual program is an evidence‐based online relationship program adapted from Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy (IBCT). Activities typically contained videos, slides with text, animations, and questions in text, drop‐down or multiple‐choice format. In the first phase, the Observe phase, individuals identify a “core issue” or challenge in their relationship. In the Understand phase, they develop a more objective and complete understanding of the core issue, learning about the Differences, External Stressors, Emotions, and Patters of Communication that are affecting their core issue. Finally, in the Respond phase, individuals develop behavioral changes to ameliorate the core issue. More information about the Individual version of the OurRelationship program is available in Nowlan et al. (2017).

Measures

Measures were collected at two different time points, before participating in the program (baseline), and immediately after completing the program (post‐program survey). As part of the program evaluation, post‐program measures were only administered to individuals who completed the program.

Demographic information

Individuals completed single item reports containing questions about age, gender, race/ ethnicity, and relationship status. Gender was assessed using the following measure—“What is your gender?”—with options of either “Male,” “Female,” or “Other.” To assess relationship status, individuals were asked “What is your CURRENT relationship status?” The following responses were available to choose from: single, broken up, dating = 6 months, living together = 6 months, engaged, and married.

Relationship confidence

Relationship confidence was measured using a one‐item question from the 10‐item Confidence Scale: “I believe my partner and I can successfully handle conflicts that come up around the biggest problem in our relationship” (Stanley et al., 1994). Response options were assessed using a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Relationship knowledge

Relationship knowledge was measured using the following item: “I know what I can do to stop a fight (even if I have trouble doing it in the moment).” This one‐item measure was created for the purposes of the program evaluation. Response options were assessed using a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

Program satisfaction

Upon completion of the program, participants were asked to report on their experience with the program. Using a one‐item measure taken from the 8‐item Client Evaluation of Services Questionnaire (Nguyen et al., 1983), participants responded to the question: “Overall, how satisfied are you with the OurRelationship program?” Response options were assessed using a 7‐point Likert scale ranging from very unsatisfied to very satisfied.

Perceived program benefits

At the end of the program, participants were asked to rate how much they agreed with four statements regarding perceived improvements in their relationship following completion of the program: “As a result of doing the OurRelationship program: (a) I'm more confident my partner and I can talk about things constructively; (b) I'm more confident that I know how to improve my relationship; (c) I will invest more time in our relationship; and (d) I will work more with my partner as part of a team.” Participants rated the four items from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7), with higher scores indicating greater levels of perceived program benefits to their relationship. Participant's responses were combined to create a composite score. Internal consistency across the samples and assessments was excellent (α = 0.94).

Data analyses

Analyses were conducted using linear regression. Effect sizes were calculated using the “paired” version of the cohensD function in lsr (https://rdrr.io/cran/lsr/man/cohensD.html). Because White (45%) and male (58%) were the most commonly endorsed categories, all models used Race = White or Gender = Male as the reference group to increase stability of estimates.

RESULTS

Individual satisfaction with the OurRelationship program

Among those who completed the program, 78% reported that they were “Very” or “Mostly” satisfied with the program, and 22% said they were “Mostly” or “Very” unsatisfied. The remaining responses fell between “Somewhat Unsatisfied” and “Somewhat Satisfied.” Overall, participants evaluated their satisfaction with the OurRelationship program positively (M = 2.36, SD = 0.98). A one sample t‐test indicated that program satisfaction was significantly greater than a neutral score of 1.5 (t(3033) = 48.38, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 0.88).

Perceived program benefits to relationship

Analyses revealed that 72% of participants agreed that there were perceived program benefits to their relationship, 16% reported feeling neutral, and 12% disagreed. Indeed, the average participant evaluated perceived programs benefits positively (M = 5.64, SD = 1.63). A one sample t‐test showed that perceived program benefits were significantly greater than neutral (t(3033) = 55.52, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 1.01).

Program gains in relationship confidence and relationship knowledge

Participants reported significantly greater relationship confidence (M = 5.52, SD = 1.74) post‐program than pre‐program (M = 4.76, SD = 1.89; t (3033) = 20.04, p < 0.001, within‐group Cohen's d = 0.36). Participants also reported higher levels of relationship knowledge (M = 5.67, SD = 1.65) after completing the program (M = 5.04, SD = 1.75; t (3033) = 17.77, p < 0.001, within‐group Cohen's d = 0.32). Post‐program ratings

Analyses revealed that program satisfaction and perceived program benefits significantly varied by race/ethnicity and gender (but not age). Specifically, “Latino/Hispanic” individuals rated their program satisfaction significantly higher than “White” individuals (b = 0.43 SE = 0.06, p < 0.001; d = 0.45). Additionally, participants who identified as “Asian / PI” reported higher program satisfaction than “White” participants (b = 0.49, SE = 0.08, p < 0.001; d = 0.52). There was no significant difference in program satisfaction between men and women. However, gender‐Other individuals rated their satisfaction with the program significantly higher than male individuals (b = 0.56, SE = 0.08, p < 0.001; d = 0.57).

Similarly, perceived program benefits varied by both race/ethnicity and gender. Individuals who identified as “Latino/Hispanic” reported significantly larger program benefits to their relationship compared to White individuals (b = 1.17, SE = 0.10, p < 0.001; d = 0.79), as did individuals who identify as “Asian/PI” (b = 1.60, SE = 0.12, p < 0.001; d = 1.10), Black (b = −0.23, SE = 0.07, p = 0.00; d = 0.15), and race‐Other (b = −0.66, SE = 0.14, p < 0.001; d = 0.45). Compared to male individuals, women (b = 0.17, SE = 0.06, p = 0.01; d = 0.11) and those who identified as gender‐Other (b = 1.53, SE = 0.13, p < 0.001; d = 0.98) reported significantly larger perceived program benefits.

DISCUSSION

Findings from this study demonstrated that an online, individual‐based, relationship help program can serve as an effective resource for individuals in jail. Despite not having access to some of the feedback and conditional functionality that permits tailoring of program content based on their previous responses (because the tablets did not have internet access), 78% of jailed individuals reported being mostly or very satisfied with the services received. This result is slightly lower than reports of program satisfaction found in a nationwide sample using the OR‐I program (97%; Nowlan et al., 2017) and those found in our studies of the OurRelationship couple program in a lower‐income sample (93%; Doss et al., 2020). Therefore, while the OR‐I is not currently being used in jail settings across the United States, it could function as a reliable and acceptable relationship intervention for incarcerated individuals.

As hypothesized, individuals who completed the program reported that, as a result of doing OR‐I, they were more confident in their general communication with their partner, have the tools and skills to improve their relationship, plan to invest more time in their relationship, and hope to work together with their partner as a team. To date, studies examining the impact of incarceration on individuals have found detrimental and long‐lasting negative effects on romantic relationships. In fact, jail settings have been found to negatively impact an individual's coping mechanisms, thus making a return to normal life and relationships extremely challenging (Haney, 2001). However, incarcerated individuals who are able to maintain their relationship satisfaction and quality tend to do better over time (Granot & Einat, 2022). Therefore, although this study did not have access to longitudinal relationship quality post incarceration, the findings support the positive influence of a relationship help program on individuals preparing to re‐enter society and their romantic relationships.

Compared to a nationally representative sample of individuals who completed the OR‐I (Nowlan et al., 2017) and did not experience changes in relationship confidence (within‐group d = −0.04), jailed individuals were more likely to report improvements in relationship confidence post‐program completion (d = 0.36). There are two potential reasons for the larger changes in the present sample. First, in the Nowlan et al. study, individuals initially signed up for the couple version of the OurRelationship program but their partners were unable or unwilling to participate. As such, their partner's lack of involvement may indicate less commitment to the relationship or lack of willingness to improve it. Second, the Nowlan et al. study collected post‐intervention assessments from all participants (including those who did not complete the program), likely decreasing the overall effect size estimates in that study relative to the current study.

In contrast, there were smaller‐sized effects on relationship confidence in the current sample (d = 0.36) compared to those found in the couple version of the program (Men: d = 0.62, Women: d = 0.70; Doss et al., 2016). The greater magnitude of change for individuals in the OR couples program is unsurprising given that individuals who want to improve their relationship often find it more challenging to do so when they don't have the involvement of their partner. Thus, relationship confidence—or the confidence to successfully handle conflicts that arise around core relationship issues—likely would increase more when both partners are engaged in improving their overall relationship functioning. The limited interaction that incarcerated individuals had with their partner also made it challenging to apply their newly learned skills and relationship understanding. More interaction may be possible in a less restrictive jail setting, allowing for more in‐person communication or videoconferences between incarcerated individuals and their romantic partner. Participants also reported significant increases in relationship knowledge during OR‐I, similar to participants in both the Relationship Enhancement Program and Creating Lasting Family Connections Marriage Enhancement Program.

Gender and race/ethnicity differences

To date, there is minimal research on potential racial and ethnic differences in effectiveness of relationship‐intervention programs. Doss and colleagues (2020) did not find any gender differences in the couple version of OR's effects on relationship outcomes in a study of low‐income couples, nor did Hatch et al. in their replication study (Hatch et al., 2021). Furthermore, Rothman and colleagues found that neither race nor ethnicity moderate relationship‐intervention outcomes in OR‐C (Rothman et al., 2021).

Descriptive findings from the current study indicate that there are baseline differences in relationship confidence and knowledge between individuals who identify as male, female, and other. Specifically, individuals who identify as gender‐other report lower relationship confidence and knowledge prior to beginning the program; however, they experience larger gains post completion.

Similarly, Latino/Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander, and race‐Other individuals reported lower levels of relationship confidence and knowledge at baseline, but experienced greater gains compared to White individuals. This finding may be due, in part, to statistical regression to the mean. However, the larger gains experienced by these groups are consistent with previous studies' findings that individuals/couples who start the program more distressed have more “room for gains” and experience greater relationship improvement, relative to a control group, during the program (Doss et al., 2016; Roddy et al., 2020). Although minimal, there were ethnic differences in program satisfaction and completion. Specifically, compared to White individuals, Latino/Hispanic individuals were more likely to complete the program and tended to be more satisfied with the program.

Limitations and conclusions

This study was the first of its kind to investigate the role of a digital relationship program for jailed individuals. The results indicate that an online‐based intervention for this population can be effective in improving specific relationship domains and is a suitable intervention as evidenced by the completion rates and program satisfaction ratings. While these results are important, a number of limitations must be considered.

The exclusion of a control group poses the question of whether the OurRelationship program was the driver of change for the observed improvements in relationship confidence and knowledge; however, the previous study of OR‐I conducted by Nowlan and colleagues did include a waitlist group (Nowlan et al., 2017). In that study, control couples experienced non‐significant deterioration in relationship functioning, suggesting that naturalistic change may not explain gains observed in the present study. However, it is also possible that individuals in the two samples would experience different rates of naturalistic change.

Further, because these data were collected as part of a program evaluation, there were many limitations regarding accessibility and study design. Most importantly, the post‐intervention assessment was only administered to people who completed the program. Additionally, the lack of flexibility made it impossible to conduct follow‐up assessments, thus impacting the ability to test maintenance of gains across relationship outcomes. Nonetheless, prior evidence suggests that improvements in relationship functioning post completion of the OurRelationship program do indeed persist over time (Roddy et al., 2020).

A final limitation of the current study was that it was impossible to disentangle individual from jail/procedural sources of variance in the outcomes of interest in the present study. The limited knowledge—especially regarding which jail each participant was in, racial and gender demographics within each jail, and information about institutional procedures or tablet usage—may be clouding some of the study conclusions.

Future program revisions for this population may wish to include cultural and gender‐specific content to increase demographic representation. Indeed, the original couple version of the OurRelationship program has since been adapted for many different populations, including same‐gender and military couples. Such programs offer more tailored content and increase representation of minority groups. Tailoring content specific to experiences and relationship issues most common for incarcerated individuals, for instance, could increase overall program satisfaction and allow for greater pre‐post gains in relationship functioning.

CONCLUSION

Despite these limitations, the preliminary results from the current study demonstrate the promising benefits of the OurRelationship program for jailed individuals. An initial goal of the program evaluation was to determine the impact of the OurRelationship program for jailed individuals; results suggest that the program is successful in improving relationship functioning for the average participant. This is especially meaningful given the lack of current programs offered to jailed individuals that focus on relationship functioning pre‐ and post‐incarceration. To date, research shows that program involvement while in jail, such as completing a substance use program, can positively impact an individual's re‐entry (Bahr et al., 2012). Independently, research highlights social support as significantly improving re‐entry into community (Kjellstrand et al., 2021). Thus, in addition to providing a resource for this otherwise underserved population, the OurRelationship program can be a valuable tool to help re‐integrate incarcerated individuals into society. Ultimately, this study contributes to the burgeoning field of relationship interventions targeting jailed individuals and suggests that a digital individual program—one that can be broadly disseminated with no staff training—can serve as an effective service for this population.
