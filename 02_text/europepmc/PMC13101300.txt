ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, Gambling Disorder (GD) has gained increasing attention in psychological and neuroscientific research due to its significant behavioral, social, and economic consequences. GD shares core features with substance addictions, including craving, tolerance, and withdrawal, and is influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, neurobiological, and environmental factors. The couple's context plays a pivotal role in both the development and management of GD, as dysfunctional relational dynamics can perpetuate the disorder, leading to instability, chronic stress, financial difficulties, and even domestic violence. This systematic review aims to synthesize current evidence on the intersection between GD and couple relationships while identifying potential directions for future therapeutic and preventive interventions.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines (BMJ 372:n71, 2021), a systematic literature search was conducted using five databases (PsycInfo, PsycArticles, Scopus, PubMed, and MEDLINE).ResultsThe 30 studies included in this review examined various aspects of GD within the couple's context. Specifically, 40% of the studies focused on the effectiveness of treatments for gamblers and their impact on partners and family members, while 30% investigated correlation between violence and GD. Additionally, 13.33% analyzed the bidirectional relationship between GD and couple dynamics, both as a contributing factor and as a consequence, 13.33% explored the influence of marital status and relationship conditions on GD, and 3.33% examined the role of attachment styles in gambling behaviors.ConclusionThis review underscores the intricate interplay between GD and couple relationships. The couple dynamic plays a crucial role in both the progression and management of GD, with dysfunctional interactions exacerbating stress and instability. Addressing these relational factors in treatment approaches may improve outcomes for individuals with GD and their partners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04213-1.

BODY
Introduction

In last decades, gambling has gained increasing attention in psychological and neuroscientific research due to its significant behavioral, social, and economic implications. Gambling Disorder (GD) is classified as a behavioral addiction, akin to substance use disorders, as it develops gradually and progressively, varying in onset and manifestation based on individual differences, the type of gambling activities, and vulnerability factors [58]. Like other addictions, GD is characterized by key features such as craving, tolerance, and withdrawal [51]. Craving refers to an intense, uncontrollable urge to engage in gambling, often perceived as an overwhelming necessity that must be satisfied immediately, regardless of the consequences [24]. The dysphoria and exhilaration experienced by individuals with GD closely resemble the emotional states induced by substance use [6]. Beyond its individual psychopathological features, GD has significant social and relational consequences. In particular, gambling-related behaviors often affect intimate partnerships, exposing romantic partners to emotional distress, financial strain, interpersonal conflict, and broader harms to affected others [20, 21, 36, 47].

Current studies suggest that GD arises from a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, neurobiological mechanisms, and environmental influences [53]. Importantly, these individual vulnerabilities and protective factors unfold within a broader interpersonal and relational context, in which close relationships—particularly couple relationships—may either exacerbate or buffer the development and maintenance of gambling-related problems [20, 21, 36, 60]. Specifically, the intermittent reinforcement inherent in gambling activities has been shown to disrupt dopaminergic reward pathways, mirroring the neural alterations observed in substance addictions [10]. This multifaceted phenomenon is shaped by various risk and protective factors that influence an individual's susceptibility or resilience to gambling behaviors [6]. Key individual risk factors include impulsivity, emotional dysregulation, and co-occurring psychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety disorders ([55], 2022, [46]). Environmentally, factors like the accessibility of gambling venues and societal acceptance of gambling practices contribute to its prevalence [1]. Conversely, protective factors such as robust social support networks, strong self-regulation abilities, and effective coping strategies play a crucial role in mitigating the risk of developing GD [17].​

Couple Relationships in GD

Numerous studies have explored the relationship between GD and couple dynamics [38, 39, 56]. For instance, research indicates that romantic partners of individuals with problem gambling report increased romantic conflict, reduced relationship satisfaction, and heightened distress compared to partners of non-problem gamblers. Factors such as age, gender, presence of children, and duration of the relationship can influence perceptions of relationship quality [16].

GD adversely affects both the gambler and their partner, leading to emotional distress, financial hardships, health issues, poor communication, inadequate conflict resolution, and ineffective parenting [20, 21, 47]. The disorder often manifests in marital conflicts, emotional instability, and financial difficulties, significantly compromising relationship quality. Engagement in gambling behaviors is frequently associated with feelings of distrust, resentment, and chronic stress between partners, resulting in a gradual erosion of relationship stability [36]. In this perspective, studies have highlighted a significant association between GD and Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) [54]. A review of 14 studies found that 36.5% of individuals with problem gambling acknowledged perpetrating physical IPV, while 38.1% had been subjected to physical IPV. Partners of individuals with GD often experience economic coercion, emotional neglect, and social isolation, which can amplify situations of abuse [60]. Anxiety, depression, and emotional distress tend to be higher in partners of individuals with GD, increasing the risk of dysfunctional relationship dynamics [20, 21]. Regarding treatment, integrating family and group support interventions,, such as Gamblers Anonymous, is considered crucial for maintaining abstinence and providing psychological support to the patient [33]. However, despite increasing recognition of the relational impact of Gambling Disorder, couple dynamics and partner involvement are still only partially and inconsistently integrated into treatment approaches [13, 20, 21, 33]. Consistently, a systematic review and meta-analysis found that treatment groups often did not demonstrate significantly greater benefits than control groups in reducing harm to affected others [13]. This suggests a need for more effective, evidence-based interventions that address the complex dynamics within relationships affected by GD.​ Despite the growing body of literature on the impact of GD on couple relationships, existing findings remain fragmented and heterogeneous in terms of study designs, populations, and outcomes assessed. This lack of integration limits the ability to draw comprehensive conclusions and to inform the development of effective couple-based interventions. A systematic synthesis of the available evidence is therefore warranted to clarify current knowledge and identify gaps in the literature.

Present study

​Existing research has explored the relationship between GD and couple dynamics, focusing on risk and protective factors that influence gambling behavior. However, a comprehensive systemic perspective integrating these findings remains absent in current literature. This systematic review aims to synthesize the available evidence on the association between GD and couple relationships.Specifically, the review focuses on relational functioning, couple dynamics, and relationship-related outcomes associated with GD, and, where available, their links to gambling-related behaviors.

Method

The systematic review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines [50](). The review protocol was not preregistered in PROSPERO or other registries. Search strategies

Database searches were conducted from 2014 to May 2024. Database searches were conducted in the following electronic databases:: PsycInfo, PsycArticles, Scopus, PubMed, and MEDLINE. The search strategy was developed using two conceptual domains—Gambling Disorder and Couple Relationship—and applied across all databases. Keywords within each domain were combined using the Boolean operator OR, and the two domains were combined using AND (see Appendix A for full search strings). For PubMed, controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH) related to the second construct were additionally used (i.e., Marriage and Family Characteristics). Searches were conducted in the Title and Abstract fields for PsycINFO, PsycArticles, PubMed, and MEDLINE, while for Scopus, searches were conducted in the Title, Abstract, and Keywords fields.

Study selection

For the purposes of this review, “couple dynamics” and “relationship functioning” were broadly defined as indicators of relational quality and processes within intimate partnerships, including relationship satisfaction, conflict, communication patterns, intimacy, attachment-related characteristics, and experiences of interpersonal violence. GD was defined according to established diagnostic frameworks (e.g., DSM-5 or ICD criteria) or validated measures of problem or pathological gambling, as reported in the included studies. All titles and abstracts were analyzed to determine the study's eligibility for inclusion. The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: (a) empirical studies investigating the relationship between GD and couple or intimate relationships; (b) studies providing original data using quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods designs; (c) assessment of gambling behavior and/or couple functioning using validated instruments, structured interviews, or qualitative methodologies; and (d) studies published in peer-reviewed journals. Qualitative studies were explicitly included to capture relational processes, lived experiences, and contextual dynamics within couple relationships affected by GD, which are not adequately addressed by quantitative designs alone, in line with the systemic-relational perspective guiding the present review. The term “instruments” refers to the measurement tools and assessment methods used in the primary studies to evaluate GD and couple-related constructs (e.g., validated gambling severity scales, relationship functioning measures, structured interviews, or qualitative methodologies), and not to instruments developed by the authors of the present review.

After conducting electronic database searches, which identified a total of 3340 articles using the initial search terms, these were reviewed according to the eligibility criteria. After removing duplicates, 2361 articles were selected and examined according to the inclusion criteria; subsequently, 155 full-text articles were selected for further examination. Following a closer review, 30 articles were deemed suitable as they met the study's inclusion criteria.

Data extraction and references

Data extraction was conducted using a structured coding protocol developed a priori to ensure consistency and replicability across studies. A coding protocol was prepared and used to extract relevant information from the selected studies, according to four categories of information: publication characteristics (authors, year); sample characteristics (sample size, age of sample subjects, identified with mean and standard deviation, and percentage of males in the sample); information on methodological characteristics (type of research design, indicated as cross-sectional or longitudinal, instruments used to measure the two main constructs); main results. The data extraction framework was systematically applied to all included studies and is described in sufficient detail to allow replication of the review process.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute [2] Critical Appraisal Tools, selecting the appropriate checklist according to study design (cross-sectional, comparative, longitudinal, or qualitative). The quality assessment was conducted only on the 30 studies included in the final synthesis and was based on the methodological information reported in the primary studies, as summarized in the data extraction tables. Key domains included study design, sample characteristics, validity and reliability of measurement instruments, and adequacy of data analysis. The quality assessment was used to inform the interpretation of findings and did not constitute an exclusion criterion. However, findings from studies with lower methodological quality were interpreted cautiously and primarily used to inform exploratory patterns rather than firm conclusions.

Results

In this systematic review, 30 studies were identified and analyzed, each examining various aspects of GDwithin the context of couple relationships. A methodological quality assessment was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute [2] Critical Appraisal Tools. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies ranged from low to moderate, and no study met criteria for high methodological quality. Common strengths included clarity of research objectives and the use of validated instruments to assess gambling-related and relational constructs. However, frequent methodological limitations were observed, including the predominance of cross-sectional designs, small or non-representative samples, reliance on self-reported measures, and limited control of confounding variables. Treatment assessment and effectiveness

Across the included studies, partner- and couple-involved interventions were generally associated with improvements in both gambling-related outcomes and relational functioning. Most studies reported reductions in gambling severity, cravings, and gambling-related expenditures, alongside improvements in couple adjustment, emotional regulation, and relational well-being. These effects were observed across different therapeutic approaches and study designs, although study designs and sample characteristics differed across the included studies. Table 2 shows the characteristics of the 12 studies that investigated the effectiveness of treatments for gamblers and their partners and/or families, and the couples functioning in the presence of a gambler within the household. The studies reviewed utilized comparative, cross-sectional, and qualitative research designs and were conducted over the past 10 years. Some studies focused on the effectiveness of therapeutic treatments for gamblers. Several studies compared couple-based interventions with individual treatments, generally reporting comparable reductions in gambling behavior but greater improvements in relational and emotional outcomes in couple-based conditions (e.g., [43, 64]) Lee et al. [44, 45] analyzed 171 individuals with GD seeking couple therapy at two addiction service centers in Alberta. Participants sought help for relational issues related to addiction and family concerns. Seventy-seven percent had participated in other treatments, many met criteria for AUD and/or GD, and 30% reported suicidal thoughts in the past year. The study reported high levels of relational distress among individuals with GD seeking couple therapy, with a substantial proportion presenting comorbid conditions and prior treatment experiences. Nilsson et al. [49] used a sample of 136 couples undergoing couple therapy and 136 gamblers receiving cognitive-behavioral therapy, aiming to compare the effectiveness of the two treatments for gamblers. Lee e Awosoga [41] considered differences between couples receiving couple therapy and those not receiving it where one partner is a gambler. Both studies showed that couple therapy or cognitive-behavioral therapy was effective in motivating gamblers to reduce gambling habits, with gambling reductions and a significant percentage of gamblers abstaining during treatment. These factors negatively influenced couple relationships and addictions. Overall, studies consistently indicated impaired couple functioning in the presence of Gambling Disorder, with partners reporting lower relationship satisfaction, poorer adjustment, and higher levels of distress compared to gamblers’ self-reports. Across studies, discrepancies were reported between gamblers’ and partners’ perceptions of relationship functioning, with partners consistently reporting lower satisfaction and higher distress.

Correlation between violence and GD

Across studies, GD was consistently associated with elevated levels of IPV, including physical, psychological, and economic abuse. Although prevalence rates varied, the direction of findings was largely consistent, indicating increased relational harm in the presence of gambling-related problems. Table 3 presents the characteristics of nine studies conducted over the past decade that examine the intersection of GD and domestic violence. These studies employ diverse methodologies, including cross-sectional designs and qualitative interviews. Notably, two studies by Dowling et al. extracted subjects from larger research projects: Dowling et al. [18] utilized a population sample, while Dowling et al. [19] focused on treatment-seeking individuals from a broader study [22].​ Research indicates significant findings regarding partner victimization and the emergence of gambling behaviors as a coping mechanism to escape reality. Specifically, Dowling et al. [18] observed that such coping strategies are more prevalent among female subjects. Family members—including partners, parents, and children—often serve as both victims and perpetrators of domestic violence. For instance, fathers and fathers-in-law are frequently identified as perpetrators of violence toward gamblers, whereas partners and mothers-in-law are commonly victims of gamblers' aggression [18]. Across studies, gambling-related problems were reported in association with increased occurrences of domestic violence within families. Hing et al. [26, 28] analyzed how male partners' gambling contributes to violence against women, revealing that such individuals, often holding patriarchal views and exerting dominant decision-making control, use violence to support their gambling behavior. Gambling not only increases the frequency and severity of violence but also leads to various forms of physical, psychological, and financial abuse. This relationship is further intensified by gender inequality, which provides a conducive environment for violence.​ In a subsequent study, Hing et al. [28, 30, 31] explored how gambling amplifies IPV, linking it to gender factors such as male control. Banks and Waters [4] confirmed that violence perpetrated by men with GD is often instrumental, involving coercive practices to obtain money for gambling, conceal behavior, and shift blame onto partners. They recommend improvements in the criminal justice system, victim support services, and gambling support agencies to address these issues effectively. Hing et al. [28, 30, 31] further investigated how male partners' gambling contributes to domestic violence, arguing that patriarchal attitudes and male control exacerbate the situation. The prioritization of gambling over the well-being of partners and families, along with financial control by male partners, are central elements leading to punitive violence, keeping women in a state of fear and abuse.​ Focusing on older women, Hing et al. [27, 29, 32] explored how gambling amplifies IPV within this demographic. Generational factors and a lack of adequate services make it difficult for these women to recognize and report gambling-related violence, thereby worsening coercive control and partner abuse.​ Finally, Brambilla et al. [7] analyzed the relationship between gambling and IPV in Tanzania, highlighting that gambling is associated with various forms of sexual, emotional, and economic violence. The results suggest that gambling contributes to the perpetration of IPV.

Associations between GD and relational and psychosocial factors

Table 4 presents the characteristics of four studies examining associations between GD and relational, psychosocial, and contextual factors within couple relationships. The included studies employed cross-sectional, longitudinal, and qualitative designs and explored how sociodemographic, relational, and psychological factors are associated with gambling behavior and related harms. Given the observational nature of most studies, findings are described in terms of associations rather than causal effects.. The identified studies employ cross-sectional, longitudinal, and qualitative research designs, mostly analyzing the relationships between sociodemographic, psychosocial factors, behavioral variables, and factors associated with gambling severity and related harms. Across studies, GD was reported in association with poorer mental health outcomes and relational difficulties within couples. Castrén et al. [8] showed that emotional, relational, and financial harms vary by gender and the type of relationship with the gambler [48]. Tulloch et al. [65, 66] confirmed that family gambling is associated with stress factors and other addictions, and was associated with poorer quality of life. Lastly, Cheung [9] showed that factors associated with increased gambling involvement vary by gender. Women tend to gamble more in association with work stress and adolescent difficulties, while men do so in association with family conflicts and childhood stress.. Discussion

This systematic review aimed to synthesize the existing evidence on the associations between GD and couple relationships, adopting a systemic-relational perspective to examine relational functioning, IPV, psychological and contextual correlates, marital status, attachment patterns, and treatment-related processes within intimate partnerships affected by GD. Across the five thematic areas examined, the findings consistently indicate that GD is associated with impaired couple functioning, increased relational conflict, emotional distress, and elevated risk of IPV. In addition, relational variables such as attachment patterns, marital status, and partner involvement in treatment emerged as central factors linked to the experience and management of GD. Taken together, the evidence highlights the relevance of a systemic-relational perspective for understanding gambling-related harms beyond individual behavior. Research has generally shown that the couple's context can be a crucial factor in understanding GD. In fact, on one hand, the couple can act as a protective factor for the development and maintenance of gambling behavior; at the same time, however, it can also serve as a risk factor, contributing to the stable persistence of such behaviors. The highlighted studies have therefore emphasized the importance of considering the couple's context in the management and treatment of GD.

Treatments for problem gamblers and couples/families

Evidence from the reviewed studies suggests that couple- and family-involved interventions are generally associated with improvements in relational functioning and, in several cases, reductions in gambling-related distress and severity.. From a systemic-relational perspective, couple relationships can be conceptualized as interdependent systems in which relational patterns are associated with both the experience of gambling-related problems and the response to treatment. This framework provides a useful lens for interpreting empirical findings showing that partner involvement and couple-based interventions are linked to improvements in relational functioning and gambling-related outcomes [57]. Across treatment-focused studies, couple-based interventions such as CCT and Integrative CT were associated with improvements in couple adjustment, emotional regulation, and partner well-being. Several studies also reported reductions in gambling severity and gambling-related distress, particularly when partners were actively involved in the therapeutic process.For instance, the gambler’s partner may unknowingly facilitate gambling behavior through dynamics of co-dependency, control, and dysfunctional emotional regulation attempts, creating a vicious cycle in which the problem perpetuates itself. This process often manifests through patterns of negative and positive reinforcement: in an attempt to protect the gambler from the consequences of gambling (e.g., covering debts or minimizing the problem), the partner ultimately may be associated with the maintenance of dysfunctional patterns [35]. In this context, studies suggest that couple therapies specifically oriented toward a systemic understanding of the disorder, such as CCT, can be effective in modifying these dysfunctional relational dynamics [43]. However, further research is needed to assess the effectiveness of couple therapy in treating GD, considering the role of a broader context that includes not only the couple but also transgenerational influences and learned attachment patterns [3].

Additionally, several studies indicate that treatment outcomes and relational processes associated with GD may vary according to gender and relationship type, suggesting the need for tailored couple-based interventions that account for these differences.The systemic perspective encourages the consideration of such factors in understanding the disorder, analyzing how different couple configurations may influence the evolution of addiction and therapeutic intervention possibilities. Longitudinal studies could provide a clearer understanding of how GD and couple relationships influence each other over time [59]. Although effective therapies for couples, such as CCT, exist, further studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of couple therapy, which remains an underutilized tool in the treatment of GD [3]. A systemic reading of GD not only allows for the identification of ways in which the couple’s relationship contributes to the perpetuation of addiction but also provides insights for targeted interventions that take into account the complex network of interactions among family system members.

GD has been consistently associated with IPV, with studies reporting bidirectional associations between gambling-related problems and violent dynamics within couples. Violent dynamics and gambling intertwine, negatively affecting the functioning of the couple [61, 62]. Despite these methodological constraints, the available evidence consistently points to a strong association between GD and multiple forms of IPV, indicating that gambling-related harms frequently extend to relational safety and power dynamics within couples. The systemic perspective highlights that gender inequalities within the couple can amplify the impact of violence in relation to GD (Toula et al., 2013). In this context, gender inequalities play a central role in power dynamics within the couple and in regulating family tensions, often in dysfunctional ways. The economic dependence of the non-gambling partner can make her more vulnerable to abuse, especially if the family income is depleted by gambling, thereby increasing the risk of financial and psychological violence [4]. Although many studies describe the phenomenon, there is a lack of experimental research assessing the effectiveness of combined prevention or treatment interventions for gambling and domestic violence.

4.3 Associations between GD and relational and psychosocial factors

The limited application of longitudinal studies in GD research hampers the ability to observe the disorder's long-term progression. While some studies, such as Cheung [9], highlight gender differences in gambling motivations, they often do not thoroughly examine how cultural and contextual factors influence these behaviors, thereby limiting the generalizability of findings across diverse social and cultural contexts. Additionally, many investigations focus on individual factors or the emotional, relational, and financial harms associated with GD but frequently overlook the complex interactions between partners and the systemic dynamics that influence gambling behavior. Although some research acknowledges that stress and psychological challenges, including family conflicts, may are associated with gambling, they often fail to explore in depth how these factors interact with other dependencies or psychological conditions. Addressing these gaps requires a more comprehensive, culturally sensitive, and systemically oriented research approach to fully understand and inform prevention and intervention strategies ​

Marital status and conditions & attachment styles

Research indicates that couple dynamics and attachment styles significantly influence the risk of developing GD. Individuals with insecure attachment styles, such as fearful attachment, often experience heightened relational insecurity and communication difficulties, which can exacerbate gambling behaviors (Marionneau et al., 2023). For instance, studies have found that individuals with a fearful attachment style report greater severity of gambling behavior than those with a secure one. These findings suggest that GD not only affects the individual but also disrupts couple dynamics, perpetuating stress and conflict within relationships.​ This highlights the importance of parental involvement and supervision during critical developmental periods.​ Marital relationships also appear to serve as a protective factor against the development of GD. A systematic review identified being single or married for less than five years as risk factors for developing or maintaining a gambling disorder, highlighting the role of relational stability in psychological well-being [5]. ​

Furthermore, gambling behaviors can have legal implications, influencing decisions related to marriage contracts and demonstrating how the disorder can affect both the structural and legal aspects of interpersonal relationships [67]. For example, gambling-related issues may lead to financial disputes and considerations in legal proceedings concerning marital assets and obligations.​ In summary, the interplay between attachment styles, familial relationships, and marital status plays a crucial role in the risk and manifestation of GD, emphasizing the need for comprehensive approaches that consider these relational factors in prevention and treatment strategies.​

Limits and future directions

This systematic review presents several limitations that must be considered when interpreting its results and conclusions. While systematic reviews are valuable for aggregating and analyzing existing literature, the predominance of cross-sectional studies within this review constrains the ability to capture the complexity of couple dynamics and gambling behavior over time. Cross-sectional studies provide only a snapshot of these dynamics, making it challenging to understand their evolution or long-term influence on treatment outcomes.​ In particular, the predominant use of cross-sectional research designs prevents an understanding of the evolution of interactions between the gambler and their partner over time, hindering a deeper analysis of the problem [5]. Another significant limitation pertains to the adequacy of current measurement tools, such as the Hurt, Insult, Threaten, and Scream (HITS) scale, in assessing IPV within the context of gambling disorder. While the HITS scale has demonstrated good internal consistency and concurrent validity with the Conflict Tactics Scale, its brevity may not fully capture the frequency, intensity, and implications of violent or gambling-related dynamics within couples. For instance, the HITS scale comprises only four items, which may limit its comprehensiveness in assessing the multifaceted nature of IPV. Additionally, variations in sensitivity and specificity have been reported across different populations, suggesting potential limitations in its applicability. ​

Furthermore, the generalizability of the results is compromised by the lack of representativeness in the analyzed samples, which often do not adequately reflect the diversity of the global population in terms of socioeconomic, cultural, and gender differences. To address these limitations, it is essential to promote longitudinal studies that allow for the monitoring of changes over time and assess how they influence both gambling behavior and therapeutic interventions. Developing more precise and comprehensive measurement tools, such as expanded versions of existing scales or entirely new instruments, is crucial to capture the nuanced interactions within affected couples. Additionally, including more heterogeneous samples and considering gender differences will contribute to a more inclusive and detailed understanding of gambling disorder and its impact on intimate relationships.​

Finally, given that the overall methodological quality of the included studies ranged from low to moderate, conclusions were drawn with caution. Evidence from lower-quality studies was considered exploratory, while greater weight was given to findings that were consistent across studies and designs.

Conclusion

This study underscores the critical importance of considering the couple's context in understanding and managing GD. Couple dynamics can serve as both risk and protective factors, with GD exerting profound and complex effects not only on the couple but also on the entire family system. Research indicates that these dynamics are multidimensional, significantly influencing gambling behavior and its progression.​ However, current research faces notable limitations, including a predominant reliance on cross-sectional designs, variability in measurement tools, and a lack of representative samples that adequately consider gender differences. Addressing these gaps is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of GD.​ A systemic and integrated approach that incorporates couple and family dynamics, along with socio-economic and cultural variables, is crucial for developing more effective treatment and prevention strategies. Family therapy, for instance, offers a comprehensive approach by improving communication, resolving conflicts, and fostering a supportive environment for recovery. Studies have shown that family engagement in treatment is linked to lower dropout rates and better attendance in outpatient settings. ​Furthermore, integrating couple therapy into treatment plans can address the complex interplay between addiction and intimate relationships, promoting health system change and enhancing recovery outcomes. ​

In conclusion, advancing research methodologies to include longitudinal studies, standardizing measurement tools, and ensuring diverse, representative samples will significantly contribute to a deeper understanding of GD. Such efforts will facilitate the development of nuanced, effective interventions that consider the intricate dynamics of couples and families affected by gambling disorder.​
