ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimIntimate partner violence has a significant impact on people as individuals, as families, and as communities. The ripple effects extend beyond the immediate victims, straining healthcare systems, stifling economic productivity, and hindering social progress. The study aims to present the spatial distribution of intimate partner violence prevalence, identify socio-demographic and contextual predictors, and establish associations with public health indicators.MethodsThis is a secondary data analysis, using a nationally representative demographic and health survey (DHS) conducted in Nigeria. A two-stage stratified random sampling was employed in the standard DHS survey and 10,678 women of childbearing age were randomly selected for the intimate partner violence module. A Research Proforma of the selected variables from DHS was used to extract the data required for the study. Descriptive and inferential analyses were carried out on the data using Microsoft Excel dashboards, spatial map and random-intercept logistic regression.ResultsThe study found the prevalence of intimate partner violence across the country to be 33.9%. Region and place of residence had significant association with intimate partner violence (p < 0.05). The multivariate analysis identified ethnicity difference of couple (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.03–1.48), age (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03), sex of household head (OR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.22–3.94), jealousy (OR = 1.67, 95%, CI: 1.50–1.86), family history of intimate partner violence (OR = 2.79, 95% CI: 2.29–3.40), and alcohol consumption (OR = 3.01, 95% CI 2.54–3.58) among other factors as the socio-demographic and contextual factors influencing intimate partner violence. Intimate partner violence also had a significant association with mental health, physical health, and sexual health. The spatial analysis done on the prevalence of intimate partner violence found a variation in the occurrence of intimate partner violence across the States of the country, while the probability of intimate partner violence obtained from the model ranges from 0.24 to 0.85.ConclusionThe study identified communities in Nigeria with higher probability of intimate partner violence (IPV). These communities should be priortised for targeted intervention, also it is recommended that laws on IPV should be strengthened at national and regional levels.Keywords: Intimate partner violence, Physical abuse, Public health, Wealth index, Abusive behaviour

BODY
Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is one of the hidden pandemics that has endangered the health and well-being of millions of people worldwide [1]. In addition to causing suffering for individuals, it places a significant strain on public health systems due to physical injuries, mental health problems, reproductive health difficulties, and increased death rates [1, 2]. In the context of marriage, cohabitation, or any other kind of relationship, this violence comprises controlling behaviours, emotional and financial abuse, and physical, sexual, or mentally destructive actions [3]. IPV is one of the major health problems that both men and women face [4, 5]. However, according to research, the incidence and reporting rate among women is higher than that of men [5]. This is not unconnected to cultural and social factors [6, 7]. While 1 in every 3 women has faced one kind of abuse or the other [8], 1 in every 7 men has faced physical violence from an intimate relationship at some point in their lives [9].

The significant burden of intimate partner violence worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic due to its control measures such as curfews, movement restrictions, and lockdowns [10]. Domestic abuse is prevalent and entrenched; according to WHO estimates, 736 to 852 million women aged 15 and above have experienced some kind of violence [11]. In 2022, around 47,000 women and girls were killed by their intimate partners or other family members worldwide [12]. Studies have also shown that 36% of women in Sub-Saharan Africa aged 15 to 49 have suffered intimate partner abuse at some point in their lives [13], 33–51% in South Asia [14], and 51.7% In Europe in 2022 [15].

According to a recent study, the frequency of IPV varies throughout African nations [16]. This stands between the lowest which is 10% in Comoros to 60% which is the highest reported in Sierra Leone, making it one of the regions with the highest reported rates. Additionally, a survey of 16 African countries found that the frequency of intimate partner violence (IPV) was 30.7% across the board, with South Africa reporting the lowest incidence at 12.7% and Uganda reporting the highest at 44% [17]. A study in a low-income community in Southwest Nigeria found that 28.2% of people have experienced physical intimate partner abuse throughout their lives [18].

A research conducted among pregnant women in Northern Nigeria showed that 28% of women in Zaria, 62.7% in Jos, and 30.4% in Sokoto has one time or the other experienced IPV, with the forms of abuse being psychological abuse (53.5%), financial abuse (48.5%), and sexual abuse (57.3%) [18]. Despite its prevalence, little is known about the regional distribution of IPV and its impact on public health in Nigeria, making it an important field for research. IPV may take many different forms, each with its effect [10]. Sexual abuse, including coercion and coerced sexual behaviours, have a negative influence on reproductive health [19].

Long-term mental health conditions, such as depression and post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), may be caused by emotional and psychological abuse, including humiliation and threats [20]. When a victim's financial resources are limited or controlled, this undermines their autonomy and fosters dependence [21]. IPV’s geographical distribution is influenced by socioeconomic status, cultural beliefs, and levels of gender equality [22]. Study shows that groups with high levels of social disorder, economic instability, and low educational level are more likely to experience intimate partner violence [23]. Economic inequality and well-ingrained cultural norms explain regional disparities in IPV incidence in Nigeria. According to data, violence against women is more common in nations where patriarchal institutions are well-ingrained [24]. Another difficulty is the disparity between rural and urban locations; underreporting persists in rural areas, while urban areas report more instances due to increased awareness and availability of support services [25, 26].

IPV has long-term consequences for victims, in addition to the immediate trauma it causes [16]. Studies revealed that witnesses to IPV are more prone to developing mental and behavioural issues as children, perpetuating a cycle of abuse that spans generations [27]. While studies have shown regional and local disparities in IPV prevalence, there is a paucity of studies that fully capture IPV at the community level and the sociodemographic and contextual predictors contributing to these disparities [28].

This study therefore aims to provide evidence of the geographical variation of IPV in Nigeria by investigating the sociodemographic and contextual factors that contribute to its frequency, as well as assessing the potential public health consequences.

Methods

This study used a nationally representative demographic and health survey conducted in Nigeria. The country is situated on Africa's western coast with a latitude and longitude of 10 00 N, 8 00 E and boasts diverse geography and a multitude of languages, including Yoruba, Igbo, and Hausa. Established in 1914, Nigeria gained independence in 1960, remaining a Commonwealth member since 1963. It shares borders with Niger, Chad, Cameroon, and Benin, with access to the Gulf of Guinea. It's both geographically expansive, exceeding the size of Texas, and densely populated, standing as Africa's most populous nation with over 250 ethnic groups.

Study population

The target population is women of childbearing age who are in intimate relationships. In the DHS survey, 41,821 women aged 15–49 years were covered and 10,678 were randomly selected for the intimate partner violence module. Women who are included in the intimate partner violence module but could not be interviewed because privacy could not be established were excluded from the study.

Sampling technique/sampling size

A subset of women of childbearing age were randomly selected for intimate partner violence. A two-stage stratified random sampling was employed in the standard DHS survey to select a total of 10,678 women of childbearing age who are in intimate relationships.

Study Variable

Dependent Variable: Intimate parter violence, is a dichotomous variable that takes a value of one if the respondent has experienced at any time or in the last 12 months at least one of physical, sexual, and emotional violence by her intimate partner.

Independent Variable: The independent variables are socio-demographic (respondent’s age, respondent’s occupational status, respondent’s educational level, religion, ethnicity difference, wealth index, head of household, place of residence, media exposure, number of unions, number of wives), partner’s characteristics (partner’s alcohol use, partner’s age, partner is jealous if respondent talks to other men, partner accuses respondent of unfaithfulness), history of intimate partner violence (respondent’s father ever beat her mother) and wife-related factors (currently residing with husband/partner. reasons for not having sex, relationship with most recent sex partner, the person who decides how to spend respondent’s earnings, time of the first event).

Data management and analysis

A Research Proforma was developed to extract the required data for the study from the DHS. Data preparation, sorting, cleaning, and variable computation were done in Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) version 27. Descriptive analysis, frequency count, and percentage were used to determine the prevalence of intimate partner violence across the regions. Inferential analysis; multilevel logistic regression implemented in R software was used to identify the socio-demographic and contextual factors influencing intimate partner violence, and bivariate chi-square was used to determine the association of intimate partner violence with some public health indicators. Quantum Geographical Information Science (QGIS) was used for mapping, dashboards were generated using Microsoft Excel.

Ethical consideration

The authourization to access and utilize the NDHS data for this study was granted by the DHS programme.

Socio-demographic and Contextual Factors Influencing Intimate Partner Violence

The results revealed that various socio-demographic and contextual factors significantly impact the likelihood of experiencing intimate partner violence, such as the respondent’s age (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03), indicating that as the respondent’s age increases, the odds of experiencing intimate partner violence also increase. The age of the household head (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00) also plays a role, with an increase in the age of the household head resulting in a 1% decrease in the odds of experiencing intimate partner violence. The geographic location of respondents also plays a role, with respondents from the northeast region with an OR of 2.54 (95% CI: 1.99–3.26) being 3 times more likely to experience intimate partner violence compared to the Northwest.

The media exposure level (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.15–1.61) also affects the likelihood of intimate partner violence, with respondents exposed to media facilities being 36% more likely to experience it compared to respondents who are not exposed to media facilities. Ethnic differences between partners (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.03–1.48) also play a role, with respondents from different ethnic backgrounds being 23% more likely to experience intimate partner violence compared to respondents who have the same ethnic background. Female-headed households (OR = 2.19, 95% CI: 1.22–3.94) are 2 times more likely to experience intimate partner violence than male-headed households. The wealth index variables also play a role, with the richest wealth group with an OR of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.51–0.84) being 34% less likely to experience intimate partner violence than those in the middle wealth index group.

Unemployed respondents (OR = 1.19, 95%, CI: 1.02–1.39) are 19% more likely to experience intimate partner violence compared to employed respondents. Those whose partners; are jealous (OR = 1.67, 95%, CI: 1.50– 1.86), accuse them of unfaithfulness(OR = 4.59, 95% CI: 3.67–5.73), afraid of their partners (OR = 2.74, 95% CI: 2.38–3.16), family history of intimate partner violence (OR = 2.79, 95%, CI: 2.29–3.40), and alcohol consumption (OR = 3.01, 95%, CI: -2.54–3.58) are also more likely to experience intimate partner violence, compared to those whose partner do not accuse them of unfaithfulness, who are not afraid of their partners, who do do not have family history of IPV, and whose partner do not consume alcohol.

Variables such as type of place of residence, educational difference, justification for beating, literacry level, number of unions, residence with partner, number of other wives, relationship with the most recent sex partner are not significant predictors of intimate partner violence.

Discussion of findings

The findings revealed that about 33.9% of respondents reported having experienced IPV, which is consistent with global data, including the WHO’s report on violence against women, noting that 1 in 3 women encounter intimate partner violence (IPV) [29]. The markedly higher prevalence (33.9%) in this study, compared to Australasia (3%), Central Europe (5%), and North America (6%) [30], likely reflects structural gaps—such as weak enforcement of protective laws, normalization of violence, and socio-cultural silence around domestic abuse in Nigeria [31]. However, a study in India where socio-cultural norms condone male dominance and normalize spousal abuse report similarly high prevalence rates (31.4%), highlighting the role of entrenched gender norms in perpetuating intimate partner violence [32]. This study's high prevalence, despite current legal frameworks like the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act 2015 [33], highlights notable spatial variations in enforcement and public confidence in legal recourse. This implies that without context-specific implementation, culturally grounded involvement, and regionally unique awareness strategies that address ingrained sociocultural norms that support intimate partner violence, legal presence alone is insufficient [34].

Intimate partner violence prevalence varies significantly by location, with the North East (47.5%) and South-South (41.5%) having the greatest rates. In the Northeast, issues like prolonged conflict, displacement, and weak institutional frameworks might make it harder for women to receive protective services and increase their susceptibility to abuse [35]. The high prevalence in the South-South, despite relative economic advancement, may be attributed to long-standing gender power disparities, the normalisation of partner control, and the underutilisation of legal or psychosocial support services [36]. These trends demonstrate the necessity of enhanced response mechanisms and prevention tactics tailored to a certain area.

Similar trends are seen around the world, with greater prevalence rates reported in areas like Sub-Saharan Africa that are impacted by poverty, conflict and underutilization of support services [37]. The considerably lower prevalence in the South West (17.6%) may indicate higher access to education, legal resources, and media exposure in urban areas like Lagos and Ibadan, which could influence awareness and reporting [38]. However, the high rates in the equally urbanised South-South imply that urbanisation alone does not explain regional disparities. The observed patterns are probably the result of complicated interactions between additional contextual factors, including local understandings towards gender standards, the efficiency of law enforcement, and the accessibility of support services [39]. This emphasises the necessity of conducting region-specific research on the causes of IPV in addition to using crude urban–rural classifications [2].

According to the results, intimate partner violence is more common in rural areas (36%) than in urban areas (30.6%), which is consistent with previous studies on rural–urban disparity in IPV, which found that rates were higher in rural areas because of cultural norms, limited availability of support services, and economic dependency [40, 41]. While protective regulations benefit all regions, their efficacy is increased in urban areas due to superior infrastructure, enforcement mechanisms, and support service accessibility, necessitating focused interventions in rural regions, such as community-based education, enhanced reporting mechanisms, and more legal and psychosocial support [42].

The results show that the prevalence of IPV is lowest among victims in the wealthiest households (14.15%) and highest among victims the middle-income (23.44%) and poorest (22.47%) wealth index categories. This is consistent with research demonstrating that financial stress is a contributing factor to intimate partner violence, especially in households with few resources [43]. Similarly, [44] discovered that economic dependency and stress-related disputes led to increased abuse rates in low-income environments. On the other hand, the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act (2015), currently operational primarily in the Federal Capital Territory and a limited number of states, may be more accessible to wealthier households who often reside in urban areas with stronger legal infrastructure, better awareness, and greater capacity to seek redress [33]. While this result shows the disparity between the poor and the wealthy, there is concern about the possible underreporting of violence among the more affluent groups due to the fear of stigma [45].

The results show that the prevalence of intimate partne violence varies significantly depending on the characteristics of women, with higher rates recorded among Christians (53.31%) and the Hausa ethnic group (19.83%). These trends may reflect the result of regional factors, including reduced female autonomy, patriarchal attitudes, and differences in religion or community-level tolerance of IPV, especially in areas where conventional gender roles are more strictly enforced [39]. These results are consistent with research conducted in Ethiopia and India, which found that deeply ingrained sociocultural practices rooted in some religious and ethnic traditions, such as male dominance, stigmatising survivors, and normalising wife-beating, increase vulnerability to IPV and discourage reporting [46, 47]. The findings revealed lower rates of violence among women with higher education (6.75%), therefore highlighting educational attainment has a protective impact, supporting the idea that education enables women to identify and fend off violence [48]. The prevalence among younger women (25.75%), aged 20 to 29, is consistent with global trends that associate youth with an increased risk of intimate partner violence because of their inexperience, as reported by [49, 50]. These results highlight the necessity of concentrated interventions that address cultural norms, education, and policies aimed at preventing intimate partner violence among young people.

The findings revealed that the respondents justified intimate partner violence by citing cultural norms, which is indicative of deeply ingrained patriarchal ideology, as documented in a study among African women and girls [51], and in another study among South Asian women [52], both reporting similar ideologies of justifying intimate partner violence because of gendered power dynamics. Another study found that women's roles in caregiving, household chores, and sexual compliance are weaponized to justify violence and are consistent with justifications including ignoring children, burning food, or refusing sex [53]. Furthermore, cultural norms in Nigeria frequently silence women, which reinforces these violent practices [54]. International human rights frameworks such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) are directly violated by these occurences, which also show the normalization of marital rape and autonomy abuses [55]. Although marital rape and spousal abuse are illegal under Nigeria's VAPP Act [56], these negative attitudes are perpetuated due to the poor implementation of the law. To reduce cultural acceptability and advance equitable relationships, comprehensive initiatives are required to address these assumptions [57].

The regional differences in the occurrence of intimate partner violence reflect inherent sociocultural, economic, and legal issues which are shaped by local norms, varying levels of education, and accessibility to support services. For instance, the study recorded a higher prevalence of intimate partner violence in states like Edo, Kogi, and Ebonyi, which may be attributed to strong patriarchal customs, restricted access to resources, and underreporting. These results align with those of [52, 57], who noted that IPV has become culturally accepted in rural communities. Regional disparities in IPV prevalence may be attributed to the presence and implementation of protective legislation [39]. Prevalence rates are lower in states that have implemented the VAPP Act, such as Lagos and Osun. The Act can lessen IPV by giving victims legal safeguards and holding offenders accountable. However, states without such legislation might not have the resources needed to deal with and stop violence, which would raise prevalence rates [33]. It is important to note, nevertheless, that Nigeria has difficulty enforcing these legal frameworks [58]. Furthermore, states with low IPV rates might have created a false sense of security, thus underreporting the situation because of social stigma or fear of consequences [45]. While these differences are exacerbated by the government's inconsistent application of the law in different areas, to address IPV nationwide, the results highlight the urgent need for more efficient law enforcement, awareness programs, and regional resource distribution.

The findings highlighted important social and contextual factors influencing intimate partner violence. For instance, the increased odds of violence with increasing respondent age are consistent with previous research findings that older women often suffer heightened vulnerability due to ingrained gender norms and power imbalances [59]. The connection between alcohol use and IPV (OR = 3.01) is in line with previous research, which demonstrates that alcohol worsens violence by lowering judgment and boosting aggression [60]. Furthermore, the increased probability of violence in female-headed families (OR = 2.19) deviates from conventional expectations and implies that women in these households suffer particular vulnerabilities, possibly attributable to a lack of protecting male partners, greater economic demands, societal stigma, and dimished family authority, all of which may increase vulnerability to abuse by intimate or non-intimate abusers [61].

Regional differences are highlighted by the geographical representation of intimate partner violence risk, which identifies high-probability locations, particularly in the North East and South South. This result is consistent with earlier research, including that of [62], which found that socioeconomic factors and deeply ingrained cultural norms contribute to greater IPV rates in both northern and southern Nigeria. The use of colour-coded gradients improves visual clarity and comprehensive understanding of the geographical context of the occurrence of intimate-partner violence across the country.

The statistically significant association between IPV and humiliation aligns with findings from previous research, which highlight the emotional and psychological toll of violence [63]. The mental health implications, such as anxiety, depression, and PTSD, are consistent with global literature, which underscores the long-term impact of emotional abuse [64]. In Nigeria, where intimate partner violence is often underreported and women lack sufficient legal protection [65], the findings underscore the need for stronger enforcement of laws like the VAPP Act, which should also provide better mental health support and reduce the stigma associated with seeking help.

The statistically significant association between humiliation and IPV is consistent with findings from other research that emphasize the psychological effects of abusive relationships, such as despair and low self-esteem [66]. Long-term mental health conditions including anxiety, sadness, and PTSD are frequently brought on by the emotional trauma caused by such insults [67]. In Nigeria, where victims are deterred from seeking assistance by social shame and inadequate legal frameworks, the findings underscore the urgent need for enhanced mental health services for survivors [48].

The statistically significant association between IPV and experiences like being kicked or dragged reflect the severity of physical abuse reported. Such acts are known precursors of physical health consequences, including chronic pain, injuries and disability, as documented in previous studies [32, 68]. These results support concerns about how widespread IPV is in Nigeria, where victims cannot receive the required care due to societal hurdles and insufficient healthcare interventions [69]. While physical stress is commonly associated with chronic diseases such as hypertension and reproductive health difficulties [70], failure to address IPV exposes many victims to further harm.

The statistically significant association between sexual violence and intimate partner violence is consistent with data from previous studies indicating that sexual assault is typically a component of IPV, causing significant physical and psychological harm [71, 72]. Marital sexual assault in Nigeria remains underreported due to cultural shame and weak legal protections [72]. Despite the fact that the VAPP Act addresses sexual violence, its enforcement is challenging [33]. The study's findings highlight the urgent need for strategies to address the multiple public health implications of intimate partner violence.

Conclusion and recommendations

The study explores the spatial variation of IPV and its association with selected public health indicators. It reveals that socio-demographic and contextual factors, such as education, wealth index, ethnic differences, and family history of intimate partner violence, significantly influence the prevalence of intimate partner violence. The findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions and policy responses to address the complex interplay of contextual factors and the widespread consequences of intimate partner violence. The study underscores the need for a multidisciplinary approach to combating intimate partner violence and its public health consequences.

To address the threat that intimate partner violence poses on public health, this study suggests that governments should strengthen existing intimate partner violence laws, aligning them with international standards and criminalizing all forms of abuse. Community leaders and local groups should conduct culturally specific awareness programs to educate citizens on the negative impacts of intimate partner violence. Healthcare practitioners should incorporate routine screening for IPV during patient visits and train staff to spot indicators and offer psychological first aid—a compassionate, non-intrusive approach to help survivors feel safe, listened to, and connected to further care. Socio-demographic characteristics, such as low income and limited education, increase the likelihood of intimate partner violence. Initiatives that support economic and educational empowerment of vulnerable groups, especially women, should be prioritized, to reduce susceptibility to IPV. Strong intimate partner violence surveillance systems should be established, and advocacy groups, public health agencies, and non-governmental organizations should push for the inclusion of intimate partner violence prevention in national public health agendas. Ultimately, safe havens should be established in high-occurrence regions, and programs should engage boys and men in preventing intimate partner violence.

Srength and limitation

The study's strengths include a comprehensive analysis using a nationwide cross-sectional design, advanced statistical methods, and dashboards and maps. It provides a robust framework for identifying socio-demographic and contextual factors influencing intimate partner violence and its association with public health indicators. The large sample size from the NDHS program increases generalizability.

Despite its strengths, the study also has some limitations, including reliance on secondary data from the NDHS program, a cross-sectional design that may not accurately measure causality between intimate partner violence and public health outcomes, and potential generalizability to different countries or contexts.
