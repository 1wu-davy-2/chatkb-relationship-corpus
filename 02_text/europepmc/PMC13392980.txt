ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMindfulness is beneficial for mental health and romantic relationships. Recently, there has been a novel application of mindfulness to romantic relationships called “mindful partnering” (MP). Emerging evidence from interpersonal mindfulness frameworks suggests that relational forms of mindfulness may provide unique benefits for individuals and their relationships beyond those associated with partners' individual intrapersonal mindfulness. However, interpersonal mindfulness in relationships (MP) is multifaceted, and previous examinations of interpersonal mindfulness in couples have been limited by focusing on a small number of isolated features. In the current study, we applied actor–partner interdependence models in two samples (Ns = 168;170) to test how MP (including self‐reports and observations) predicts mental and relational health outcomes. Results indicated that greater MP predicts fewer mental health symptoms and greater relationship health. Alongside literature supporting mindfulness as beneficial for couples, these results suggest that MP may be a strong target for couple‐focused interventions.Keywords: depressive symptoms, dyadic adjustment, interpersonal mindfulness, mental health, mindful partnering, mindfulness, observational methods, romantic relationships, romantic satisfaction

BODY
Introduction

There are consistent, positive associations between relationship quality and well‐being (Proulx et al. 2007). Low relationship satisfaction is a major risk factor for many mental health diagnoses, including mood, anxiety, and stress‐related disorders (South 2023). However, there is also evidence for bidirectional associations, such that relationship difficulties predict individual psychological distress, and individual distress predicts relationship difficulties (Lebow et al. 2012). Thus, intervention approaches capable of impacting mental and relational health simultaneously are of particular interest to couples therapists. Mindfulness is one such approach. Defined as “paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally” (Kabat‐Zinn 1994, 4), both dispositional mindfulness (an existing tendency to focus on the present moment with nonjudgment) and mindfulness cultivated through intervention are associated with a number of beneficial outcomes for individuals and couples, including for mental health, physical health, and relational health (Carson et al. 2007; Creswell and Lindsay 2014; Gardi et al. 2022; Tomlinson et al. 2018). Recent interest in interpersonal mindfulness, which expands on previous work on intrapersonal or within‐person mindfulness, suggests that applying mindfulness interpersonally with others (between‐persons) in ways that could be observed is particularly beneficial for relationship outcomes (Kimmes et al. 2020, 2025). Thus, targeting romantic relationship dynamics by modifying discrete behaviors and their underlying patterns of thoughts and emotions, rather than focusing solely on symptoms (Kabat‐Zinn 1994), is likely to improve mental and relational health. In this article, we present two studies that examine the extent to which mindful partnering (MP), the application of interpersonal mindfulness to romantic relationship dynamics, predicts mental and relational health outcomes in couples.

Intrapersonal mindfulness is a well‐established predictor of mental and physical health outcomes (Bogusch et al. 2016; Creswell and Lindsay 2014; De Vibe 2012; Gardi et al. 2022; Lucas‐Thompson et al. 2019, 2021; Tomlinson et al. 2018). Mindfulness‐based intervention (MBI) targeting intrapersonal mindfulness has received attention from healthcare professionals because it is an efficacious and well‐liked approach for treating health conditions (Brown and Ryan 2003; Creswell 2017; Creswell and Lindsay 2014; De Vibe 2012; Kozlov et al. 2022; Ledesma and Kumano 2009; Lucas‐Thompson et al. 2023; Rigsby et al. 2024; Schumer et al. 2018). In the context of relationships, greater intrapersonal mindfulness is associated with higher levels of romantic satisfaction (Barnes et al. 2007; McGill and Adler‐Baeder 2020; McGill et al. 2016) and quality (Wachs and Cordova 2007). Growing interest in intrapersonal mindfulness has prompted researchers and practitioners to examine how mindfulness can be applied interpersonally in relationships beyond within‐person applications.

For couples, early work on “relationship mindfulness” indicates that it is beneficial in ways similar to intrapersonal mindfulness, perhaps even with added benefits (Kimmes et al. 2018, 2020, 2025). Yet, research on relationship mindfulness has been limited and several gaps exist; MBIs available for romantic couples tend to focus on intrapersonal mindfulness (Gambrel and Piercy 2015a, 2015b; Winter et al. 2021), and studies of interpersonal mindfulness in couples have rarely captured all five theorized facets of mindfulness, including observing, awareness, describing, nonreactivity, and nonjudging (Baer et al. 2006; Carpenter et al. 2019; Kimmes et al. 2018; Mattes 2019). It is important that these limitations are addressed in the study of interpersonal mindfulness for couples, in large part because mindfulness expressed interpersonally can be perceived or observed by a partner (Kimmes et al. 2025; Rigsby et al. 2026). Distinct from self‐reported intrapersonal mindfulness, which is an inherently internal characteristic reflecting individual processes, interpersonal mindfulness can influence interpersonal communication dynamics and thus has the potential to impact outcomes for self and partner. Interpersonal conceptualizations of mindfulness, then, have the potential to affect romantic partners in beneficial ways.

The MP construct includes couple‐specific interpersonal applications of all five facets of mindfulness through mindful awareness in attention and action toward one's partner, nonreactivity during conflict with one's partner, emotional awareness of one's partner, holding intentional acceptance and compassion for one's partner, and fostering self‐compassion in the partnership (Rigsby et al. 2026; Seiter et al. 2022). MP has the added benefit of capturing behaviors that are spontaneously generated, thereby reflecting internal qualities, as well as those that occur in response to a partner. Early investigations of MP have been promising for the construct, as it has beneficial associations with romantic conflict (Rigsby et al. n.d.) and both mental and physical health outcomes (Rigsby et al. n.d.; Seiter 2022). Despite its early promise, development and empirical evaluation of this construct remain in their early stages, such that robust tests of MP as a predictor of mental and relational health, including studies using observational assessments of MP, have yet to be conducted.

The Current Study

The current study aims to advance the knowledge base on interpersonal mindfulness by providing more rigorous tests of the MP construct. To date, few studies have evaluated how MP, expressed by individuals (actor effects) and by their partners (partner effects), predicts mental and relational health outcomes, and none have incorporated observational assessments of MP. Clarifying these associations is important for understanding how interpersonal mindfulness shapes individual well‐being, partner functioning, and overall relationship health. This work also has the potential to inform family therapists and intervention developers seeking to apply mindfulness in programming for romantic dyads. This multi‐study manuscript addresses these gaps by examining actor and partner effects of MP across two samples. Study 1 uses self‐report measures of MP, whereas Study 2 extends this work by incorporating a multi‐method assessment of MP that includes observational ratings of MP to provide a more robust assessment of actor and partner contributions to mental and relational health.

Study 1

Methods

Participants

Eighty‐four couples (n = 168 individuals, 54.10% female, 45.20% male, 0.60% other; M age = 33.58 years, SDage = 11.84 years) were recruited nationally to participate in a study about romantic relationships, mental health symptoms, biomarkers of stress, and romantic conflict. The sample was primarily heterosexual (79.50%), but included non‐heterosexual individuals and couples, as well (12.80% bisexual, 4.50% gay/lesbian, 3.20% other). Most couples were married (53.10%), and cohabitating (98.l0%), and 26.80% had at least one child. Individuals were educated, on average, to between the Associate's (5) and Bachelor's (6) degree levels (M = 5.99, SD = 1.52), and 83.30% were employed (56.30% full‐time, 27.10% part‐time). Average annual household income was between the ranges of $35,001–$50,000 (4) and $50,001–$100,000 (5; M = 4.85, SD = 1.52). The sample was primarily White (83.80%; 9.70% Hispanic, 6.30% Asian or Pacific Islander, 3.80% Black or African American, and 8.10% other).

Procedures

Data were collected in person and remotely via electronic surveys in Qualtrics, before and during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Participants provided demographic information along with self‐reports of mental health symptoms and relationship factors. All procedures were approved by the Colorado State University Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Dyadic Adjustment

Dyadic adjustment was rated using the four‐item version of the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS‐4), which includes items such as, “Please indicate the degree of happiness, all things considered, of your relationship” (Sabourin et al. 2005). Items were rated on a Likert scale from 1 = Not at all to 5 = A lot (α = 0.77) and averaged to create a composite score (M = 4.39, SD = 0.54).

Well‐Being

General well‐being was measured using the 10‐item Schwartz Outcome Scale (SOS‐10), which includes items such as “I feel hopeful about my future” (Blais et al. 1999). Each item was rated on a 7‐point Likert scale from 0 = Never to 6 = All or nearly all of the time (α = 0.93). Summed scores range from 0 to 60, with higher scores representing higher well‐being (M = 56.25, SD = 9.99).

Psychological Distress

Psychological distress was measured using the 14‐item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), which includes items such as, “In the last month, how often have you found that you could not cope with all the things that you had to do?” (Cohen et al. 1983). Items were rated on a Likert scale from 1 = Never to 5 = Very often (α = 0.87) and summed to create a composite score ranging from 14 to 70. Higher scores represent more psychological distress (M = 36.08, SD = 7.40).

Self‐Reported MP

Partners each reported on their own MP via the Mindful Partnering Measure (MPM; Seiter et al. 2022). This 22‐item measure captures five factors of MP via its subscales: (1) mindful awareness, (2) nonreactivity in conflict, (3) emotional awareness, (4) acceptance/compassion, and (5) self‐compassion. Agreement with items like, “When I'm upset with my partner, I notice how I am feeling before I take action,” was rated on a Likert scale from 1 = Never true to 5 = Always true (α = 0.88). Items were summed, after reverse‐scoring appropriate items, to create a total MP score (M = 82.88, SD = 9.75).

Potential Covariates

Participants reported their age (in years), marital status (married/not married), and whether they were cohabitating (cohabitating/not cohabitating). Each of these variables was evaluated as a potential covariate for inclusion in models.

Data Analysis Plan

Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, and bivariate correlations, were calculated for all key variables and potential covariates. Then, the actor–partner interdependence model (APIM; Kashy and Kenny 2000; Kenny et al. 2006) was applied to account for nonindependence of romantic partners in analyses and calculate both actor (self on self) and partner (partner on self) effects of MP on dependent variables. APIMs were specified via structural equation models (SEMs) in R version 4.3.1 using lavaan for dyadic adjustment and mental health symptom outcomes separately, with self‐reported MP as the predictor across models (Rosseel 2012). Each model used maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors to account for skewness in key variables. Missingness of data was evaluated empirically using Little's missing completely at random (MCAR) test (Little 1988), and full information maximum likelihood was used to handle data MCAR or missing at random.

This sample included heterosexual and non‐heterosexual romantic dyads. APIMs require that dyads are treated either as distinguishable or indistinguishable (interchangeable) based on a salient characteristic, such as biological sex (Kashy and Kenny 2000; Kenny et al. 2006). However, that salient characteristic must be capable of distinguishing all dyads in a sample (Kashy and Donnellan 2012; Kenny et al. 2006; Kenny and Ledermann 2010), and methods of assigning indistinguishable dyads into distinguishable models are statistically problematic (Smith et al. 2013). Further, distinguishability cannot be assumed without empirical testing even in heterosexual dyads, and treating dyads as indistinguishable is favorable with respect to preservation of statistical power (Hardy 2019; Kashy and Donnellan 2012; Kenny et al. 2006). Thus, in line with these recommendations and precedent in samples inclusive of heterosexual and queer couples for modeling all dyads as indistinguishable (Vaillancourt‐Morel et al. 2020), we treated all dyads as indistinguishable in primary analyses. Model fit was evaluated using the chi‐square test of model fit (nonsignificant test statistic acceptable), in addition to the comparative fit index (CFI; values > 0.95 considered good), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI, values > 0.95 considered good), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA, values < 0.06 considered good), in keeping with recommendations for SEM (Hu and Bentler 1999).

Due to the large proportion of heterosexual couples in this sample, sensitivity analyses were conducted to determine empirically whether effects in that subset of couples were distinguishable by biological sex. Distinguishability based on biological sex, was tested empirically in accordance with best practice recommendations (Hardy 2019) by comparing unconstrained models (which treat dyads as distinguishable based on biological sex) to constrained models at three sequential levels (which treat dyads as interchangeable to varying degrees): (1) complete distinguishability was tested using the I‐SAT test (Peugh et al. 2013) through APIMs with specifications applied such that male and female partner effects, intercepts for predictors and outcomes, and variances for predictors and outcomes were constrained to be equal; (2) outcome distinguishability was tested by specifying APIMs with specifications applied such that male and female partner effects, intercepts for outcomes, and variances for outcomes were constrained to be equal; (3) effect distinguishability was tested through APIMs with specifications applied such that male and female partner effects were constrained to be equal. Model fit of the nested models was compared through scaled chi‐squared difference tests with Satorra–Bentler corrections, which correct for non‐normality. Only models where dyads were empirically distinguishable were selected to be reported. Final sensitivity models were selected for fit, parsimony, and interpretability.

Sensitivity Analyses

A set of APIMs for only heterosexual couples (n = 72) was specified by including self‐reported MP as the predictor of dyadic adjustment. The fully constrained model did not fit significantly worse than the unconstrained model (Δχ 2(6) = 9.01, p = 0.173), suggesting that heterosexual dyads were not distinguishable by biological sex. Thus, models excluding queer couples were not necessary. Again, the fully constrained model did not fit significantly worse than the unconstrained model (Δχ 2(10) = 11.50, p = 0.320), suggesting that dyads were not distinguishable by biological sex and models excluding queer couples were not necessary.

Study 2

Eighty‐five couples (n = 170 individuals; 48.50% female, 46.20% male, 5.30% other; M age = 28.59 years, SDage = 9.31 years) were recruited in the Rocky Mountain region of the United States to participate in a study about romantic relationships, mindfulness, mental health symptoms, and romantic conflict. The sample was primarily heterosexual (74.60%) but also included non‐heterosexual individuals and couples (17.80% bisexual, 2.40% gay/lesbian, and 5.30% other). Most couples were unmarried (65.90%) and cohabitating (64.70%). Individuals were educated, on average, to between the Associate's (5) and Bachelor's (6) degree levels (M = 5.74, SD = 1.60), and 82.40% were employed (50.00% full‐time, 32.40% part‐time). Average annual income was in the $50,001–$100,000 range (M = 5.08, SD = 1.56). The sample was primarily White (72.80%; 15.50% Hispanic, 6.50% Asian or Pacific Islander, 2.40% Black or African American, and 17.20% other).

Couples attended a 2‐h laboratory visit during which they provided informed consent, completed electronic surveys about their health and romantic relationship, participated in mindfulness‐based tasks (e.g., breath counting and pre‐recorded mindfulness activities), and were audio/video recorded during a conflict discussion. Prior to the discussion task, partners individually rated the level of disagreement in their relationship related to common issues (e.g., in‐laws, finances, and sex) or issues they self‐identified for their own relationship. Research assistants chose three topics likely to produce conflict based on those responses, and instructed couples to discuss the selected issues, with the goal of resolving them, for 10 min. Procedures were approved by the Colorado State University Institutional Review Board.

Romantic Satisfaction

To measure romantic satisfaction, the seven‐item Relationship Assessment Scale (RAS) was administered to all participants. Agreement with statements, such as “My romantic partner meets my needs,” was rated on a 5‐point scale from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree (α = 0.69). A composite score, ranging from 7 to 35, with higher scores representing greater satisfaction, was calculated for the RAS by summing item scores (M = 32.89, SD = 2.62). Previous research has shown the seven‐item RAS to be reliable and valid (Hendrick et al. 1998).

Depressive Symptoms

Each participant rated their depressive symptoms using the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D; Radloff 1977). This measure, which is widely used to measure depressive symptoms, includes 20 items like “I thought my life had been a failure,” which are rated for frequency within the past week. The 5‐point rating scale (α = 0.87) includes endpoints of 0 = Rarely or none of the time ( 16 indicate clinical levels of depressive symptoms.

Anxiety Symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were rated using the seven‐item anxiety subscale of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS‐A; Zigmond and Snaith 1983). Items, such as “Worrying thoughts go through my mind,” were rated for frequency within the past week on the following 4‐point scale: 0 = Not at all, 1 = From time to time, occasionally, 2 = A lot of the time, and 3 = Most of the time (α = 0.82). A composite score was calculated for the HADS‐A by summing all anxiety subscale items (Zigmond and Snaith 1983). Higher scores represent greater anxiety (M = 7.05, SD = 3.81). Scores > 11 indicate clinical levels of anxiety symptoms.

See Study 1, Measures for a description of the MPM, which was also applied in Study 2 (M = 81.96, SD = 8.66). Internal consistency was good (α = 0.80).

Observations of MP

A team of three students (n = 2 graduate students, n = 1 undergraduate student; 100% female) reviewed recordings of the conflict task and rated observable MP based on the Ratings of Observed Mindful Partnering (ROMP) coding system (Rigsby et al. 2026). The ROMP captures observational ratings of MP through behavioral indicators that are associated with each of the five factors of MP, including (1) mindful awareness, (2) nonreactivity in conflict, (3) emotional awareness, (4) acceptance/compassion, and (5) self‐compassion. For example, an indicator of nonreactivity in conflict is taking a mindful pause, which involves delaying one's response to a partner's words or actions, particularly when feeling upset. Other indicators of nonreactivity are ability to self‐regulate and non‐escalation of negative emotionality. Raters scored active listening, body language, and consideration for partner to capture the mindful awareness factor of MP; specific attempts to understand emotion and responses to emotion that were both verbal and nonverbal to capture the emotional awareness factor of MP; kindness, compassionate understanding, and empathic concern to capture the acceptance/compassion factor of MP; and, common human experience and tolerance of criticism to capture the self‐compassion factor of MP. Mean scores were calculated by factor and then averaged to create a rating of total MP (M = 1.17, SD = 0.46) as outlined in Rigsby et al. (2026). Interrater reliability at this level of analysis was strong (α = 0.89).

Participants reported their age (in years), marital status (married/not married), whether they were cohabitating (cohabitating/not cohabitating), and the length of their relationship (in years). Each of these variables was evaluated as a potential covariate for inclusion in models.

The data analysis plan from Study 1 was repeated for Study 2, with romantic satisfaction in place of dyadic adjustment and anxiety/depressive symptoms in place of well‐being and psychological distress. Additional preliminary analyses were conducted and reported testing observed MP as a standalone predictor of these outcomes due to the novel nature of the coding system utilized, but primary models included observed MP alongside self‐reported MP as predictors, consistent with Heyman (2001) and Weiss and Heyman (2004).

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations for key variables and potential covariates in primary analyses are presented in Supplementary Table B1. Measures of MP were significantly associated with depressive symptoms (negative, small), anxiety symptoms (negative, small), and romantic satisfaction (positive, small‐to‐moderate). No covariates were significantly associated with independent and dependent variables for the proposed analyses; they were not included in the primary analyses. Little's test revealed that missing data were MCAR (χ 2(67) = 53.16, p = 0.891).

An APIM was specified, setting observed MP as the sole predictor of romantic satisfaction. The model was just identified and thus had a perfect fit. Discussion

The goal of these two studies was to examine how actor and partner effects of MP predict important mental and relational health outcomes. We also examined these patterns using both self‐reports and observational assessments of MP in Study 2. Results showed significant actor effects on mental health indicators: individuals' self‐reported MP predicted lower levels of psychological distress and anxiety symptoms for oneself, as well as higher levels of general well‐being; self‐reported and observed MP predicted lower anxiety symptoms (significant) and depressive symptoms (marginal) for oneself; in the preliminary model including only observed MP as a predictor of mental health symptoms, observed MP significantly predicted one's own anxiety symptoms and depressive symptoms. For relational health outcomes, self‐reported MP predicted higher dyadic adjustment in self and partner, as well as higher romantic satisfaction in self and partner. In sum, these studies extend the existing literature on mindfulness in romantic relationships (Kimmes et al. 2020, 2025; Zheng et al. 2025) and support hypotheses that interpersonal mindfulness in romantic dyads may be beneficial for mental and relational health.

Significant actor effects (self‐to‐self) indicated that MP predicts, in beneficial ways, dyadic adjustment, well‐being, psychological distress, romantic satisfaction, and anxiety symptoms. The same may be true for depressive symptoms. These results are consistent with existing literature documenting the benefits of unidimensional interpersonal mindfulness for relational health and general psychological well‐being (Kimmes et al. 2020), and also contribute to the literature a more holistic, five‐factor view of interpersonal mindfulness applied to couples through MP (Seiter et al. 2022). In sensitivity analyses, gender differences emerged such that observed MP predicted anxiety and depressive symptoms for female but not male participants. This pattern may be explained by the ways that mental health symptoms in relationships moderate associations between trait mindfulness and relationship mindfulness differently for men and women. Recent findings suggested that men were less likely to translate their general tendency to be mindful into interpersonal mindfulness when anxiety or depressive symptoms were present in their romantic relationship, either in self or partner (Zheng et al. 2025). Self‐reports of MP may capture beliefs about one's own tendency to be mindful, whereas our observational measure would likely be less influenced by such a bias.

Significant partner effects (partner‐to‐self) indicated that MP of one partner predicts greater dyadic adjustment and romantic satisfaction, but also greater psychological distress and anxiety symptoms, in the other. These beneficial effects of MP on partner dyadic adjustment and romantic satisfaction are consistent with extant literature documenting the positive influences of interpersonal mindfulness on romantic relationship health (Kimmes et al. 2020, 2025). Effects of MP on anxiety symptoms in an unexpected direction may indicate a bidirectional or inverse relationship between a partner's mental health indicators and a tendency to be mindful in the relationship. Although we tested hypotheses framing MP as a predictor of mental health outcomes, recent work found that mental health symptoms present in a romantic relationship (for self or partner) moderated the ability to translate intrapersonal mindfulness into interpersonal mindfulness (Zheng et al. 2025). So, it is possible that MP is more limited when responding to mental health needs already observed in a partner. The current study was limited to cross‐sectional analysis and unidirectional hypotheses, but future work should evaluate MP as a mediator and/or an outcome of mental health indicators to further explore the direction of these associations.

Our pattern of findings suggests that individuals' own mindfulness potentially has particular influence over their own mental health symptoms regardless of measurement strategy, which is consistent with extant literature on intrapersonal and interpersonal mindfulness (Creswell and Lindsay 2014; Dawson et al. 2020; Kimmes et al. 2020; Virgili 2015). Mindfulness and mental health symptoms are associated in a manner that is likely bidirectional for individuals (Rigsby et al. 2024), yet studies and theory point toward mindfulness as the most likely causal antecedent in the individual‐level association (Creswell and Lindsay 2014; Snippe et al. 2015). Work on interpersonal mindfulness, however, illustrates how mental health can modify the application of mindfulness to romantic contexts (Zheng et al. 2025). In Study 2, observed MP predicted one's own mental health symptoms (at marginal or significant levels) across observational‐only models, primary models combining self‐reports with observations, and for women in corresponding sensitivity analyses. Multi‐method assessments, inclusive of multiple measurement strategies, are important, particularly in early exploration of new constructs (Heyman 2001), as self‐reports may be impacted by biases and observational assessments may be more objective in nature. In this case, our findings provide preliminary evidence that the ROMP may improve self‐report measurements of MP in mental health contexts. The ROMP also expands the capabilities of researchers to conduct early tests of the MP construct by using secondary data from completed studies, which included recorded couple discussion tasks.

Limitations

Although the current study makes several important and novel contributions to the literature, there are limitations to consider when interpreting these results. Related to measurement, observed MP was measured using the ROMP system (Rigsby et al. 2026), which was developed using recordings of the standard couple conflict discussion paradigm (Heyman 2001). Although Study 2 utilized the conflict discussion paradigm, relieving concerns that the measurement strategy was not aligned with the task, there are broader concerns that this paradigm limits the interpretation of findings to the context of conflict itself (Weiss and Heyman 2004). Communication is highly context‐dependent (Bodie et al. 2011; Holmstrom et al. 2015), so it is possible that findings related to conflictual contexts would not apply more generally to day‐to‐day interactions in a romantic relationship.

Additionally, the sample sizes for our studies were small compared to other studies of romantic relationship dynamics. Although the sample sizes in both studies met recommendations for minimum sample sizes for simple APIMs in SEM of 80–100 couples (Ledermann and Kenny 2017), our mental health models included multiple predictor variables, introducing additional parameters and further limiting power to detect small effects. Thus, it is possible that other effects exist which we were unable to detect in these analyses. In particular, it would be incorrect, at this stage, to conclude from models that only revealed a significant actor effect that a partner effect does not exist (Kenny and Ledermann 2010), or, due to the possibility of type II error, to conclude from models that only revealed significant effects of self‐reported MP that observed MP does not have an effect. Further testing with larger samples and additional statistical evaluation (e.g., estimation of Kenny and Ledermann's k parameter) is necessary before drawing such conclusions.

Finally, the samples used in the current study are not representative of the larger United States population due to their particularly high percentage of White participants. This pattern likely limits the generalizability of our findings beyond White couples. In Study 2, a large number of couples were also unmarried and in relatively new relationships. The extent to which these findings generalize to longer, more stable relationships is an important direction for future research in this area. It is also important to note that this study relied on a community sample, and mean scores for mental health symptoms were not in the clinical range for anxiety or depression. Thus, it is important to replicate the findings in clinical samples in future work.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The MP construct is theoretically rooted in mindfulness, a well‐supported intervention approach (Creswell 2017; Creswell and Lindsay 2014; Schumer et al. 2018; Tomlinson et al. 2018). Our findings align with recent work supporting the use of MBI for couples (Gambrel 2023; Gambrel and Piercy 2015a, 2015b; Winter et al. 2021) and suggestions to apply interpersonal rather than intrapersonal strategies in these cases (Duncan 2007; Kimmes et al. 2020, 2025; Pratscher et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2025). Considering this new support for MP as associated with mental health symptoms, which extends past support for beneficial outcomes (Rigsby et al. n.d.; Seiter 2022) to include other well‐being and relationship health constructs, we suggest that couples therapists should consider applying MBIs interpersonally in their work with couples. Given successful observations of MP by research assistants (Rigsby et al. 2026), trained clinicians can likely observe behaviors associated with MP and target those behaviors for intervention. The current study suggests that increasing a partner's displayed MP may result in increased dyadic adjustment and romantic satisfaction for the target of a clinical intervention, and that increasing one's own MP may result in decreased levels of mental health symptoms for the target of a clinical intervention. It is also possible that these relations occur in the reverse direction, or have other complex or bidirectional associations (Rigsby et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2025) despite theoretical suggestions to the contrary (Creswell and Lindsay 2014). Thus, future work should directly test hypothesized causal pathways and test potential mechanisms through which MP acts on mental and relational health. Further, future studies should consider the relative strength of effects of the different factors of MP to allow for the development of interventions that target modifiable behaviors with the highest potential impact on outcomes of interest.

Conclusion

This manuscript presents findings from two distinct samples on the role of MP as a predictor of mental and relational health outcomes. Taken together, findings across models support the hypothesis that MP predicts mental (e.g., well‐being, psychological distress, and anxiety/depressive symptoms) and relational (e.g., dyadic adjustment and romantic satisfaction) health outcomes. Interestingly, models that included both self‐report and observational measurements of MP revealed differential predictive patterns, such that self‐reports and observations were associated to varying degrees with mental health symptom outcomes, whereas only self‐reports were associated with romantic satisfaction, underscoring arguments in other work about the importance of incorporating both self‐reports and observational assessments of relationship constructs (Heyman 2001; Weiss and Heyman 2004). Future research should continue to explore this novel construct for potential development into a formalized intervention strategy for couples.
