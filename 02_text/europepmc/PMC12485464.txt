ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated and compared invalidation domains (discounting and lack of understanding) in patients with fibromyalgia (FM) and non-FM chronic musculoskeletal pain. The relationship between invalidation and depression was also investigated to clarify the role of FM.MethodsA total of 207 patients (145 FM and 62 non-FM) completed questionnaires including the Illness Invalidation Inventory (3*I), Widespread Pain Index (WPI), Revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQR), and Beck Depression Inventory-second edition (BDI-II). Adjusted linear regression analyses were performed to assess the association between the 3*I and BDI-II, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to examine the relationships between FM (as the dependent variable) and other variables.ResultsWPI, FIQR, BDI-II, and 3*I scores were significantly higher in FM patients than in non-FM patients. The BDI-II total score was found to be a significant predictor of discounting and lack of understanding stemming from spouse and family sources in both groups, with slightly stronger effects in the non-FM group than in FM patients. In multivariate regression analysis, discounting from family sources (odds ratio [OR] = 1.81, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–3.20, P = 0.040) and the BDI-II total score (OR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.06–1.20, P = 0.001) remained a determinant of having FM.ConclusionsThe higher frequency of invalidation in FM patients is not fully explained by depression because of weaker statistical relationships between invalidation and depression in FM rather than other pain disorders.Keywords: Chronic Pain, Depression, Fibromyalgia, Musculoskeletal Pain, Social Stigma, Surveys and Questionnaires

BODY
INTRODUCTION

Pain is a complex perceptual experience that arises from the interplay of various neural and psychological mechanisms [1]. Cognitive and emotional processing of pain appears to be the essential factors in the establishment of the pain process [2]. Cognitive appraisal of pain and reflective social responses have been known as the modulators of pain perception and maintenance [2,3]. Fibromyalgia (FM) as a chronic state of pain and psychosocial illness appears to have the crucial component of the cognitive-social dimension that integrates closely with physical pain [4]. FM is traditionally defined by diffuse musculoskeletal pain, chronic fatigue, non-restorative sleep, and accompanying somatic symptoms. However, the novel FM symptomatology introduces “social pain or invalidation” as a tantamount complaint to physical pain in FM [5]. The term “invalidation” has been introduced to explain patients’ perception of misunderstanding of their medical condition by social environment [6]. FM often raises a high level of stigmatization, non-acceptance, and suspicion that the pain problem has been exaggerated or derived only from psychological disturbance [7]. Because pain in FM cannot be confirmed by objective physical or laboratory findings, an exaggerated skepticism has persisted about the legitimacy of symptoms [8]. FM patients have often reported facing invalidation from other people, including their family and friends, coworkers, and medical professionals. This could lead to a vicious cycle exacerbating emotional and physical dysregulation [4].

Clinical and neurobiological evidence underscores the strong connection between physical pain and social pain (invalidation) in FM, highlighting their intuitive similarities, shared neural pathways, and overlapping clinical features [9]. Although invalidation has been reported in a variety of painful conditions, FM patients have experienced more frequent and higher intensity invalidation [5,7,10]. In other words, while physical pain from any origin can potentially be associated with invalidation, invalidation appears to be intertwined in the pathogenesis process of FM as an important factor of the sooth-threat imbalance [2]. Indeed, invalidation has been considered as a distinguishing feature of FM among other chronic musculoskeletal pain disorders [5]. However, one important question arises whether the specific role of invalidation in FM stems only from a frequent psychological and depressive status in this condition, or invalidation operates as an independent factor in FM.

Invalidation has been reported to be highly associated with psychological illness and depression [3,11]. The more depressive symptoms are expressed by patients, the more perceived invalidation is recorded [11–15]. The considerable overlap between invalidation and depression has been described in various painful conditions including FM. Clinical practitioners may attribute the frequent disbelief of symptoms in FM patients to the increased prevalence of psychological illness in FM [7]. While the cause-and-effect relationship between invalidation and depression remains unclear, it seems to work both ways. Even so, growing evidence has shown that invalidation may have a fundamental and independent role in FM, irrespective of psychological disturbances. A study revealed that the link between FM and invalidation persists even when personality traits such as emotional stability and neuroticism are included in a multivariable regression model [7]. Supporting the independent role of psychological illness and invalidation in FM is the ineffectiveness of antidepressants and FM-approved treatments in the improvement of invalidation scores. Indeed, these drugs have been reported to not affect invalidation, irrespective of the drug type, as well as being ineffective in the alleviation of physical pain and depression [16].

This study aimed to assess and compare the invalidation domains, specifically discounting and lack of understanding, in patients with FM and those with non-FM chronic musculoskeletal pain. Additionally, it explored the relationship between invalidation and depression in both groups to better understand the role of having FM on the invalidation-depression relationships.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was conducted at the rheumatology clinic of Razi Hospital, affiliated with Guilan University of Medical Sciences (GUMS), from July 2018 to March 2019. Participants were consecutively enrolled by one expert rheumatologist interested in the FM and pain field. To control for confounding variables, only female individuals were included in the study. The FM group comprised participants diagnosed based on the 2016 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria. The non-FM chronic musculoskeletal pain group included subjects without a diagnosis of FM at the time of enrollment. Patients in this study were diagnosed with chronic, non-inflammatory pain conditions, including osteoarthritis, tendinitis (notably rotator cuff tendinitis and medial or lateral epicondylitis), and regional pain syndromes such as neck pain, low back pain, and myofascial pain. All participants had not undergone treatment for FM or pain management before enrollment. Chronic pain was defined as a pain syndrome lasting more than 3 months.

Individuals were excluded if they had systemic inflammatory rheumatic disorders, malignancies, or previously identified confounding non-rheumatic pain causes such as neuropathy, as well as social or educational obstacles to cooperation at the time of enrolment.

Every participant completed the written consent form. The research received approval from the GUMS ethics committee (IR.GUMS.REC.1399.231) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

1. Instruments

1) Demographic questionnaire

The questionnaire included demographic information such as age, marital status, employment status, and educational background.

2) Widespread Pain Index

Widespread Pain Index (WPI), as a component of the 2016 ACR criteria, calculates the count of painful segments (score range = 0–19) of body parts over the last week before the assessment [17].

3) Revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire

The Revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQR) was employed to assess disease severity and its impact. It consists of 21 individual numeric rating questions across three domains: function, overall impact, and symptoms. The total FIQR score is derived from the sum of the modified scores for each domain [18].

4) Beck Depression Inventory-second edition

The Beck Depression Inventory-second edition (BDI-II) consists of 21 questions, each rated on a scale of 0 to 3 (total score range = 0–63). Higher scores reflect more severe depressive symptoms experienced during the 2 weeks prior to the assessment. The questions are often grouped into the two subcategories “somatic” (13 items) and “cognitive-affective” (8 items). The subcategory scores are derived by totaling the specific items within the 21 symptom questions [19].

5) Illness Invalidation Inventory

The Illness Invalidation Inventory (3*I) is a valid tool for measuring the amount of invalidation felt by patients from different social sources. In this study, the authors selected three sources, namely, the spouse, family, and medical professionals, as the most important social connections. Each source includes eight Likert-scale items, with responses ranging from “Never” to “Very often” (1 to 5 points). A greater number indicates a greater level of invalidation. The 3*I assesses two aspects of invalidation: discounting and lack of understanding. The “discounting dimension” refers to negative social reactions that reflect rejection in personal interactions, while the “lack of understanding dimension” indicates a deficiency in positive social responses, highlighting a lack of social support [20].

All instruments, including the ACR 2016, FIQR, BDI-II, and the 3*I had been previously translated and validated in the Persian language [18–21].

2. Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined to evaluate the correlation between the Invalidation Questionnaire score and the BDI score in patients with FM, based on a pilot study involving 50 participants. A confidence interval (CI) of 95% (α = 0.05) and a statistical power of 90% (β = 0.10) were assumed. The correlation coefficient was r = 0.297, derived from the pilot study in the family domain, and the number of variables considered in the model was P = 6, where P represents the total number of independent variables in the model, including covariates to control for potential confounding effects.

Based on this calculation, the initial sample size was estimated to be 176. Assuming a 15% dropout rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 207.

3. Statistical analysis

Chi-square and independent t-tests were applied to compare the demographic qualitative and quantitative variables between the FM and non-FM groups, respectively. The normal distribution of data in both groups was assessed using the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The z test was also used to compare the invalidation-depression correlations between the FM and non-FM groups. Adjusted linear regression analyses between the invalidation domains from different sources, and the total BDI-II score (after adjustment for age, education level, work status, and WPI) were also used. Additionally, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to investigate the relationships between FM (as the dependent variable) and other variables. P values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. The analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Co.).

A total of 207 patients were consecutively recruited for this study, comprising 145 patients with FM and 62 patients with non-FM chronic pain. All participants were female. DISCUSSION

The results of this study indicated that invalidation scores were higher in the FM group than non-FM chronic pain group. The authors also found some significant correlations between the invalidation dimensions from spouse and family sources and depression scales in the FM and non-FM groups. Using linear regression analysis and after adjustment for pain and demographic variables, the overall B coefficient values between the invalidation and depression scores were slightly lower in the FM group than in the non-FM group. Furthermore, comparison of these correlations between the different severity groups of FM (mild, moderate, severe) and non-FM group showed that the relationships between invalidation and depression at the different severity groups of FM are not stronger than this relationship in the non-FM group. These findings indicate that invalidation correlates with depression in painful conditions other than FM but having FM does not augment this relationship. Multivariate analysis also revealed the discounting perceived from the family source and depression as the independent determinants of FM.

These findings displayed the relationships between the invalidation dimensions and depression domains regardless of FM diagnosis. The weaker relationships despite the higher scores of depression and invalidation in the FM group rather than the non-FM group can align with the authors’ primary hypothesis that the high perceived invalidation by FM patients may be not related to the depressive status of these patients. In the current viewpoint, a higher and more specific presentation of invalidation in FM is thought to be related to a higher prevalence of depression and psychological disturbances potentially associated with FM [22,23]. However, the study did not support this idea. Indeed, it seems that invalidation plays an independent and important role in FM, irrespective of depression.

This is the first study to seek the relationships between invalidation and depression in the patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain with and without FM diagnosis. Mounting evidence shows the neurobiological and clinical connections among physical pain, social pain, and depression [2,3,9,24,25]. The mutual interactions among these three domains could lead to a vicious cycle perpetuating pain (physical or social) and exacerbating depression [9,25]. FM is a unique condition with the prominent components of both generalized physical pain and invalidation [4]. The intertwined effects of pain, depression, and invalidation make it hard to differentiate which one has an original role in FM. Although pain and depression are usually considered the original players of FM [2,26], the authors’ findings support an independent role for invalidation in FM, irrespective of depression.

The previous studies have confirmed the importance of invalidation in the diagnosis and treatment of FM [5,16], irrespective of physical pain or depression. However, there has been no study to examine and compare the invalidation-depression relationships between patients with and without FM. The present study found that the higher frequency of invalidation in FM patients is not related to existing depression because of the weaker statistical relationships between invalidation and depression in these patients rather than other non-FM chronic musculoskeletal pain disorders. Regarding the results from the adjusted linear regression analyses, the depression could predict the invalidation by the different sources in both the FM and non-FM groups, although this prediction was weaker in FM patients rather than non-FM (lower β [egression coefficients], Table 3). Furthermore, the different invalidation dimensions and sources correlated weaker with the depression scales in the different severity groups of FM compared to non-FM patients (lower r value [correlation coefficients], Table 4). The present results were congruent with the Santiago et al. [7] study which showed that the relationship between FM and invalidation persists after the inclusion of personality variables in the multivariate model. In another mean, FM remained as a determinant of invalidation, independently from psychological traits.

It seems that the triple component (pain-depression-invalidation) is also operative in many painful conditions other than FM [4,7,8]. The chronic pain disorders without nociplastic components (e.g., non-FM group) have shown a lower frequency of invalidation [5,7] and a simpler relation with depression; higher depression associates with high invalidation and vice versa. Contrary in FM, the triple component’s relationships are not simple and predictable. The discounting from the family source, one of the most important sources of invalidation, remained as a determinant of having FM besides depression after adjustment for other factors. It indicates the importance of focusing on invalidation regardless of depression in FM.

Interestingly, the invalidation stemming from medical professionals was lower than other sources. The authors also did not find any important relationships between invalidation by medical professionals and depression in the multiple analyses. It may be related to paying more careful attention as well as the interpretation of pain and the feelings of patients by health professionals. Invalidation perception from this source may be different with other sources [7,18]. Furthermore, the lower invalidation experience stemming from medical professionals may reflect a response bias of the studied participants.

This study has a few limitations. First, only female patients were included in both groups. Also, patients were selected in a tertiary care setting with a limited sample size. Consequently, the findings cannot be generalized to the broader population of FM patients. The evaluations used self-reported instruments. The appropriate method for guiding participants in relation to the intended content of questionnaire questions remains unclear. Furthermore, it is not uncertain about the roles of cultural factors of patients in expressing their emotions, attitudes, and invalidation experiences. For the sake of the effect of medications, including antidepressants, on the results, the authors selected naive patients before starting treatment. However, they were unaware of the medications taken by participants before enrolling the study.

FM has a substantial psychosocial complexity and appears to be markedly influenced by emotional and cognitive imbalance in the context of social factors. Indeed, it has been shown that interpersonal rejection and other forms of social disconnection result in hyperactivation of threat systems and underactivation of soothing systems, and, finally, elicit the biological responses underlying FM symptoms [2]. Therefore, it would be reasonable to preserve invalidation or social pain as an intuitional feature of FM which needs dedicated attention. Acknowledgment of the independent roles of invalidation in FM diathesis, diagnosis, and management is essential for successful and pragmatic care of FM patients.

Invalidation stemming from spouse and family were perceived higher in the FM rather than non-FM patients. The higher frequency of invalidation in FM patients is not fully explained by depression because of weaker statistical relationships between invalidation and depression in FM than in non-FM chronic pain patients. This study supports the independence of invalidation from depression in the FM group and warrants dedicated attention to invalidation in this disorder.
