ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the relationship of changes in the meibomian glands as visualized through meibography with demographic characteristics and ocular surface parameters.MethodsThis prospective study included a total of 155 participants. Tear breakup time (TBUT) and Schirmer test results were recorded for each participant, and symptoms were assessed using the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI). Meibographic imaging of the upper and lower eyelids was performed on all individuals. The rates of meibomian gland loss were manually measured by the same experienced specialist, and morphological changes in the meibomian glands (distortion, tortuosity, thinning, thickening, shortening, hooked appearance, dropout, overlapping, ghost appearance, and abnormal gaps) were examined.ResultsAmong the cases, 83 (53.5%) were female and 72 (46,5%) were male, with a mean age of 41.50 ± 20.2 years. The most frequently observed morphological change in the meibomian glands was distortion (67.9%), followed by shortening (57.7%) and increased tortuosity (53.8%). Ghost morphology was the least commonly observed. Patients with more than two morphological changes in the meibomian glands had significantly shorter TBUT (more than two morphological changes; 6.18 ± 2.22 s vs. more than one morphological change; 8.98 ± 2.17 s) and significantly higher OSDI scores (33.18 ± 7.53 vs. 14.36 ± 5.11) compared to others (both p < 0.05). The gland loss rates in both the upper and lower lids showed a significant negative correlation with TBUT (r = -0.278, p < 0.05) and a significant positive correlation with OSDI and age (r = 0.343, p < 0.01; r = 0.701, p < 0.05, respectively).ConclusionThis study demonstrated morphological changes and gland loss in the meibomian glands of the young population. Meibography is a non-invasive and reproducible test that provides detailed information regarding the correlation between dry eye symptoms/signs and morphological changes.

BODY
Introduction

The meibomian glands (MGs) secrete lipids into the tear film and form a superficial lipid layer that ensures the stability of the tear film. MG dysfunction (MGD) is defined as a chronic and widespread abnormality of the MGs, commonly characterized by terminal duct obstruction and/or qualitative or quantitative changes in glandular secretions [1]. As indicated in the TFOS DEWS (Tear Film & Ocular Surface Society Dry Eye Workshop) III report, MGD accounts for 50–70% of dry eye disease and causes tear film instability and evaporative dry eye [2]. It is often associated with symptoms of irritation, an unstable tear film, damage to the ocular surface epithelium, contact lens intolerance, and giant papillary conjunctivitis [3].

Objective assessment of MG morphology and function is crucial for evaluating the severity of MGD and the effects of therapeutic interventions. To objectively identify MGD, techniques such as biomicroscopic examination of the lid margin, meibometry, assessment of meibum quality and quantity, and meibography may be employed. However, although these techniques provide information about MG morphology, they are not widely adopted in clinical practice due to their time-consuming nature and patient discomfort [4].

Meibography was first described by Tapie [5]. Initially, meibography was performed using infrared film and was considered both costly and time-intensive. Nevertheless, with the development of video meibography, this expensive and time-consuming procedure has evolved into a low-cost, noninvasive, and rapid method for evaluating the anatomy of the MGs during clinical examinations [6]. Noninvasive, contactless infrared meibography offers the ability to assess the structure and various morphological changes of the MGs with minimal patient discomfort. This method has increasingly been adopted in clinical practice due to its ability to rapidly reveal MG morphology [7]. The TFOS DEWS III report emphasizes that meibography enables the ‘direct’ visualization of meibomian gland morphology, highlighting the role this feature plays in its wider application within diagnostic processes [8]. Previous research evaluating the reliability of meibography in the morphological assessment of the meibomian glands has demonstrated that inter-examiner and inter-rater reproducibility is satisfactory and that a high level of concordance is achieved among observers [9, 10]. However, this technique is limited to the quantitative analysis of the morphological structure and deficient areas of the MGs, and the relationship between the obtained images and patients’ clinical symptoms has not yet been fully elucidated.

Although MGD has been reported to be associated with age and sex, there is still a lack of sufficient studies that thoroughly examine the morphological changes occurring in the glands. This study aimed to investigate the relationships of MG loss rates and morphological changes with demographic characteristics and dry eye parameters. The results of this study are expected to shed light on the diagnosis and treatment of dry eye, a multifactorial disease.

Materials and methods

Participants

The study included patients presenting to the ophthalmology clinic from 02/2024 to 09/2024. Within the scope of this prospective study, 155 eyes from 155 participants were evaluated. Only one eye from each participant was included in the study, and the selected eye was determined using a random number sequence (a dichotomous sequence of 0 and 1). Of the 155 cases, 83 were female and 72 were male.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for those younger than 18 years, consent was obtained from their parents. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Yozgat Bozok University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent.

Assessment and follow-up

All participants underwent a general ophthalmologic and medical history evaluation. The MGs were objectively assessed using slit-lamp biomicroscopy and infrared meibography. In addition, tear breakup time (TBUT) was measured, and the Schirmer I test was performed under anesthesia. Patient symptoms were evaluated using the Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI).

For TBUT measurement, a fluorescein strip was moistened with preservative-free balanced saline solution. The strip was then applied to the inferior conjunctival fornix, and the participants were instructed to blink three times. Subsequently, they were asked to look straight ahead without blinking. Using a biomicroscope with a cobalt blue filter, the tear film was examined, and the time interval between the last blink and the first appearance of tear film breakup was recorded. To assess basal tear secretion with the Schirmer test, a topical anesthetic was instilled into the inferior conjunctival fornix, and excess moisture was blotted. A Schirmer test strip was placed in the inferotemporal fornix. The amount of wetting on the strip was recorded after a waiting period of five minutes. A 10-minute interval was maintained between the two tests.

The OSDI is a validated, self-reported questionnaire designed to assess symptoms of ocular surface disease, particularly dry eye disease, and the effect of these symptoms on patients’ quality of daily life. The questionnaire consists of 12 items that evaluate symptoms affecting quality of life over the past week. These items fall under three main categories: ocular symptoms, difficulties in visual tasks, and discomfort due to environmental triggers. OSDI scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms and greater impact from ocular surface disease. The OSDI score is categorized as follows: 0–12, normal; 13–22, mild dry eye; 23–32, moderate dry eye; and 33 or above, severe dry eye.

Objective imaging of the MGs

Meibography was performed on all participants to assess MG morphology and to identify glandular loss. Meibographic images were obtained by the same experienced technician using the Sirius Meibography Module (Sirius Topographer; CSO, Florence, Italy). After proper positioning of the patient’s chin and forehead, the patient was instructed to look straight ahead. The participants were asked to blink completely just prior to measurement, after which the imaging was conducted. For the lower eyelid, the procedure was carried out with an upward gaze while the lid was everted, while for the upper eyelid, it was performed with a downward gaze and lid eversion. The central five MGs in the upper and lower lids were morphologically examined. Alterations in both eyelids were recorded. In cases of low-quality scans where irregularities or motion artifacts were detected, an additional scan was performed. If the quality of the repeated scan remained insufficient, the participant was excluded from the study.

A physician, blinded to the patient’s presenting complaints, determined the MG loss rate in the upper and lower eyelids using the manual measurement tools of the Sirius Meibography Module, as previously described [11]. The same physician also evaluated and recorded morphological changes in the MGs, including distortion, tortuosity, thinning, thickening, hooked appearance, dropout, shortening, overlapping, ghost appearance, and abnormal gaps (Fig. 1). To assess intra-observer reliability, all images were classified by the same observer on two occasions separated by a two-week interval, and agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa statistic.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 22.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as relative frequencies for categorical variables. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The χ² test was used to compare categorical variables. Associations between meibomian gland morphological features and TBUT, Schirmer test results, and OSDI scores were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Among the 155 evaluated samples, the most frequently observed morphological change in the MGs was distortion (67.9%), followed by shortening (57.7%) and increased tortuosity (53.8%). A hooked appearance was detected in 39.7% and dropout in 31.4% of the patients. Thickening and thinning were observed in 32.1% and 30.1% of the cases, respectively. Other anomalies, such as irregular morphology and abnormal gaps, were reported in 46.8% and 26.9% of the cases, respectively. Discussion

Meibography is a rapid and straightforward method for evaluating MG morphology in patients with ocular surface disorders. The current study demonstrated that dry eye symptoms and findings correlated with morphological changes in the MGs, with the strongest correlations observed in the presence of gland shortening and failure to reach the lid margin. Moreover, while age-related morphological alterations and MG loss were observed, such changes were also detected in the first and second decades of life.

In the studies undertaken by Kim et al. and Pult, it was reported that even among healthy individuals, MG morphology showed substantial variability and that MG loss could be observed even in children and adolescents [12, 13]. Similarly, our study revealed that in addition to MG loss, morphological changes increased with age; however, such changes were also present in some individuals under the age of 20.

It has been suggested that early MG loss is initially tolerated and that functional changes emerge as morphological degeneration progresses [12]. Rico-del-Viejo et al. found that MG loss correlated with ocular findings only in individuals with advanced gland atrophy and reported increased tear osmolarity and ocular surface staining when the loss exceeded 50% [14]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that increases in morphological alterations and MG loss were associated with exacerbated clinical symptoms and signs.

In our study, MG loss was found to increase with advancing age in both female and male participants. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between females and males regarding age-related MG loss. Morphological evaluation revealed that shortening and thinning of the glands were more prominent in females, whereas thickening and the formation of abnormal gaps were more frequently observed in males. In a study by Pult et al., the MG loss rates between females and males were similar to those in our study; however, no morphological analysis was conducted in that investigation [15].

In a study employing semi-automated software such as ImageJ to analyze MG loss, Pult and Riede-Pult noted that despite approximating automation, observer input was still required to delineate gland areas manually, which resulted in inter-observer variability [16]. Using such software, the authors reported that MG loss area in a group of 17 participants was significantly negatively correlated with noninvasive TBUT and positively correlated with OSDI. Similarly, another study identified a weak but statistically significant correlation between MG loss area and OSDI in individuals with MGD [17]. In contrast, Eom et al. did not detect any correlation between MG loss and either TBUT or Schirmer test results in both healthy individuals and those with MGD [18]. In our study, however, the loss ratios of both upper and lower eyelid MGs were found to be significantly negatively correlated with TBUT and significantly positively correlated with OSDI and age. These findings indicate that MG loss is associated with decreased tear film stability and increased ocular surface symptoms and becomes more pronounced with advancing age. Our study aligns with findings in the existing literature and further emphasizes the diagnostic and clinical relevance of MG loss.

It has been suggested that morphological features of the MGs may be valuable in the assessment of MGD [19]. Nevertheless, studies examining the effect of MG morphology on gland function, tear film characteristics, and ocular surface health remain limited. One of the strengths of our study lies in its comprehensive analysis of 13 distinct morphological changes in the MGs and their correlation with TBUT, Schirmer test results, and OSDI. Our findings revealed a significant positive correlation between distortion and OSDI, a significant negative correlation between shortening and TBUT, and a significant negative correlation between shortening and the Schirmer test result. In addition, there was a strong positive correlation between failure to reach the lid margin and OSDI. In a study by Ban et al., the lengths of five central MGs were evaluated using ImageJ software in a healthy population. The findings demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between gland length and expressibility; however, in contrast to the study by Llorens-Quintana et al. [14] no correlation with fluorescein TBUT was found [20]. In general, our study showed that morphological changes such as distortion, shortening, and failure to reach the lid margin were significantly associated with both tear film parameters and subjective symptoms.

Pult et al. noted that while gland width might contribute to evaluating MG status, the most important factor remained the area of gland loss [15]. Similarly, in our study, both upper and lower eyelid MG loss rates showed significant negative correlations with TBUT and significant positive correlations with OSDI and age. Furthermore, our findings indicated that thinning, thickening, and thinning appearance in the MGs did not exhibit statistically significant correlations with TBUT, Schirmer test results, or OSDI. In another study, the mean width of all glands showed a weak but statistically significant correlation with lid margin thickness, TBUT, and conjunctival staining [14].

There are only a limited number of studies evaluating MG morphology in the healthy general population [21, 22]. In a study that aimed to assess MG irregularity or tortuosity, tortuosity was only subjectively classified as “present” or “absent,” and no analysis was conducted regarding its potential impact on MG function or ocular surface condition [23]. In our study, no significant association was found between tortuosity with the TBUT, Schirmer test result and OSDI. Therefore, we believe that tortuosity should be evaluated in conjunction with MG loss percentage in order to be considered a reliable metric. The findings suggest that tortuosity does not have a comprehensive effect on tear film stability or ocular surface health. In other words, the impact of tortuosity on tear film stability appears to be limited. We propose that as long as the gland is not completely lost, it may continue to function despite some alterations in lipid secretion and composition, resulting in only minimal changes in tear film properties. Previous studies have shown that variations in meibum composition contribute to the pathogenesis of MGD [24, 25].

In our study, the loss rates and morphological characteristics of the MGs in both the upper and lower eyelids were comprehensively evaluated. Most previous studies in the literature have primarily focused on lower eyelid meibography [26, 27]. However, it has been reported that the upper eyelid contains a greater number of MGs than the lower eyelid, that the length of individual glands corresponds to the length of the tarsal plate, and that the glands in the upper eyelid are longer than those in the lower eyelid [28]. Additionally, it is believed that the upper eyelid contributes more significantly to meibum production compared to the lower eyelid [29]. Despite this, clinical evaluations of MG loss in patients with MGD typically favor assessment of the lower eyelid, mainly due to the ease of eversion and reduced discomfort experienced by patients during imaging [30]. However, the relative contributions of the upper and lower eyelid MGs to the tear film and ocular surface health have not yet been fully elucidated. By successfully visualizing the MGs of both the upper and lower eyelids, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the anatomical and functional differences between these regions.

In their study, Srinivasan et al. graded meibography images using both subjective and digital methods and demonstrated that this approach was simple and feasible [23]. In contrast, Pult et al. advocated for computer-assisted grading methods as being more advanced and appropriate [16]. In a study employing artificial intelligence–based analysis of meibography images, it was suggested that this approach could assist clinicians in evaluating images more rapidly and with higher accuracy. However, the authors also emphasized that the current dataset was insufficient, which represented a major limitation for further model development [31]. While such software may support certain analytical steps, the outcomes remain partially dependent on the subjective judgment of the observer, which can lead to variability. In our study, the MGs were manually segmented, allowing for a more objective evaluation of gland loss. Nonetheless, further research is needed to establish appropriate cut-off values for digital or computer-assisted analysis of these images.

Previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of MG loss among older individuals, along with increased morphological changes associated with aging [32, 33]. In a histopathological investigation by Obata, various morphological alterations were identified in the MGs, leading to the conclusion that these changes form the morphological basis of MGD in elderly populations [34]. A more recent study utilizing meibography to examine MGD risk factors also highlighted advanced age as a significant risk factor for MG loss [35]. In our study, the finding that both upper and lower eyelid MG loss rates showed a strong positive correlation with age supports the notion that MG loss is directly associated with the aging process and is consistent with the existing literature. The slightly higher correlation observed in the upper eyelid may suggest that aging exerts a somewhat more pronounced effect on the upper lid.

In a study examining meibomian gland loss in both the upper and lower eyelids of contact lens wearers, consistent with our findings, a correlation was observed between gland loss and OSDI scores; however, no significant association was found between morphological changes and contact lens wear [36]. In another study investigating the effect of contact lens material on meibomian gland loss, rigid materials were reported to be more traumatic to the glands; in line with our findings, dry eye symptoms were shown to correlate with the degree of meibomian gland loss [37]. In our study, patients using contact lenses were excluded to eliminate potential adverse effects on the meibomian glands, thereby aiming to obtain more objective data.

One of the key strengths of our study is its detailed investigation of MG morphology in relation to age. A particularly noteworthy finding is the observation of morphological changes not only in older individuals but also among those under 30 years of age. While previous studies have predominantly focused on the lower eyelid, our study objectively analyzed both upper and lower eyelid MG loss, as well as 13 distinct morphological alterations, through manual segmentation. Moreover, by exploring the relationships between these morphological features and TBUT, Schirmer test results, and OSDI scores, our study offers important insight into the effects of MG structure on tear film and ocular surface health. Nevertheless, our study has several notable limitations. Although the single-center design may be considered a methodological constraint, the relatively large sample size compared to similar studies partially mitigates this issue. The selection of participants exclusively from a tertiary care center introduces the potential for selection bias. In the pediatric population, complete eyelid eversion could not always be achieved, which limited the visualization of MGs in the far periphery and prevented accurate morphological assessment of those glands. Furthermore, there are significant anatomical differences between the upper and lower eyelids. Although strong correlations between upper and lower eyelid glands have been reported in the literature, such anatomical variations must be taken into consideration in future studies. while the present study conducted a detailed evaluation of MG morphology, the analysis was limited to the five central glands of the upper and lower eyelids. This limitation prevents comprehensive interpretation of morphological changes across the entire gland population. Therefore, there is a need for advanced studies encompassing wider regions and a greater number of glands. Although our study utilized a single observer, the impact of this limitation may have been partially mitigated by the use of standardized morphological definitions, the conduct of all evaluations under masked conditions, and the assessment of intra-observer reliability—where all images were re-classified by the same observer after a two-week interval, with agreement evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa statistic.

In conclusion, advanced age results in morphological changes and MG loss. The prevalence of these morphological changes differs between females and males. Among the morphological changes, gland shortening and failure to reach the lid margin were found to be most strongly associated with dry eye symptoms and findings. Meibography is a noninvasive, easily administered test that provides detailed information for evaluating dry eye and the structure of the MGs.
