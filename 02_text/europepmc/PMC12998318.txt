ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo adapt the response format and validate the factor structure of the Spanish SELSA-S for use with older adults.ResultsA total of 147 older adults (127 female; age range 65 to 90 years) participated in the study, all of whom lived independently in their homes. Of these, 11% were classified as socially isolated. The sample included 62 participants (42%) from urban areas and 85 (58%) from rural areas of the Pyrenees in La Rioja, Spain. The SELSA-S scale showed variable internal consistency, with alpha and omega coefficients ranging from 0.54 to 0.95 across models and subscales. Convergent and discriminant validity were supported by expected correlation patterns with other loneliness measures and unrelated constructs. No significant associations were found between SELSA-S scores and age. Among the tested models, the original three-factor structure, distinguishing family, social, and romantic loneliness, showed the best fit, despite an elevated RMSEA and negative loadings for two items in the romantic domain. Inter-domain correlations revealed moderate associations between family and romantic loneliness, while social loneliness remained largely independent. These findings support the SELSA-S as a suitable tool for assessing multidimensional loneliness in older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07699-5.

BODY
Introduction

Loneliness in older adults is a complex phenomenon with significant implications for physical, emotional, and social health [1]. Despite growing interest in its assessment, most scales used in research and clinical settings with older adults tend either to conceptualize loneliness as a global experience or to focus exclusively on its social dimension [2]. While this approach is useful, it overlooks a critical aspect: emotional loneliness, understood as the absence of intimate and meaningful bonds [3]. Recent longitudinal evidence further underscores the relevance of this issue, showing that loneliness in later life is dynamically associated with cognitive and health‑related outcomes [4, 5].

Currently, the only instrument that distinguishes between different types of loneliness, including emotional, is the Social and Emotional Loneliness Scale for Adults – Short Form (SELSA-S) [6]. However, the SELSA-S has not been specifically developed for use in older adult populations. In fact, Thomson and Pollet carried out the only study that has applied the SELSA-S in this age group [2]. Their findings suggest that the SELSA-S captures differentiated dimensions of loneliness (social, family, and romantic), but the study did not include structural validation or cultural adaptation for older adults. A Spanish version of the SELSA-S has been validated in the general population [7], demonstrating high reliability across diverse cultural contexts and demographic groups, including university students [8–12], healthcare professionals and workers [13, 14], and the general adult population [15–22]. To date, this version has been used in five studies with older adults [16, 23–26]. Two reduced the number of items and Likert-type response options [23, 24], but none reported psychometric validity analysis (for more details see Supplementary File 1). Moreover, recent evidence shows that social and emotional loneliness in older adults present differentiated associations with physiological stress markers, such as diurnal cortisol patterns [25], further underscoring the need for instruments capable of distinguishing between loneliness dimensions.

Given that older adults are particularly vulnerable to the negative effects of loneliness [27] adequately understanding the different dimensions that comprise it may be of interest for the development of interventions aimed at addressing the issue. To achieve such understanding, the first requirement is the availability of tools that allow for the accurate measurement of the construct. The present study aims to address this gap by adapting the response format of the Spanish SELSA-S and validating its factor structure in a sample of older adults. This approach preserves the original item wording while assessing the instrument’s dimensional consistency and suitability for this population.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted from October to December 2024 with older adults living in private homes across adjacent urban and rural areas of the Pyrenees in La Rioja, Spain. Rural areas were defined as settlements with less than 300 inhabitants/km² [28]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation. Data were obtained via face-to-face interviews in community centres, following written informed consent and a protocol approved by the “Comité de Ética de Investigación con medicamentos de La Rioja” (CEImLAR), an independent ethics committee (Ref. CEImLAR-PI-795).

Participants and eligibility

Participants were required to be native Spanish speakers, older than 64 years, and residing in a private home in the La Rioja region to be eligible for inclusion. Individuals living in nursing homes, presenting cognitive impairment, or exhibiting total or severe physical dependency were excluded. The Mini Examen Cognoscitivo (MEC) [29], the Spanish adaptation of the MMSE [30], and the Barthel Index (BI) [31] were used as screening tests for cognitive impairment and dependency, respectively. Participants scoring below 25 on the MEC or below 61 on the BI were excluded. Participants were approached through community centres and municipal programmes for older adults, where staff informed them about the study and invited them to participate voluntarily.

Based on recommendations for confirmatory factor analysis (5–10 participants per parameter) [32, 33], and considering the 15-item structure of the SELSA-S, a minimum sample of 135 fully completed responses was targeted to ensure psychometric robustness and model stability.

Main measures

Loneliness measures

The SELSA-S (15 items) is a multidimensional scale designed to assess loneliness in adults [6]. It comprises three subscales (social, family, and romantic loneliness), each consisting of five items, which allows for both global and domain-specific assessments. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). To enhance clarity among older adults, the original endpoints were replaced in this study by “no” and “yes”, respectively. A sample item of the family domain is “I feel alone when I am with my family” (item 1). For the romantic domain, an example is “I wish I had a more satisfying romantic relationship” (item 10). For the social domain, a representative item is “I do not have any friends who understand me, but I wish I did” (item 13).

The Escala de Soledad Social ESTE-II, referred as ESTE-II (15 items), is an instrument developed for measuring social loneliness in older adults [34]. Items of the ESTE-II are answered using a 3-points frequency scale from 0 (always) to 2 (never).

The Three-Item Loneliness Scale, referred as TIL (3 items), is a brief instrument for assessment of overall loneliness in older adults [35, 36]. Items of the TIL are answered using a 3-points frequency scale from 1 (hardly ever) to 3 (often).

In all three instruments, higher scores indicate greater perceived loneliness.

Other measures

In addition to loneliness measures, Spanish validated versions of the Geriatric Depression Scale, GDS-15 (15 items), the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory, GAI-20 (20 items), and the Satisfaction With Life Scale, SWLS (5 items), were used to assess depression [37, 38], anxiety [39, 40], and life satisfaction [41, 42], respectively. To explore happiness and sadness as affective states, participants also responded to two questions: “Do you feel happy in general?”, from 0 (not at all happy) to 10 (completely happy), previously validated as the Self-Rating Happiness Scale (SRH) [43]; and “How much sadness do you feel?”, from 0 (not at all sad) to 10 (completely sad). Social isolation was also assessed using a UK Biobank tool including three yes/no (1/0) items on cohabitation, social contact, and participation in social activities. Scores range from 0 to 3. Values above 1 indicated social isolation [44, 45]. Finally, information regarding age, sex, marital status, cohabiting, having children, education level, and main living environment (urban or rural) was collected during the interviews as it is shown in Supplementary File 2.

Data analysis

Internal consistency

To evaluate the internal consistency of the scale Average Variance Extracted (AVE), Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients were computed. For alpha and omega, coefficients equal or higher than 0.70 were considered satisfactory [46], and for AVE values equal or higher than 0.5 were considered satisfactory [47].

Criterion-related validity

Convergent validity was examined using the ESTE-II and TIL. The GDS-15, GAI-20, SWLS, age, SRH and a single-item question about sadness were used to assess criterion validity. The GDS-15, GAI-20 and single item sadness measure were expected to show a significant and positive correlation with the SELSA-S. In contrast, the SWLS and SRH were expected to correlate significantly and negatively with the SELSA-S. Finally, based on previously reported evidence [48], no significant correlation was expected between age and SELSA-S scores. As none of the loneliness scales met the assumption of normality, Spearman correlations were used to study these relationships.

Construct validity assessment

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the dimensional structure of the SELSA-S. Three models were tested: (1) a one-factor structure (overall loneliness); (2) a two-factor structure that merges the family and romantic items into a single emotional factor while keeping the social items separate under a second factor; and (3) a three-factor model that preserves the original structure. Model fit was assessed using two criteria: (1) statistical fitness, based on the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), with values of CFI and TLI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.10, and SRMR < 0.10 considered indicative of acceptable fit [49]; and (2) theoretical meaningfulness. CFAs were performed using polychoric correlations (ordinal measurement) and the diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimator with robust standard errors due to lack of multivariate normality. Discriminant validity between factors was assessed using the Heterotrait–Monotrait Ratio of Correlations (HTMT), a criterion shown to outperform traditional approaches [50]. HTMT values < 0.85 indicated strong discriminant validity, and values < 0.90 were considered acceptable in social sciences.

All abovementioned analyses were performed in R language and programming environment for statistical and graphical analysis, RStudio version 2024 for Windows.

Results

Participants

Of 154 individuals initially contacted, seven were excluded in the screening tests (six in the MEC and one in the BI), resulting in a final sample of 147 participants (127 female; M = 72 years, SD = 6.2, age range 65–90). Of these, 61 (41%) were currently married. A full description is provided in Supplementary File 3.

Validation of the SELSA-S

The SELSA-S scale showed variable internal consistency, with alpha and omega coefficients ranging from 0.54 to 0.95 across models and subscales. The correlation analysis supported convergent, criterion and discriminant validity criteria for the SELSA-S scale (see Table 2). Inspection of the correlation matrix revealed that the SELSA-S scores correlate according to expectations with most variables, exhibiting the expected direction and a sufficient magnitude that ensures the statistical significance of the relationships. As expected, none of the SELSA-S scores showed a significant correlation with age. Regarding the SELSA-S domains, in the three factor model, social loneliness was positively correlated with family loneliness (ρ = 0.46; p < 0.001), and with romantic loneliness (ρ = 0.17; p = 0.04). Family and romantic loneliness also showed a moderate positive correlation (ρ = 0.30; p < 0.001). Discriminant validity was supported by adequate HTMT values, all of which were well below recommended thresholds, with romantic vs. social (HTMT = 0.36), romantic vs. family (HTMT = 0.42), and family vs. social (HTMT = 0.68), confirming strong discriminant validity among the three SELSA-S domains.

Of the three models tested, the three-factor structure (distinguishing family, social, and romantic loneliness) showed the best fit. This model exhibited acceptable global fit indices, although the RMSEA and SRMR exceeded conventional thresholds, indicating some degree of misfit. Some localized issues were also observed, particularly with items 10 (“I wish I had a more satisfying romantic relationship”) and 15 (“I have an unmet need for a close romantic relationship”), which showed negative loadings on the romantic loneliness factor. Items 4, 7 and 13 also had suboptimal loadings on their respective factors. Despite these anomalies, the original three-factor structure provided a reasonable explanation for the response patterns observed in the sample, as is shown in Fig. 1. Discussion

These findings provide new evidence supporting earlier research identifying the SELSA-S as a reliable and multidimensional measure of loneliness in older adults [2].

Internal consistency varied across subscales, with stronger reliability in the emotional and family domains. In contrast, the SELSA-S social subscale showed lower reliability, particularly when compared to the ESTE-II scale, which was specifically developed to measure social loneliness in older adults [34]. In this sample, ESTE-II demonstrated greater internal consistency and conceptual clarity than the SELSA-S social domain, suggesting that the latter may be less sensitive to the social dynamics of aging or that its items require further refinement.

Convergent and discriminant validity were supported by expected associations. SELSA-S scores correlated positively with sadness, depression, anxiety, and other loneliness measures [2, 51, 52]. Conversely, they showed negative correlations with happiness and subjective well-being, consistent with prior findings [53]. The lack of association with age suggests perceived loneliness may be independent of chronological factors [48]. Moderate correlation between family and romantic loneliness, and the absence of association between romantic and social domains, highlight their conceptual distinctiveness [3] and the importance of maintaining separate dimensions in loneliness assessments for older adults [54]. Furthermore, discriminant validity among subscales was supported by HTMT values clearly below recommended thresholds, reinforcing that the three SELSA-S dimensions remain empirically distinct and capture non-overlapping aspects of loneliness in later life.

In this study, the original three-factor structure of the SELSA-S showed the best fit compared to other tested models, confirming its ability to capture the multidimensional nature of loneliness [3, 6, 55]. Overall, global fit indices were acceptable. However, the elevated RMSEA and the negative loadings of items 10 and 15 suggest localized tensions within the romantic domain. This may relate to the sample’s relational context, with over half of participants widowed or single. It is plausible that this influenced responses to romantic loneliness items, as highlighted in previous studies with older adults [54]. Future generations of older adults may, however, exhibit different response patterns, given ongoing social changes and evolving understandings of intimate relationships across cohorts. While item wording was left unchanged to avoid affecting the psychometric properties of the instrument, items 4, 7 and 13 present double negatives. The items themselves are phrased negatively and one of the endpoints for the response scale is “no”. This may explain their lower loadings, which were also below commonly recommended CFA thresholds of 0.40.

Conclusion

Altogether, these findings indicate that the SELSA-S is a conceptually grounded and multidimensional measure of loneliness in older adults, supported by coherent patterns of reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, and a factor structure consistent with its theoretical foundations. The results also underscore the differentiated nature of emotional, family, and social loneliness in later life, reinforcing the value of assessing these domains separately. Future research may further refine specific aspects of the scale to deepen the understanding of loneliness and enhance the precision of its measurement in aging populations.

Limitations

Four limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings: (1) recruitment took place in community settings attended primarily by independent and active older adults, which may introduce participation bias and limit generalizability; the final sample size was also modest for CFA standards, potentially reducing the stability of the factor structure; (2) reliability estimates were uneven across domains, with particularly low internal consistency in the social subscale; (3) although convergent and discriminant validity were supported, the assessment could have been strengthened by including additional measures of social support or social network structure to broaden criterion validity; and (4) although the three-factor model showed the best relative fit, RMSEA, SRMR, and the problematic loadings of items 4, 7, 10, 13, and 15 indicate localized tensions in the factor structure, potentially related to item clarity (e.g., double negatives) and the shifting relevance of relational domains in later life. Even so, the strict cognitive and functional screening applied, the homogeneity of the community-dwelling sample, and the theoretically coherent pattern of associations support the robustness and interpretability of the findings.
