ABSTRACT
Previous research indicates that having children can negatively affect relationship satisfaction, yet it may also strengthen bonding between partners. Romantic love is hypothesized to serve as a commitment device contributing to marital satisfaction. Interestingly, the relationship between romantic love and the number of children is complex and has received limited empirical attention, especially in diverse cultural contexts. However, some evidence from traditional societies suggests a positive correlation. Guided by Sternberg’s Triangular Theory of Love, we examined the relationship between the number of children and romantic love and its three components (passion, intimacy, commitment) across 25 populations. Based on prior research, we hypothesized that the number of children would be positively associated with passion and commitment but negatively associated with intimacy. Our global sample included 3,187 married or engaged individuals (55.9% women), aged 18–99 years (M = 38.69, SD = 10.55), from 25 countries. Contrary to our predictions, having children, but not the number of children, was negatively related to overall romantic love, intimacy, and passion, but unrelated to commitment. These findings suggest that parenthood may be linked to reduced romantic love, particularly in terms of intimacy and passion, across diverse cultural settings. This pattern may reflect challenges commonly associated with the transition to parenthood, including increased stress, fatigue, financial strain, and work-life conflict, which can diminish partners’ sense of closeness and attraction. Overall, the results underscore the importance of supporting couples’ romantic relationships during the parenting stage to help sustain emotional and physical connection under the demands of family life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12110-025-09505-w.

BODY
1. Introduction

From an adaptive perspective, pair bonding and stable mating relationships in humans have often been linked to successful reproduction and the benefits of bi-parental care of offspring (Quinlan, 2008). At the same time, it is important to acknowledge that romantic partnerships are not always directly tied to reproduction. In many modern contexts, where contraception is widely available, same-sex relationships are recognized, and non-reproductive unions (e.g., childfree or post-menopausal couples) are increasingly common, partnerships may prioritize companionship, emotional connection, and personal fulfillment over childrearing (Fletcher et al., 2015). Nevertheless, numerous studies indicate that mate choice is still largely influenced by morphological cues of fertility and good genes inherited by offspring, potentially enhancing their viability (e.g., Marcinkowska, 2023), despite substantial variability in paternal investment across human populations (Fernandez-Duque et al., 2009). Furthermore, research shows that having children is generally associated with greater perceived and actual relationship stability (Goode, 1993; Kowal et al., 2024), while childlessness remains one of the main causes of divorce across cultures (Betzig, 1989). Surprisingly, despite buffering effects of having children on relationship stability, parents often report lower marital satisfaction compared to non-parents (Dillon & Beechler, 2010; Kowal et al., 2021; Twenge et al., 2003), though this effect may not be universal across cultures (Onyishi et al., 2012; Twenge et al., 2003). These declines in satisfaction may stem from the challenges associated with raising children, including chronic stress, fatigue, financial strain, and work-family conflict (Cowan & Cowan, 1995; Crnic & Greenberg, 1990; Deave et al., 2008; Fillo et al., 2015; Stanca et al., 2012). Thus, while the adaptive function of stable pair bonds may be to support reproduction and child-rearing, the impact of children on relationship quality appears to be complex and context-dependent, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Romantic love is a cross-culturally universal phenomenon (Buss, 2019; Jankowiak & Fischer, 1992; Kowal, 2023; Kowal et al., 2023; Sorokowski et al., 2021, 2023, 2025a), hypothesized to serve as a complex suite of behavioral, cognitive, physiological, and emotional adaptations and evolutionary by-products that likely emerged with the evolution of pair-bonding in humans (Buss, 2019; Bode & Kushnick, 2021; Fletcher et al., 2015). These adaptations are expected to serve a range of functions related to reproduction, including facilitating mate choice (Fisher et al., 2006), sexual access, increased frequency of sexual intercourse (Diamond, 2004; Fisher et al., 2016; Leridon, 1993), and the provision of emotional and material resources, caregiving, and parental investment (Buss, 2019; Fletcher et al., 2015; Gelbart et al., 2025; Yoo & Joo, 2022). Furthermore, some evidence also suggests that romantic love may be associated with changes in sex hormone levels related to higher fecundity (Hopcroft, 2006; Marazziti & Canale, 2004; Sorokowski et al., 2019). Therefore, romantic love may contribute to a higher number of pregnancies and, consequently, offspring. While in many contemporary populations factors such as reproductive health (Aitken, 2024), delayed childbearing (Sobotka, 2017), economic conditions (Shenk et al., 2013), and social support (Mathews & Sear, 2013) may play a more prominent role in shaping fertility, the association between romantic love and reproduction may have been particularly significant in pre-contraceptive societies, where frequent sexual activity typically led to pregnancy and bi-parental care was critical for child survival (Goetz et al., 2019). Despite these theoretical links, surprisingly few studies have directly examined the relationship between romantic love and the presence of children. One notable exception is research conducted among the Hadza, a traditional hunter-gatherer population in Tanzania (Sorokowski et al., 2017). Investigating the cultural universality of the positive relationship between the number of children and romantic love—and whether having children impacts parents’ romantic love—could help clarify the contradictory findings on bond stability, marital satisfaction, and family size reported in previous studies (Dillon & Beechler, 2010; Goode, 1993; Kowal et al., 2021; Twenge et al., 2003).

There are several definitions of romantic love, most of which emphasize its cognitive, emotional, and behavioral characteristics (Fletcher et al., 2015). According to Sternberg (1986), romantic love comprises three core components: passion, intimacy, and commitment. Passion refers to romance, physical attraction, and sexual desire, which often serve as the initial drivers of romantic connection. Intimacy encompasses emotional closeness, warmth, and the sharing of personal experiences, fostering interdependence between partners. Commitment involves the cognitive decision to initiate and maintain a long-term relationship, contributing to its stability over time (Sternberg, 1986). Factor analytic studies in Western samples generally support this model, consistently identifying three underlying dimensions (e.g., Aron & Westbay, 1996; Sorokowski et al., 2021). Similar support for this tripartite model of love has been found in traditional societies, including populations where arranged marriages are common (Sorokowski et al., 2025b). While the labels for each factor vary across studies, they generally reflect passion (sexual attraction), emotional bonding (intimacy), and commitment (caregiving). These components can function independently or combine in various ways to create different types of love experiences (e.g., infatuation, unrequited love), though full-blown romantic love typically involves a mixture of all three elements (Sternberg, 1986).

Previous research suggests that the components of romantic love may relate differently to the number of offspring. Sorokowski et al. (2017) found that among hunter-gatherers, passion was positively associated with the number of children, but only in women, possibly because a woman’s level of passion influences the frequency of sexual encounters and, consequently, reproductive success. In the same population, commitment was positively associated with the number of children in both men and women, likely reflecting the role of commitment in sustaining stable relationships, and the decision to stay together often precedes having children. Conversely, intimacy was negatively associated with the number of children in women, perhaps because mothers devote more time to their children than to their partners. Similarly, Meyer et al. (2016), using a sample of Americans, reported that having more children may reduce relationship satisfaction, potentially due to diminished intimacy between partners.

In the present study, we examine the relationship between the number of children and romantic love in contemporary populations, where birth control is widely available and having multiple offspring is typically a matter of choice. We re-analyze a large-scale, cross-cultural dataset originally published by Sorokowski et al. (2021). This publicly available dataset can be utilized by both the original authors, as in the present study, and by independent researchers. Romantic love was assessed using the Triangular Love Scale (Sternberg, 1986), allowing a detailed investigation of its three components: intimacy, passion, and commitment. Based on prior findings, we hypothesize that the number of children will be positively associated with passion and commitment, and potentially negatively associated with intimacy (Meyer et al., 2016; Sorokowski et al., 2017).

2. Methods

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee (Ethics Committee of the Institute of Psychology, University of Wrocław) and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration, including its later amendments or comparable ethical guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before they participated in the study. This research was funded by the IDN Being Human Lab at the University of Wrocław, Poland.

2.1. Participants

The study was part of a larger cross-cultural project investigating the psychological and sociobiological foundations of romantic love (Conroy-Beam, 2019a, 2019b; Sorokowska et al., 2023; Sorokowski et al., 2021, 2023; Walter et al., 2020, 2021). The initial dataset included 11,422 individuals from 45 countries across six continents (see Sorokowski et al., 2021). Participants were excluded for the following reasons: missing or inadequate responses regarding sex (N = 27), age (N = 52), or relationship status (single, dating, engaged, married, divorced, widowed) (N = 142); being single or in a relationship other than marriage or engagement (N = 6 531); not providing information on the number of children (N = 471); or completing a version of the Triangular Love Scale that had not been linguistically validated (N = 913). Additional exclusions were applied to participants under 18 years of age or in a relationship lasting less than one year (N = 88), as well as those providing invalid responses on the Triangular Love Scale (e.g., entering “0” on a scale ranging from 1 to 9) (N = 11). After applying these exclusion criteria, the final study sample consisted of 3,187 individuals in committed relationships, either engaged (18%) or married (82%), representing 25 countries (see Table 1). The sample included 44.1% men and 55.9% women, with ages ranging from 18 to 99 years (M = 38.69, SD = 10.55).

2.2. Procedure

In each country, questionnaires were administered either face-to-face or during group sessions with volunteers who agreed to participate. All data were collected in person, with computer software used for direct entry when applicable. Data collection took place across a variety of settings, from large urban centers to small villages, and included individuals with diverse educational backgrounds. No restrictions were imposed on participants’ educational or occupational status; however, to promote sample diversity, researchers were instructed to limit student participation to no more than 50% of each sample. Although the samples are not nationally representative, their heterogeneity allowed us to capture a broad range of sociocultural environments and examine romantic love and parenthood across diverse communities.

Questionnaires were completed in participants’ native languages. Whenever the scales had to be translated into a language other than English, the measures were translated and back-translated following a standard procedure (Sechrest et al., 1972). This process involved one person translating the measures into the target language, a second person translating them back into English, and subsequent comparisons and adjustments to ensure accuracy (for details, see Kowal, 2024). Data collection across all locations occurred during a comparable period (2017–2018).

2.2. Romantic Love Assessment

Participants completed the 45-item version of the Sternberg Triangular Love Scale (STLS) (1986, 1997), which includes 15 items measuring intimacy (e.g., “I receive considerable emotional support from ___.”), 15 items measuring passion (e.g., “There is nothing more important to me than my relationship with ___”), and 15 items measuring commitment (e.g., “I view my relationship with ___ as permanent.”). Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a 9-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 9 (extremely). The internal consistency of the scales across countries was excellent: for intimacy, Cronbach’s α ranged from α = 0.89 (Estonia) to α = 0.96 (China, Hungary, Poland, Serbia, Uruguay, Vietnam), for passion, Cronbach’s α ranged from α = 0.91 (Brazil, Pakistan) to α = 0.96 (Algeria, Hungary, Poland, Romania, Turkey, Vietnam), and for commitment Cronbach’s α ranged from α = 0.88 (Slovakia) to α = 0.98 (Hungary, Poland, Romania, Vietnam) (see Table 1 for details). The three components correlated with each other (Intimacy x Passion: r = 0.73, p < 0.001; Intimacy x Commitment: r = 0.80, p < 0.001; Passion x Commitment: r = 0.75, p < 0.001). Configural, metric, and scalar invariance of the STLS were previously established in our earlier research (Sorokowski et al., 2021).

2.3. Questionnaire - number of Children & Covariates

Participants completed a questionnaire that included demographic questions (age, sex). Participants also reported the number of children, which ranged from 0 to 10 (M = 1.56, SD = 1.21).

2.4. Statistical Analysis

Participants were nested within countries (level 2 variable), so all analyses were conducted using multilevel models with a Maximum Likelihood estimator. We tested four models, with the composite love score (the average of the three love components) and each love component as dependent variables.

The number of children was modeled as a count predictor. Additionally, we created a dichotomous variable concerning parenthood, dividing participants into two groups: childless (N = 699) and parents with at least one child (N = 2488). Age and sex, key factors related to love (Sorokowski et al., 2017; Sternberg, 1986), were included as control variables. Since relationship length was highly correlated with age (r = 0.83, p < 0.001), we controlled for age only. Age and the number of children were group-mean-centered before inclusion in the models. We also tested whether interactions between the number of children and sex or age influenced the relationship between the number of children and romantic love.

For each dependent variable, we estimated two models: a random intercept model and a model that allows both intercepts and slopes to vary across countries. The models were compared and selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with a difference greater than 10, which is considered strong evidence against the model with the higher BIC (Kass & Raftery, 1995).

In addition to the multilevel models, we conducted forest plot analyses to examine the association between the number of children and romantic love, as well as its components, across the 25 country samples separately.

All analyses were performed in Jamovi (version 2.3). Results were considered significant at p < 0.05.

3. Results

3.1. Number of Children and Love

A random intercept model, with a composite love score (an average of 45 items) regressed on the number of children, controlling for participants’ sex and age, revealed no relationship between the number of children and the composite love score. However, love was negatively related to age, and men scored higher than women (see Table 2 - Model 1). A model allowing intercepts and slopes to vary across countries showed a worse fit, as indicated by a higher BIC (BIC = 10,141.38, ΔBIC = 13.73).

We also tested a random intercept model with love score regressed on a dichotomous variable, childless vs. having at least one child, again controlling for participants’ sex and age. In this model, parents with at least one child reported lower composite love scores than childless parents (Table 2 – Model 2). As before, allowing intercepts and slopes to vary across countries resulted in a poorer model fit (BIC = 10,138.72, ΔBIC = 14,16).

Finally, we found no interactions between sex and number of children, or between sex and age, in predicting love scores.

3.3. Number of Children and Passion

A random intercept model regressing passion score on the number of children and controlling for participants’ sex and age showed no relationship between passion and the number of children. Passion was negatively related to age, and women showed lower passion than men (see Table 3 – Model 3). We also tested a model allowing intercepts and slopes to vary across countries, but this model had a worse fit, indicated by a higher BIC (BIC = 11,634.90, ΔBIC = 13.37).

We found no interaction between sex and the number of children or age and the number of children in predicting the passion score.

A random intercept model regressing passion score on the dichotomous variable, childless vs. parent, controlling for participants’ sex and age, showed that parents with at least one child exhibited lower passion than childless individuals (Table 3 – Model 4). We also tested a model allowing both intercepts and slopes to vary between countries, which showed a similar fit, as indicated by comparable BIC values (BIC = 11,624.39, ΔBIC = 6.97).

3.4. Number of Children and Commitment

A random intercept model regressing commitment score on the number of children and controlling for participants’ sex and age showed no relationship between commitment and the number of children. Women had lower commitment scores compared to men, and there was a negative trend between age and commitment score (Table 3 – Model 5). A model allowing intercepts and slopes to vary between countries showed a worse fit, as indicated by a higher BIC (BIC = 10,522.80, ΔBIC = 15.86).

We also found no significant relationship between commitment and the dichotomous variable of being childless versus having at least one child (Table 3 – Model 6). We also found no relationship between commitment score and the interaction between an individual’s age and the number of children.

Discussion

The results of our analysis, based on a cross-cultural sample of individuals in committed romantic relationships, indicate a negative relationship between having children and romantic love, particularly with its components, such as intimacy and passion (but not commitment), as measured by the Triangular Theory of Love questionnaire (Sternberg, 1997).

Our findings contrast with earlier research showing a positive relationship between love and reproductive success among traditional hunter-gatherers, the Hadza from Tanzania (Sorokowski et al., 2017). This discrepancy may reflect differences in optimal reproductive strategies and decision-making across various societies. In more traditional populations, large families often indicate wealth and social status and serve as important sources of social and economic support, which may, in turn, enhance their desirability (Caldwell, 1982). These dynamics may increase marital satisfaction and help sustain romance and love. In contrast, in Western societies, higher fertility often involves substantial costs for parents and children, including increased stress, strain, fatigue, work-family conflict, and decreased marital satisfaction (Cowan & Cowan, 1995; Crnic & Greenberg, 1990; Deave et al., 2008; Fillo et al., 2015; Lu, 2006; Metzger & Gracia, 2023). Moreover, research suggests that in Western contexts, the mental health benefits of parenthood are diminished by lower socioeconomic status and full-time employment (Metzger & Gracia, 2023). These contextual differences may help explain the contrasting relationships between parenthood and romantic love in Western versus traditional societies.

Interestingly, although the costs associated with parenthood might intuitively be expected to increase with each additional child, our analyses revealed that the main distinction in romantic love and its components was between individuals with and without children, rather than showing a linear relationship with the number of children. One possible explanation is that the transition to parenthood itself marks a substantial shift in relationship dynamics, perhaps more so than the arrival of subsequent children. This interpretation is supported by prior research indicating, for example, that in Australia, the number of daily childcare hours varies with the age of the child but not necessarily with the total number of children. While the first child significantly alters household routines, subsequent children appear to bring relatively modest additional demands in terms of childcare and paid or unpaid work (Craig & Bittman, 2008). Moreover, second-born children often receive less quality time with parents compared to first-borns (Price, 2008), and the economic burden of child-rearing may scale non-linearly with family size, with the most substantial financial impact occurring during the appearance of the first child (Robb, 2019). Further evidence comes from a recent study in China showing that the prevalence of postpartum depression does not significantly differ between first-time and experienced mothers, suggesting that the psychological burden does not necessarily increase with additional children (Zhang et al., 2024). Additionally, childbirth is known to affect sexual functioning, yet studies indicate that sexual dysfunction is actually lower in multiparous than in primiparous women (Banaei et al., 2020). Nonetheless, we fully acknowledge the complexity of these dynamics and emphasize the need for further research to examine potential moderating factors such as culture, socioeconomic context, and the availability of parental support systems.

Interestingly, previous research found that marital satisfaction, which is closely linked to romantic love (Yoo & Joo, 2022), was negatively related not only to being a parent but also to the number of children (Kowal et al., 2021). This may suggest that the most significant shift in love dynamics occurs during the transition to parenthood, likely due to the demands of new roles, identity changes, and time constraints. Once this adjustment is made, having additional children may not significantly affect emotional closeness, passion, or commitment, though it can still influence overall relationship satisfaction.

Intimacy was lower in parents compared with childless individuals, but the number of children was unrelated to intimacy. Intimacy also showed no association with age or sex. The negative association between parenthood and intimacy partially aligns with a previous study of the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer society in Tanzania, where the number of children was negatively related to women’s (but not men’s) intimacy (Sorokowski et al., 2017). Several factors may explain why having children is linked to lower intimacy. Intimacy involves mutual trust, closeness, and emotional connectedness (Sternberg, 1986). While it is associated with increased sexual activity and proximity seeking, both important for conceiving children (Rubin & Campbell, 2012), the transition to parenthood often shifts the focus of emotional investment. As spouses incorporate their children into their emotional sphere, they may have less time and energy to invest in intimacy with one another. This effect may be especially pronounced for women, who are typically the primary caregivers (Ridgeway & Correll, 2004), leaving less time and availability for their partners (Ahlborg & Strandmark, 2001). Furthermore, mothers, especially in the early postpartum period, frequently experience fatigue, lifestyle adjustments, and body image concerns (Woolhouse et al., 2012), all of which can impact intimate dynamics. However, these challenges are not limited to women and can affect both partners. Indeed, previous research has shown that the transition to parenthood can interfere with a couple’s companionship (Twenge et al., 2003) and that the quality of marital and parent-child relations is closely interrelated (Erel & Burman, 1995). This interdependence may help explain why we did not observe a significant effect of participant sex on intimacy in our study.

The results of the study showed that having children is associated with lower levels of passion. As noted earlier, previous studies have found that children are linked to reduced marital satisfaction in Western societies (Twenge et al., 2003). Parenthood often brings increased demands on time and energy, resulting in having less time for the romantic relationship, a shift in priorities toward the needs of children, physical and emotional exhaustion, and reduced privacy resulting from the presence of children in the household – factors that can diminish passion in a romantic relationship (Twenge et al., 2003). Therefore, similar to marital satisfaction, passion may decline in parents.

Finally, we found no relationship between having children or the number of children and commitment, the component of love expected to increase in long-term relationships (Sternberg, 1986) and with a higher number of children (Sorokowski et al., 2017). Commitment is expected to foster a sense of security and stability in relationships (Johnson & Rusbult, 1989). Having children may strengthen the desire to maintain a partnership and deepen emotional interdependence between spouses, as suggested by studies showing that a higher number of children is associated with lower divorce rates in countries such as the USA (Cherlin, 2010), Sweden (Hoem & Hoem, 1992), Finland (Jalovaara, 2001), Italy and Spain (Coppola & Di Cesare, 2008), and China (Xu et al., 2015). However, the relationship between children and marital stability—and potentially commitment—is complex. As children grow older, their stabilizing effect on marriage appears to weaken (Heaton, 1990; Bracher et al., 1993; Andersson, 1997), with some studies even suggesting that children over the age of 13 may negatively affect marital stability (Waite & Lillard, 1991). Although we controlled participants’ age in our analyses, we did not control for the age of their children. Future research should consider this factor to better understand the nuanced relationship between parenthood, love, and its components. Additionally, it is possible that couples with children who experience low commitment are more likely to separate and, therefore, are underrepresented in samples. As a result, the lack of association between parenthood and commitment could reflect a form of bond survivorship bias. In contrast, relationships characterized by high commitment but lower levels of intimacy or passion may persist and thus be more likely to be included in population-based studies. This possibility warrants further investigation.

The results of our study also support a negative relationship between age and romantic love and its components, consistent with Sternberg’s (1986) predictions and prior research on how love changes across the lifespan (e.g., Sumter et al., 2013). This pattern may reflect age-related differences in the experience and expression of romantic love, as well as changes that occur as relationships progress (Knox, 1970; Montgomery & Sorell, 1997; Sumter et al., 2013). In this sample, relationship length was strongly correlated with age, so we did not control for it separately. Nevertheless, relationship length may influence romantic love independently of age and should be considered in future studies.

Additionally, we also found that men reported higher levels of passion and commitment than women, but there was no sex difference in intimacy level. Although it is often assumed that men score lower on all three love components, the evidence is mixed (Sumter et al., 2013). Many studies show that women generally report higher intimacy than men, but according to a meta-analysis of Dindia and Allen (1992), this sex difference is small. Furthermore, more recent research has reported no sex difference in intimacy (Connolly et al., 1999; Gao, 2001; Ha et al., 2010; Kowal et al., 2025). In conclusion, sex differences in the component of romantic love and their links to offspring number require further studies.

It is also important to acknowledge several additional limitations of our study. The database used in this study did not include information on participants’ ideal or desired number of children, nor on their satisfaction with their current family size. These factors could influence the relationship between the number of children and romantic love. This is especially important as many individuals form and maintain romantic relationships without having children, including those who are childfree by choice, beyond reproductive age, in social structures where childrearing is communal rather than strictly bi-parental, and participate in consensual non-monogamous or arranged relationships where childrearing or romantic love may not be central. Future research should include these measures. The database did not include information on whether couples were opposite-sex or same-sex, as such questions might be culturally sensitive or even legally problematic in some countries. Consequently, we cannot rule out the inclusion of some same-sex couples. As romantic relationship dynamics and parenting structures may differ in same-sex relationships, future studies should explicitly account for couple type when examining associations between romantic love and the number of children. Finally, the forest plots revealed that associations between parenthood and romantic love vary across countries, with some samples showing positive effects and others negative ones. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in sample sizes, some of which were relatively small and therefore underpowered to detect subtle effects, but it may also indicate that cultural, social, and demographic contexts play a significant role in shaping the link between family structure and romantic relationships. Accordingly, future research should examine cultural moderators or conduct culture-specific analyses to better understand variability in the associations between parenthood and romantic love. Relevant cultural factors may include, for example, individualism–collectivism (Kowal et al., 2025), gender equality (Sorokowski et al., 2023), and relational mobility (Eastwick et al., 2025).

In summary, our results indicate that having children, rather than the number of children, is negatively related to romantic love in urbanized, developed societies. These findings contrast with previous results from traditional Hadza society (Sorokowski et al., 2017) and previously reported differences among age groups (Twenge et al., 2003). Our study highlights the complexity of the relationship between parenthood and romantic love, emphasizing the role of cultural and societal context. Furthermore, our research suggests that future studies should employ longitudinal designs, following couples from the early stages of their relationship to better understand how love and reproduction influence one another over time, in both modern and traditional societies. Tracking changes in romantic love and relationship satisfaction before and after the birth of each child may provide valuable insights into these dynamics.
