ABSTRACT
AbstractAmong the diverse sexual signals used by birds, the possible signaling functions of non-bodily objects during mating/courting displays remain unexplained. It is unclear whether such object-holding gestures are purely ritualistic or serve as an advertisement of an extended phenotype to potential mates. Estrildid finches (family Estrildidae) are characterized by mutual courtship, during which either a male or female will hold nesting material (usually grass stems or straw) in the beak while singing and dancing toward the opposite sex. We predicted that this display of potential nesting material advertises the ability of an individual to collect and transport such materials. Captive adult red-cheeked cordon-bleus (Uraeginthus bengalus) and star finches (Neochmia ruficauda) were presented with different string lengths in choice tests, and results were compared with those obtained from field observations of courtship displays among U. bengalus and blue-capped cordon-bleus (Uraeginthus cyanocephalus). Both captive and wild estrildid finches preferred longer strings for displays, which is compatible with the idea that non-bodily ornaments (strings) convey information on the maximum performance potential. The evolution of nesting material holding displays is likely associated with the nesting ecology of estrildid finches, which is characterized by a high predation risk and joint nest building by mating pairs.Keywords: Courtship, Tool-use, Object-use, Extended phenotype, Sexual signal, Estrildid finchSubject terms: Behavioural ecology, Sexual selection

BODY
Introduction

Avian courtship displays can involve extremely strange rituals that exemplify how sexual selection can promote the exaggeration and diversity of communicative signals in animals. In general, conspicuous morphological ornamentation and a physically challenging display performance reflect individual quality or conditions and can therefore serve as reliable clues for mate choice1,2. This prediction holds true for many avian mating signals, such as colorful plumage3,4, birdsong5–8, and acrobatic courtship displays9,10, and it may be extended to explain the use of external (i.e., non-bodily) objects during courtship displays. One notable example comes from bowerbirds, in which males build bowers decorated with colorful objects to attract females11.

In animals, the products of construction behavior can serve as mating signals, provided that they reflect the phenotypic quality of the individuals that built the structures12–14. This explains why the bowers of bowerbirds are the targets of female mate choice. The building and maintenance of better bowers are influenced by male–male competition, because males may actively damage the bowers of rivals (satin bowerbird, Ptilonorhynchus riolaceus15) or even steal their bower decorations (great bowerbird, Ptilonorhynchus nuchalis16,17). Such behaviors suggest possible associations between bower quality, cognitive performance, and mating success, although such concepts are still being debated18,19. In polygynous weaverbirds, males develop nest-weaving techniques20 that are crucial for their reproductive success, as the number of females with which males mate is highly dependent on the number and quality of the nests they manufacture21,22. In certain fish species, the features of male nests (or bowers) similarly play an important role in female mate choice23–25. Therefore, nest decorations or adornments by birds may also act as a mating signal26,27.

During courtship, various animals also use external objects that do not require construction, but the signaling function of such items is more obscure. Among birds, object-holding gestures are sometimes shown as part of mating displays by both sexes (cf. dolphin object carrying display28). When grebe pairs exhibit mutual courtship, they perform a “weed ceremony” by holding pieces of weed in their beaks29,30. Male herons (Hydranassa tricolor) appeal to females by holding and shaking twigs31, whereas male red-backed fairy wrens (Malurus melanocephalus) carry flower petals32. In these cases, the objects on display do not appear to serve any practical use, because they are neither consumed nor utilized immediately after the display. None of these studies have tested whether the birds actively choose particular features of the items to advertise phenotypic quality. Hypothetically, individuals could show off rare or valuable items to signal their own quality12, which has been well tested for the use of decorations by bowerbirds33,34. In cooperatively breeding Arabian babblers (Turdoides squamiceps), both alpha males and females as well as subordinate males have been observed to pick up and display a variety of objects, which is frequently followed with copulation; edibility of the item was not associated with copulation rate, rejecting the hypothesis that such items functioned as nuptial gifts35.

Given that some items used for object-holding displays are potential nesting materials (e.g., weed in grebes and twigs in herons) and that the display-performing sex corresponds to the nest-builder sex (e.g., both sexes in grebes and males in herons), these displays may plausibly function as advertisements for the nesting ability of the individual. This concept can be equally applied to straw-holding displays in estrildid finches. A considerable proportion of estrildid species hold pieces of sting—mostly plant material such as grass stems—in the bill when performing dancing and singing displays during courtship36–40. This behavior is referred to as a stem/straw display or nesting material (straw) holding display in the literature36–39,41. In a previous study, we conducted interspecific comparative analyses targeting the family Estrildidae that revealed that males perform straw-holding behavior in species where the division of nest-building labor is biased toward males39, which could support the hypothesis that the display may serve as an indicator of nesting skill. Some estrildid species, such as cordon-bleus (Uraeginthus spp.), are capable of singing courtship songs while holding nest materials in the beak42–44, which could advertise physical ability. In one estrildid species, the diamond firetail (Stagonopleura guttata), among-individual variation in the lengths of grass used for displays was examined, but it had no clear association with mating success45.

To test our hypothesis that the straw characteristics in straw-holding displays by estrildid finches serve as an extended phenotype signal12, this study examined the straw length selection preferences of cordon-bleus (Uraeginthus spp.) and star finches (Neochmia ruficauda). The choice of such “props” may be non-random. Specifically, birds may prefer to use longer strings to (1) make their courtship display more visually appealing, (2) advertise their physical performance by retaining objects in the bill, and (3) indicate their ability to collect superior quality nesting materials. We conducted prop-choice tests by providing captive subject birds with experimentally controlled lengths of wire. In addition, we compared the resultant preferentially selected string lengths with those used for courtship displays by cordon-bleus in the wild44.

Materials and methods

String length choice tests in the laboratory

The subject birds for our choice test consisted of captive adult red-cheeked cordon-bleus (Uraeginthus bengalus, male: n = 12, female: n = 9) and star finches (N. ruficauda, male: n = 12, female: n = 4), which were purchased from local pet supplies. All birds were kept in single-sex cages with conspecifics. For the choice tests, an individual bird was introduced into an observational cage (26 × 20 × 30 cm) in a soundproof box along with a conspecific individual of the opposite sex to evoke a potential courtship display. The birds were housed at a temperature of 25 ± 3 °C, humidity 30–60%, with a 12L:12D lighting schedule, and provided with food and water ad libitum. During each observation session (90 min), their behaviors were recorded using two video cameras (JVC Victor GC-PX1; Zoom Q4, Q4n, or Q8), with one positioned in front of the cage to capture courtship and the other hung from the ceiling of the soundproof box to observe the subject’s string choice. Because the subject birds were not always motivated to court, we repeated the observations using different bird combinations.

Both red-cheeked cordon-bleus and star finches initiate courtship display by searching for and then picking up a string, holding onto its tip with the beak36 (Fig. 1a,b). They rarely perform courtship without carrying an object in the beak44. This straw-holding courtship gesture is exhibited by both sexes but less frequently by females. Through an analysis of our video recordings, we could determine whether any three string length types (short, medium, or long; see below for details) were preferentially selected for courtship display. The presence of courtship was established when characteristic dancing movements were performed, including the repetitive ritualistic hopping/bobbing of birds on a perch38,43,46 (Supplementary Video S1, Video S2). We also recorded the number of times each string type was picked up without subsequent courtship dancing; occasionally, strings are dropped before dancing is initiated, and females (which rarely perform courtship dances) may also pick up strings.

String length selection during courtship in cordon-bleu and star finches. Photograph of courtship display by a male red-cheeked cordon-bleu carrying a 10 cm string (a) and a male star finch with a 20 cm string (b). In a choice test, subject birds were presented with a choice of three string lengths. Courtships with a 20 cm string were observed more frequently than those with either a 5 cm or 10 cm string (c). As some subjects were observed to perform courtships in multiple sessions, each connected scatterplot corresponds to the number of courtships within a session and is labeled with subject ID (c). In field observations, similar string lengths were observed to be used for courtship (d).

To examine string length preference, we prepared three different lengths of string that were cut from nylon-coated black wire (φ = 0.4 mm; Kiwa Seisakujo, Tokyo) and distributed randomly across the floor of the observation cage. For each test, we provided twelve short (5 cm), six medium length (10 cm), and three long (20 cm) strings, thus offering an equal total length of each type (60 cm). Resultantly, the chance level of choosing each of the three types of strings would be identical (33.3%) if the birds do not exhibit any preferences and their choice reflects length-based probability (but not the number of strings).

For each observation session, we counted the total number of string pick-up behaviors for each string length type, either in the presence of a subsequent courtship dance (hereafter, “courtship string choice”) or in the absence thereof (“pick-up string choice”). Additionally, we also measured the duration of the time that the subjects kept holding the string in the beak for pick-up string choice, as an index of preference. We tested whether string choice was explained by string length using a generalized linear mixed model for data (GLMM) with a Poisson error distribution; species and individual bird identities were entered as nested random effects, with string length as the fixed effect. Using the same set of fixed and random effects, string holding duration was analyzed with linear mixed-effect (LME) model. When analyzing courtship string choice, specifically, we only included data from sessions where a courtship dance occurred; resultantly, we could not include sex as an explanatory variable, as there was only one female who actively performed courtship dancing. When analyzing the pick-up string choice, we were able to test for the effect of sex.

This experiment was conducted with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National University Corporation at Hokkaido University (No. 16-0020, approved on April 11, 2016) in accordance with the Hokkaido University Regulations of Animal Experimentation. During the experiment, stress was minimised, and all birds were cared for and treated appropriately in accordance with the Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments from the Science Council of Japan and the Guidelines for Ethological Studies from the Japan Ethological Society. The methodologies of this study were carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

String length choice assessments using field observation data

Video data were obtained during a field study in Tanzania where the courtship dance displays of blue-capped (Uraeginthus cyanocephalus) and red-cheeked (U. bengalus) cordon-bleus were filmed42. The main aim of the filed work was to record and assess tap-dance stepping performance involved in courtship display of cordon-bleus (see42 for details). The observer (NO) and a filed assistant walked around the two study sites (the Mbeya region: 8° 43ʹ S 34° 04 E; the Iringa region: 7° 48ʹ S 35° 05 E) and attempted to film their courtship behavior using normal speed (GZ-E355, JVC KENWOOD Corporation, Japan; 30 frames per second) or high-speed (GC-P100, JVC KENWOOD Corporation, Japan; 240 frames per second) cameras, using ad-lib sampling. The focal individuals were detected based on the bobbing movements, singing activity and straw-holding.

From each video clip of cordon-bleu courtship dancing, we extracted an image that contained the entire body of the bird and the entire string. String length was measured relative to body height by manually tracing the string using ImageJ software47. Some dance clips were not used because of unclear images or foliage masking. Our final data comprised 34 dance clips (U. bengalus: n = 5; U. cyanocephalus: n = 29), which included only one female dance—shown by a red-cheeked individual—and all the others by males. The measurement technique is not very accurate because string length can be underestimated depending on the angle of the string; however, it is sufficient for measuring minimal performance in the wild. When a bird used a branching string (e.g., grass or a flower spike) for courtship42, we measured only the longest stem (from its tip, at the bird beak, to its end), excluding any additive length. The ratios of string length measurements to body height were used to approximate actual string lengths, with body height being set to 7.0 cm for both species, and string length preferences were compared with the outcomes from our string choice tests on captive birds.

This filed study was conducted under research permits from the Tanzania Wildlife Research Institute (TAWIRI), and the Tanzanian Commission for Science and Technology (COSTECH), permit no. NA-2019-85 obtained in 2019.

Results

Laboratory choice test

Overall, cordon-bleus and star finches showed a strong preference for longer strings. We observed 56 courtship bouts (cordon-bleus: n = 38 courtships [three males]; star finches: n = 18 courtships [one male, one female]). Both species used longer strings significantly more frequently than shorter ones for courtship displays (GLMM, length effect: p < 0.0001; Table 1a, Fig. 1c). The courtship string choices were especially pronounced in star finches, where neither males nor females used medium- or short-length strings for courting (Fig. 1c). For the pick-up string choice, we found a similar preference for longer strings (Table 1b). We observed 2142 pick-up behaviors (cordon-bleus: n = 833 [three males, three females]; star finches: n = 1309 [six males, seven females]), across which longer strings were selected more frequently (GLMM, length effect: p < 0.0001) and without any statistically significant effect of sex (GLMM, sex effect: p = 0.584). We also found that shorter strings tend to be thrown away immediately after picking-up, while longer ones not (holding duration [mean ± SD] 5 cm: 0.80 ± 1.35 s; 10 cm: 1.33 ± 3.72 s; 20 cm: 2.15 ± 4.65 s; LME, length effect: p < 0.0001).

The effect of string length on courtship (a) and pick-up (b) string choice (GLMM with Poisson error distribution with species/bird identity as nested random effects).

Field observation

The courtship string length preference exhibited by both red-cheeked and blue-capped cordon-bleus in the wild showed a tendency similar to that observed in the string choice test on captive birds (Fig. 1d). Strings with an estimated length of 15–20 cm were frequently used by blue-capped cordon-bleus, which matched the observed preference for 20 cm strings in the choice test (Fig. 1c). However, as the frequency distribution shows, the wild birds appeared to be less choosy regarding string length; wild blue-capped and red-cheeked cordon-bleus also used strings shorter than the range described for the choice test, but less frequently so (Fig. 1d). When comparing average string length, the wild birds used statistically significantly shorter strings (12.68 cm) than the captive birds (17.74 cm) (LME with random effect of species, average captive = 17.74 cm, average wild = 12.68 cm, p < 0.0079), but it should be noted that the subject species were not balanced between filed and captive dataset.

Discussion

To understand the role of non-bodily objects used in courtship displays, this study tested the prop choice of estrildid finches known to perform complex courtship dances, called straw-holding displays36–40. In support of our prediction that they would preferentially select longer strings to advertise their ability to find and carry nest materials, captive estrildid finches tended to choose longer strings in a choice test (Fig. 1c), but it should be noted that this result was obtained from small number of subjects, due to the choosy nature of the species. Consistent with captive choice test result, wild estrildid finches similarly selected longer string lengths when performing courtship dances, which however is not free from the sampling bias that birds with longer stings were more easily detected in the field observation. In addition, wild birds displayed a wider selection in string length range and were less choosy about using longer materials than captive birds (Fig. 1d). This could be due to the differential availability of materials in the wild or a possible preference for certain shapes (in addition to length), as wild finches are often observed to use highly-branched plant materials42. In addition, our image data analysis may have underestimated the true string lengths. The choice of string length did not vary between species or sexes, although our sample size was not sufficiently large to draw concrete conclusions in this regard. More importantly, the obtained results were not conclusive enough to tell whether the use of strings was for (1) visual conspicuousness of the display, (2) advertising physical performance, (3) reflecting nesting ability, or combination of these. Considering that this is the first pioneering experimental study that examined the object choice in straw-holding displays, we believe that it would stimulate future research that helps us to answer these questions.

Nesting or nesting-related behaviors function as mating signals in diverse bird species12,13,48–51. The quality of nests built by males can affect mate attraction22,26, which would make sense given the potential direct benefit that females receive from such nests and the nest-builder13,52. As a similar-functioning signal, nest scrape displays are performed by males to advertise potential nesting sites in some shorebirds that nest on the ground53,54, a behavior likely to have existed in dinosaurs as well55. Male palm cockatoos (Probosciger aterrimus) attract mates by performing drumming displays using twigs on tree hollows, which may advertise potential nesting sites56. The nesting material holding displays of estrildids may serve a similar function to such displays, potentially indicating the phenotypic quality of the mate, specifically, the ability to collect and carry nest materials39. If the use of strings in their displays were purely ritualistic, estrildid finches would have picked up random available strings, but our findings suggest that they were selective (Fig. 1c). This fits our prediction that non-bodily ornaments can convey information on the maximum performance potential of individuals12.

To elucidate the evolution of object-holding displays in general and that of nesting material holding displays in estrildid finches in particular, it would be worthwhile for future studies to investigate the contribution of objects/strings to mating success (see also57). However, since our subject species (cordon-bleus and star finches) seldom copulated after courtship, it was technically difficult to judge whether courtship was successful in our experiments (cf.58). In addition, although both cordon-bleus and star finches are characterized by the presence of female displays, females performed courtship less frequently than males in our experiments, complicating any assessment of female string choice and its effect on male preference. Based on the number of pick-ups observed for both males and females (Table 1b), we can safely speculate that both sexes share a similar string length preference. Such a result is notably relevant to joint nest making. Typically, estrildid males collect and bring nesting materials to the nest, while females remain in the nest to assemble and weave them36,39,59. In captivity, females are occasionally choosy enough about nesting materials that they will discard the contributions brought by their mates.

One crucial unanswered question is whether symbolic gestures reflect actual nest building capacity in species with ritualized displays. It remains unknown whether estrildid individuals capable of holding longer strings during courtship can also construct higher quality nests using such longer grasses/straws. We have never documented either captive or wild estrildid finches utilizing the string displayed during courtship to build nests, because courtship is not necessarily performed near nests or during nest-building36,42. Typically, the finches will discard the strings during or after courtship displays. Wild red-cheeked cordon-bleus construct dome-shaped grass nests with dimensions of approximately 10–15 cm59, for which the preferred string length recorded in this study (Fig. 1) could serve as highly suitable building material. Another estrildid species, the diamond firetail (Stagonopleura guttata), also prefers using long (25 cm) rather than short (15 cm) plant stems to build nests in captivity60. In addition, in the zebra finch (Taeniopygia guttata), captive males adjust the choice of nesting materials depending on the previous experience61,62, and the use of materials affect reproductive success63, questioning how much flexibility estrildid finches would show for the use of strings for courtship.

From an evolutionary perspective, it is still unclear why estrildid finches have adopted a straw-holding display whereas many other passerine species have not. Even among estrildid finches, some species do not show straw-holding when courtship dancing, suggesting that the use of straw is not mechanistically necessary for bobbing/hopping on the perch. One strong candidate factor is the selective force acting on nest dynamics. Estrildid finches show a low breeding success due to both predation and brood parasitism36,64–66. Therefore, their nesting behavior is characterized by a number of unique counterstrategies against predation and brood parasitism, such as nesting in thorny acacia trees containing wasps nests67,68, building false nests (also called cock’s nests)36, or placing carnivore scats on the nests69. Therefore, straw-holding displays probably evolved because of the importance of securing nests in socially monogamous species exercising mutual mate choice.
