ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND:Statistical significance is a commonly used method for assessing the effectiveness of psychological treatments. However, statistically significant differences do not necessarily indicate reliable change. Therefore, methods that complement statistical significance, such as the clinical significance of change, are necessary. This study explores the evaluation of psychological treatment outcomes beyond conventional statistical significance measures.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study has a pretest and post-test design. After the initial assessment, 30 participants attended 11 weekly 90-minute sessions of Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy (IBCT). All couples completed the Spanier Dyadic Adjustment Scale 1 week before the first intervention session and 1 week after the final intervention session. The data collection tool was a questionnaire, and the statistical method used was the clinical significance of change based on Jacobsen and Truax.RESULTS:After conducting the IBCT, the clinical significance can individually identify each participant’s status. Additionally, categorized groups offer more detailed information compared to statistical significance methods. Regarding clinical significance, 46.7% of couples were treated or recovered, 16.7% showed improvement, 33.3% remained unchanged (no reliable change), and 3.3% deteriorated.CONCLUSION:Change studies are essential for evaluating all clinical interventions and research, and it is important to use methods that can provide more comprehensive information. After the IBCT sessions, we assessed the client’s recovery based on clinical significance. The study indicates that the following subjects have recovered: 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, and 28.Keywords: Clinical significance, couples therapy, integrative behavioral couple therapy, marital satisfaction, randomized controlled trials, statistical significance, treatment outcomes

BODY
Introduction

Couple therapy is a widely employed intervention designed to assist partners in resolving relational issues and enhancing marital satisfaction. Its primary goal is to foster adaptive behaviors, encouraging spouses to respond appropriately to each other’s needs, cooperate, and compromise over time.[1] Marital adjustment, crucial for healthy family functioning, plays a key role in helping spouses fulfil their parental responsibilities and maintain a satisfying relationship. However, inadequate efforts toward marital adjustment can lead to maladjustment, causing significant emotional strain and distress for the individuals involved.[2]

Recent clinical observations and research suggest that a growing number of couples are experiencing maladjustment, which poses significant challenges in forming and maintaining intimate relationships.[3] Marital discord not only increases the risk of psychological issues, such as depression and anxiety, but also contributes to physical health problems, including cardiovascular diseases and changes in immune system functioning.[4,5] Furthermore, marital dissatisfaction has been shown to negatively affect the quality of parenting, which, in turn, impacts children’s psychological development.[6,7] On a larger scale, unresolved marital conflicts can undermine societal wellbeing.[8]

In Western societies, approximately 50% of marriages end in divorce, while in Iran, national statistics show that one divorce occurs for every 3–4 marriages, pointing to a concerning trend in marital instability. While these statistics reflect severe outcomes, many couples continue to experience ongoing conflicts without resorting to legal separation. As awareness of the benefits of couple therapy increases, more couples are seeking therapeutic interventions to resolve conflicts, improve communication, and enhance marital satisfaction.[9]

Couple therapy encompasses a variety of approaches, including traditional behavioral couple therapy,[10] cognitive behavioral couple therapy,[11] schema therapy-based couple therapy,[12] acceptance and commitment therapy-based couple therapy,[13] Gottman couple therapy,[14] emotionally focused couple therapy,[15] brief integrative marital therapy,[16] choice theory-based couple therapy,[17] and insight-oriented couple therapy.[18] These therapeutic models aim to evaluate the outcomes of interventions, which often include improving communication, emotional intimacy, and reducing conflict.[4]

No single therapeutic method has been found sufficient to address the complex dynamics of marital issues.[4] As such, integrative approaches have gained popularity, allowing therapists to employ multiple strategies and techniques to meet diverse relational needs.[19] One such integrative approach is Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy (IBCT), developed by Andrew Christensen and Neil Jacobson. IBCT combines principles of traditional behavioral couple therapy with strategies aimed at fostering both acceptance and change.[20] Therapists combine acceptance-based techniques, such as empathic joining and unified detachment, with change-focused strategies like behavioral exchange and problem-solving training.[21,22] These techniques are designed to strengthen emotional intimacy, increase tolerance for each other’s flaws, and foster positive behavioral changes.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of IBCT on various variables, including emotional divorce,[3] sexual dissatisfaction,[10] forgiveness following marital infidelity,[23] cognitive emotion regulation,[2] expectations of mind reading, belief in the immutability of a spouse,[24] relationship disorders, and negative emotions.[25] However, much of this research has relied on statistical significance to evaluate the efficacy of IBCT and other couple therapies.[26]

Despite its usefulness, statistical significance has limitations. While differences between control and treatment groups may be statistically significant, they may not reflect clinically meaningful changes. Statistical methods do not provide insights into the treatment response of each client or indicate the practical significance of treatment outcomes.[27] It remains unclear how these changes affect clients in their daily lives or whether they translate into meaningful improvements.[28,29] Thus, it is crucial to complement statistical significance with methods like clinical significance, which can offer a more nuanced understanding of treatment effectiveness.[17]

The clinical significance approach, introduced by Jacobsen and colleagues,[29] aims to assess whether changes in treatment outcomes surpass measurement error and whether those changes are meaningful from a clinical perspective.[27] This method categorizes outcomes into four distinct categories: recovery, improvement, no reliable change, and deterioration.[30] The assessment of clinical significance allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of how marital adjustment changes impact individuals on a personal level.[27] Additionally, some studies in couple therapy research have overlooked nonsignificant or negative findings, which may lead to a biased view of therapy effectiveness.[31,32] It is crucial to report such outcomes as they can inform treatment methods and theories.[30] In practice, not all clients will improve; some may experience no change or even deterioration, a factor often overlooked in couple therapy research.[31] The clinical significance framework can address this gap by providing a more detailed understanding of treatment outcomes on an individual basis.[27,33]

This article aims to explore the clinical significance of changes in marital adjustment following IBCT, focusing on whether these changes are meaningful for the participants involved. Using Jacobsen and Truax’s method[29] to evaluate clinical significance, the study will examine whether participants experience reliable changes that are clinically significant. The findings will contribute to a deeper understanding of IBCT’s effectiveness in improving marital satisfaction and highlight the importance of considering clinical significance in therapeutic research and practice.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design and did not include a follow-up as it is part of a broader qualitative study. Including a follow-up evaluation could have introduced bias in the continuation of the research. The study sample consisted of couples aged 20–45 who visited counseling and psychological service centers in Tehran, Iran, for marital conflicts in 2022.

Study participants and sampling

This article is part of a more comprehensive study (N = 58 after dropout) in which the participants experienced a significant increase in marital adjustment after receiving IBCT compared to the control group, according to the one-way ANCOVA (P = 0.001). The effect size was 0.50, meaning that half of the observed variance in adjustment scores was affected by IBCT. Considering the amount of test power (OP = 1), the analysis has 100% accuracy in identifying significant differences.[9]

In this phase of the study, 30 participants, all members of the intervention group, from the previous phase were involved. The sampling method was purposeful and based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were selected based on their interview and initial assessment, taking into account their demographic information as well as the inclusion/exclusion criteria and informed consent. Participants attended 11 weekly 90-minute sessions of IBCT. The participants were purposively selected from individuals who met the inclusion criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

Diagnosis of marital adjustment problems based on interviews with participants and scores below 110 on Spanier DAS (excluding severe maladjustment leading to an application for legal divorce), educational attainment (a high school diploma or higher), an age of 20–45 years, duration of marriage of 1–7 years, and having enough free time for participation in the intervention sessions.

The exclusion criteria were also as follows:

Absence in more than three sessions (the data obtained from such participants were excluded from analysis): Distorted evaluation results (questionnaires and interviews); affliction with severe psychiatric disorders (according to the initial interview); being under no psychological or psychiatric treatments (to prevent possible interferences with the intervention); addiction to drugs, alcohol, and gambling over the past 3 months; engagement in other romantic relationships (betrayal); and experiencing severe domestic violence.

Data collection tool and technique

The subjects completed the Spanier Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS) questionnaire 1 week before the first intervention session and again 1 week after the final intervention session. The DAS is a 32-item questionnaire developed by Spanier[34] to measure the adjustment of couples or romantic partners. This scale consists of four subscales: dyadic cohesion, affection, dyadic satisfaction, and dyadic consensus. The overall score on this scale indicates the general satisfaction of spouses with a relationship. Higher scores denote higher marital adjustment. The overall score on this scale ranges between 0 and 151, and items 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 31, and 32 measure dyadic satisfaction, whereas items 24, 25, 26, 27, and 28 measure dyadic cohesion. In addition, items 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 15 measure dyadic consensus, and items 4, 6, 29, and 30 measure affection. Spanier’s original research indicated that the scale has an exceptionally high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96 for the total score.

In addition to the overall internal consistency, the subscales of the DAS have also shown strong reliability. Specifically, the subscale for satisfaction has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94, indicating excellent internal consistency. The solidarity subscale has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81, which is considered good. Similarly, the agreement subscale has an alpha of 0.90, reflecting a high level of reliability, while the affection subscale has an alpha of 0.73, demonstrating adequate internal consistency.[35,36] An internal consistency of 0.95 has been reported for this scale, indicating a high level of reliability. Additionally, it demonstrated acceptable concurrent validity throught a significant correlation with the Lock-Wallace Marital Adjustment Scale (LWMAT). In Iran, the reliability coefficient of this scale was reported to be 95%.[37]

Statistical and data analysis

Calculating the reliable change index (RCI)

The reliable change index (RCI) indicates whether the observed changes in each person at the end of the treatment compared to before the treatment are statistically reliable. In other words, it helps determine whether these changes are authentic or the result of measurement errors. Is the magnitude of the observed change too large to be explained by measurement errors? The key concept here is that all measurements are somewhat unreliable, and the variance between two scores from the same individual may be attributable to measurement error rather than any other factor, such as treatment.[38,39] It can also be used to inquire whether a person’s condition has worsened: Has their condition reliably deteriorated?

For a change to be considered reliable (better or worse), the change score must exceed the RCI value (i.e. ±1.96). Here, the sign of the reliable change index indicates whether the change is positive/beneficial or negative/worse.[39] Classification of change compared to ±1.96 can be categorized as reliable improvement (RC+), uncertain/indeterminate change (RC0), or reliable deterioration (RC−).[30,38] It should be noted that some researchers, including neuropsychologists, often use ±1.645 as a criterion, which is less strict than ±1.96.[40]

In almost all cases where change is measured, the direction of change can be determined by theory. For instance, in the present study, a higher change score indicates improvement and clinical improvement in the measured variable. Increased raw scores should be the result of effective psychotherapy when measuring marital adjustment before and after IBCT is measured. Calculating the clinical cutoff line

After calculating the RCI, it is necessary to establish a clinical cutoff line. This point will determine whether the clinical significance of change has happened. Following completion of treatment, if a patient’s score in the treatment outcome measure has changed to the extent that their classification shifts from “people with dysfunction due to disorder/disease” to “people with normal functioning,” they can be categorized as “Recovered.” As per Jacobsen and colleagues’ definition, this indicates a clinically significant change. Discussion

This study aims to explore the clinical significance of changes in marital adjustment following IBCT. By examining whether these changes are meaningful to participants, we categorized outcomes into four distinct groups: recovery, improvement, no reliable change, and deterioration. This classification provides a more comprehensive view of the impact of IBCT on marital adjustment.

Findings indicate that 63.3% of participants showed reliable improvement (RC+), 33.3% exhibited indeterminate change (RC0), and 3.3% experienced reliable deterioration (RC−). Additionally, based on the clinical cutoff for entering the nonpatient distribution, 46.7% of couples transitioned into the nonpatient category, 43.3% remained in the patient distribution, 6.7% stayed in the nonpatient category, and 3.3% entered the patient distribution post intervention.

Combining RCI and the cutoff line analysis, the data revealed that 46.7% of couples were treated or recovered, 16.7% showed improvement, 33.3% remained unchanged, and 3.3% deteriorated after IBCT. These results emphasize that while a majority recovered or improved, some participants did not benefit, highlighting the importance of individualized assessments. Although 63.4% of participants recovered (1, 2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, and 28.) and improved (4, 6, 11, 17, and 26), in practice, not all clients improve; some experience no change (5, 13, 15, 16, 23, 24, 25, 27, 29, and 30) or even deterioration (number 3). Therefore, the clinical significance can individually identify each participant’s status and provide more comprehensive information about treatment outcomes.

Limitations and recommendations

This study offers a detailed analysis of clinical significance, providing a deeper and more nuanced understanding of treatment outcomes compared to traditional statistical methods. By focusing on individual participants, we were able to identify specific outcomes, thereby enriching the overall findings. However, the quantitative nature of the clinical significance assessment does not fully capture the subjective experiences of participants. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of treatment effectiveness, future research should integrate qualitative methods that align clinical outcomes with clients’ perceptions. This would provide a holistic view of how interventions like IBCT are experienced by individuals.

One major limitation of this study was the restricted age range of participants. Due to time and resource constraints, the sample size could not be expanded to include a broader age spectrum, particularly older couples. Additionally, the maximum duration of marriage for participants was limited to 7 years. Couples with longer marriages, who may require more extensive therapeutic interventions, were not included in this study. Consequently, the results may not fully reflect the needs of couples with more complex relationship dynamics or longer histories.

Furthermore, this study did not include a follow-up phase, which would have helped to mitigate potential biases in participants’ responses. Without a follow-up, it was not possible to assess the long-term effects of IBCT. The absence of follow-up data limits our ability to draw conclusions about the sustainability of treatment outcomes. Given the limited sample size, the findings should be cautiously generalized to other couples, although they may be applicable to those meeting the study’s inclusion criteria.

The IBCT can also be effective for informal romantic relationships and same-sex couples. Future research should explore the impact of IBCT on these populations. Additionally, studies incorporating larger sample sizes and follow-up periods (ranging from 2 to 6 months) would provide more robust data on the long-term effectiveness of IBCT. Future studies could also investigate the effects of IBCT on older couples with longer marriages as well as explore the potential impact of IBCT on other relevant variables, such as individual mental health or family dynamics.

Conclusion

This study investigates the clinical significance of changes in marital adjustment following IBCT, providing more detailed insights than traditional statistical methods. Our findings indicate that 63.3% of participants experienced reliable improvement, 33.3% showed indeterminate change, and 3.3% deteriorated. These results emphasize the importance of using clinical significance to understand individual treatment responses, rather than relying solely on group-based statistical outcomes.

From a health policy perspective, integrating clinical significance measures into routine assessments of therapeutic interventions is crucial for a more personalized approach to treatment. This would allow clinicians and policymakers to better understand and address the diverse outcomes of therapy, leading to more tailored interventions that improve individual and couple’s wellbeing.

Future studies should explore the long-term effects of IBCT with follow-up periods of 2–6 months to assess the sustainability of treatment outcomes. Research should also include larger and more diverse samples, such as older couples and those with longer marriages, to better understand how IBCT impacts different demographic groups. Additionally, qualitative methods should be incorporated to capture clients’ subjective experiences and offer a more holistic view of treatment effectiveness. Finally, investigating predictors of long-term success in IBCT could help refine therapeutic techniques for sustained marital satisfaction.
