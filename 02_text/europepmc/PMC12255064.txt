ABSTRACT
Given the wide gap in cultural values and practices between Indian and Western cultural contexts, there is a need for culturally validated measurement instruments in couple and family relationships for use in India. To that end, the current study aimed to translate the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS) into Tamil and examine its psychometric properties and validity in a sample of married couples from India. Using standard dyadic design—data were collected from both husbands and wives—this study involved 340 married heterosexual couples (N = 680) in India. The confirmatory factor analysis results indicated that both the first- and second-order, four-factor structures with 21 items of the Tamil version of the DAS (DAS-T) fit the data well. Additionally, the multigroup analysis indicated that the factor structure, item loadings, item means, and residual variances were invariant across the dyad (husbands and wives), type of marriage (love and arranged), and type of family (nuclear and joint). The DAS-T also showed satisfactory convergent and divergent validity with other constructs, such as dyadic coping, communication patterns, and relationship satisfaction. In addition to research on marital adjustment in Indian context, the DAS-T can be used in counseling settings involving marital and romantic relationships.

BODY
In India, marriage is viewed as a sacred institution [1, 2], and most marriages are arranged by parents and elderly family members based on caste and religious match [3]. Marriage is considered a commitment that binds two individuals in holy matrimony while both families bond together [4]. Compared to couples in the Western cultural context, which are guided by personal choices and individual freedom, married couples in India endorse the traditional and collectivistic gender roles that promote interdependence among couples [5]. Indian cultural values emphasize relational hierarchy and collective efforts for couples in managing their relationships and conflicts [6, 7]. Marital relationships are sustained by firm commitment, pragmatism, and institutional partnerships [8]. On the other hand, couples from the Western cultural context emphasize individual efforts, attachment and commitment with their partner and eventually embark on a stay/leave decision process [7].

Although the marital relationships and dynamics of interactions among couples differ greatly between Western and non-Western couples—especially in India—most empirical research on marital relationships and functioning has been reported in Western cultural contexts. Nevertheless, the few available empirical examinations of marital relationships among Indian couples were based on measurement instruments developed in the Western cultural context—mostly in North America and Europe—without testing their cross-cultural equivalence. Cross-cultural researchers have warned against such a ‘transport and test’ approach (see [9]), as it might lead to incorrect inferences due to the cultural nonequiavalnce of both the construct and measurement. Given the need for culturally validated measurement instruments and the need for a measure of marital adjustment that works in the Indian cultural context, the present study evaluated the psychometric properties of the DAS translated into Tamil and tested its validity in a sample of married Indian couples.

Nature of marital adjustment

Marital satisfaction refers to how satisfied the husband or wife is in their married life [10, 11], whereas marital adjustment refers to a broader range of factors predicting a successful marriage, including satisfaction [12]. Therefore, researchers consider marital adjustment to be a dynamic process rather than a constant steady state [11, 13]. Multiple perspectives have been followed in delineating the components of marital adjustment by various researchers. Spanier [11] and Busby et al. [13] evaluated marital adjustment by the degree of (a) problematic dyadic differences, (b) personal anxiety and tensions, (c) cohesion, (d) agreement or consensus on crucial issues and (d) satisfaction of both spouses, which are the important factors for better adjustment and dyadic functioning. Similarly, Spanier and Lewis [14] illustrated three vital factors namely, personal and social resources, lifestyle satisfaction and rewards that predict marital satisfaction from marital/couple interactions. In addition, Bradbury et al. [15] reported that the following components contribute to or predict marital/dyadic adjustment: psychological (e.g., attitudes, personality traits, beliefs), societal (e.g., childbearing, delay in marriage), sociodemographic (qualification, religion, caste, age), health (physical and mental) and parenting practices. They also stated that the age difference between the couple, number of offspring, marriage duration, maturity, and religiosity were predictors of marital adjustment [15].

Significance of marital adjustment

Happiness and satisfaction in married life have been linked to many positive life outcomes. Past researches have shown that marital adjustment among couples is associated with physical wellness [16], mental health [17, 18], quality of life (e.g., [19, 20]), subjective well-being (e.g.,[21]), parenting [22–24] and child development [25, 26]. Family researchers have identified specific factors that influence marital satisfaction among couples, such as work-role demands, gender-specific roles, conflict management, sexual relationships [27], financial management, leisure activities, relationships with in-laws, and childbearing and rearing [28]. These psychological, social, and demographic factors influence marital outcomes directly or indirectly.

Measurement of marital adjustment

The quality of relationships and interactions among couples can profoundly impact their marital functioning and well-being [11]. Given that these factors are important for investigating couple and family relationships, the issues faced by researchers in this field are how best to globally assess marriage, its quality, and dynamics. Despite many limitations, self-report instruments are widely used for assessing marital adjustment [29].

The most frequently used instruments are the Marital Assessment Test [30], Dyadic Adjustment Scale [31], Quality of Marriage Index [32], Couples Satisfaction Index [33] and Marital Satisfaction Scale [34]. The Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS) by Spanier [31] was recognized to be the most popular and extensively used scale for research purposes [35]. Graham et al. [36] noted that “the DAS is perhaps the most widely used measure of relationship quality in the social and behavioral sciences literature” (p. 701).

The dyadic adjustment scale

Dyadic adjustment is defined as “a process, the outcome of which is determined by the degree of [1] troublesome dyadic differences; [2] interpersonal tensions and personal anxiety; [3] dyadic satisfaction; [4] dyadic cohesion and; [5] consensus on matters of importance to dyadic functioning” [11, p. 17]. The original Dyadic adjustment scale is a self-report measure developed by Spanier in 1976. It consists of 32 items with four factors measuring: dyadic consensus (13 items), dyadic satisfaction (10 items), dyadic cohesion (5 items) and affective expression (4 items). In addition to its extensive use in family psychology research, the DAS has been used in clinical and counseling settings to identify poorly adjusted couples [37]. Subscales of the DAS are used to determine the best choice of therapy or intervention and are used as a pretest assessment [38]. The DAS, which was originally developed in English, was revised to an abbreviated form with 14 items (RDAS: 13). Additionally, several studies have adapted the DAS in different languages and cultural settings (see Table 1) with established validity and reliability [35, 39].

Content Validity

Despite extensive research on the psychometric properties of the DAS, its dimensionality is inconclusive [51, 52]. Consequently, researchers have questioned whether the overall factor structure of the DAS is unidimensional, multidimensional, or hierarchical, as several studies have reported different results, as shown in Table 1. Although the DAS has been translated to various languages, the English version is widely used for studying dimensionality and validation [52]. Given that the overall factor structure of the DAS is inconclusive and that there is a need for cultural validation in the Indian context, in the present study, we aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the DAS-T for use among Indian couples.

The current study

Cross-cultural studies have indicated that couples from Indian and Western cultural contexts differ in terms of many characteristics of marital relationships, such as communication patterns, adjustment, conflict in relationships, in various psychological processes, such as interpersonal communication, attention, attribution perception, self-regulation, self-worth, and thinking [53]. Given this trend, research involving couples should use culturally validated measures. Despite cultural differences, studies validating the DAS in various non-Western cultural contexts (e.g., Chinese, Arabic, Iranian) have demonstrated its broad applicability and ability to capture key aspects of relationship dynamics across diverse cultures. However, the DAS has not been tested in the Indian cultural context. To that end, in the current study, we aimed to translate and validate the DAS in Tamil, a widely spoken language in southern India and Asia [54], and to establish the construct validity and reliability of the translated scale. In addition, we conducted a measurement invariance analysis to examine the equivalence of the psychometric properties across dyads (husbands and wives), marriage types (love and arranged), and family types (nuclear and joint). Based on prior validations (e.g., 45), we hypothesized that the DAS-T would show a second-order four-factor structure. In addition, we expected that the DAS-T would show measurement invariance across dyads, type of marriage and family type.

Method

Participants and procedure

Married heterosexual couples residing together for a minimum of one year were recruited from a few towns of the Tamil Nadu and Pondicherry regions in southern India as part of a larger study on marital relationships in India. The Institute Ethics Committee of the University approved the study, informed consent was collected from all the participants. Details of participant recruitment and procedure followed are similar as reported in the previous related work titled “Psychometric properties of the interpersonal emotion regulation questionnaire among couples in India” by Kanth and Indumathy [55],.

Approximately 740 married individuals (370 couples) agreed to participate in the study. Among the 370 questionnaire booklets distributed, 340 were completed and returned. The mean age of the men was 39.57 years (SD = 6.10), and that of the women was 35.33 years (SD = 5.72). The mean age of men at the time of marriage was 27.70 years (SD = 0.3.72; 17 ≥ range ≤ 38), and that of women was 23.55 years (SD = 3.43; 15 ≥ range ≤ 36). The range of the participants for years of marriage was 5–38, with a mean of 11.81 (SD = 4.87) for men and 11.87 (SD = 4.95) for women, with an overall mean of 11.84 (SD = 4.91). The majority of the participants were from nuclear families (402 (59.12%)), followed by joint family couples (267 (39.12%); χ2(df) = 27.69** [1]. Most of them had done an arranged marriage (443 (65.15%)), and others had love marriage (223 (32.79%), (χ2(df) = 72.67** [1]). Among the 680 married individuals, 299 (43.97%) had one child, 343 (50.44%) had two, 22 (3.24%) had three chidren and 16 (2.35%) did not specify. While all the participants were educated, among which 288 (42.35%) had single career (either of the spouse worked) and 378 (55.59%) both husband and wife were working.

Measures

Dyadic adjustment scale (DAS)

We translated the English version of the DAS [11] into Tamil, one of the scheduled languages spoken predominantly in southern India [54]. We translated the DAS into Tamil following the translation procedures recommended by Sireci et al. [56]. The scale was first translated from English to Tamil by a native bilingual expert. Then, the translation of the Tamil version of the DAS was verified by two psychologists who were fluent in Tamil as well as English and one language expert who knew both languages. The finalized Tamil version of the DCI was then back-translated into English by a researcher and checked again for similarity.

The 32-item DAS assesses dyadic cohesion (13 items), cohesion (5 items), satisfaction (10 items), and affectional expression (4 items) (e.g., “Do you confide in your mate?”). Depending on the nature of the item, the responses are recorded on a rating scale with different options (5—Always agree to 0—Always disagree; 0—All the time to 5—Never; 0—Never to 5—More often), except for two dichotomous items with yes/no type alternatives [29, 30]. The reliability of the subscales across genders was within the acceptable range of 0.61 0.91.

Dyadic coping inventory (DCI)

The Tamil version of the DCI [57] was used. The 29-item DCI is designed to assess self- (12 items) and partner coping behavior (12 items) and common DC (5 items) (e.g., “We try to cope with the problem together and search for ascertained solutions”). The participants rated their responses on a five-point Likert scale (1 = “very rarely” to 5 = “very often”). The inventory assesses positive coping, negative coping by self and partner coping and common dyadic coping. All subscales had an acceptable alpha coefficient ranging from 0.52 ≤ α ≥ 0.81.

Communication pattern questionnaire (CPQ; [58])

The scale consists of 23 items assessing how couples typically deal with problems in their relationship using three subscales: constructive communication, self-demand/partner withdrawal, and partner-demand/self-withdraw (e.g., Both my partner and I withdraw from each other). The items are rated on a 9-point Likert-type scale from “1 (very unlikely) to 9 (very likely)”. The Tamil version of the CPQ (Indumathy, 2021), which consists of 12 items in the same subscales, was used in the present study: constructive communication, 5 items; self-demand/partner withdrawal, 3 items; and partner demand/self-withdrawal, 4 items. In the current study, the alpha coefficients for overall communication patterns were 0.81 and 0.80 for husbands and wives, respectively.

Relationship satisfaction (RAS)

The RAS [59] measures how well the partner meets one’s needs and general relationships [satisfaction, relationship expectations, and love for a partner] and assesses the severity of problems in the relationship. The RAS is a 7-item self-report instrument that requires participants to rate their relationship using the five-point scale “Low Satisfaction [1], High Satisfaction [5]”. An example item is “How Well does your partner meet your needs?”. The score ranges from 7 to 35 after the reversal of two items. A higher score indicates a higher level of satisfaction. In the present study, we used the Tamil version of the RAS validated in an earlier study [57]. The internal consistency of the RAs in the present study was found to be 0.71.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 19) and IBM AMOS.

(version 19). Byrne (2013) suggested a kurtosis value of ± 3 for a normal distribution. The kurtosis and skewness of the variables fell within the range between ± 1. Thus all the study variables and their dimensions were normally distributed.

Descriptive statistics and intercorrelations

The mean, standard deviation, and reliability for all the scales were computed. Since dyadic data are interdependent (nonindependent) in nature, a paired t test was employed to assess gender differences. An independent sample t test was employed for the differences across the types of marriages and family types.

Factorial structure

To find the best fitting model, we performed confirmatory factor analysis for husbands and wives separately. We tested both the first-order and second-order factor structures for the data to compare and identify the best fitting model. We used the following cutoff values for the model fit indices: comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.08 [60]. We used a robust maximum likelihood estimator to handle the nonnormality of the data.

Measurement invariance (MI)

We evaluated MI to determine whether the first-order and second-order structures held between family type, and type of marriage. Since the data are dyadic by nature, dyadic invariance was also employed. We tested the multigroup CFA model to determine the configural, metric, scalar, and unique invariance. The models were examined using chi-square, CFI, RMSEA and SRMR values [60]. First, configural invariance was tested when the factor structure was constrained to be equal, but none of the other parameters were not restricted. Next, the factor loadings were constrained to be equal to assess metric invariance. Third, scalar invariance was tested by restricting the intercepts to be equal, and last, unique invariance was tested across groups by constraining the error terms or residuals to be equal. We followed the cutoff values by Chen [61] to evaluate metric, scalar, and unique invariance models across groups: Δ CFI ≤ 0.010, Δ SRMR ≤ 0.030, and Δ RMSEA ≤ 0.015 (N > 300).

Construct validity

To test the construct validity of the DAS-T, we examined its correlations with constructs such as dyadic coping, communication patterns, and relationship satisfaction.

Measurement invariance

A measurement invariance test was performed for the data across the couple/dyad, type of marriage, and family type. For both the first- and second-order factor structure, invariance analysis was employed. All the invariance models across genders showed adequate fit to the data (Δ CFI - ≤ 0.10 or Δ SRMR - ≤ 0.30 or Δ RMSEA - ≤ 0.10) and were within the acceptable range given by Chen [61]. The fit indices for both the first- and second-order models did not differ widely.

Dyadic invariance

Invariance analysis was employed across husbands (n = 340) and wives (n = 340) for the data. The four-factor structure showed full configural, metric, scalar, and uniqueness variance (see Table 6). All the indices were within the critical values given by Chen [61]. Thus, the results indicate that the couple, both the husband and wife, responded to the items identically.

The dyadic coping inventory, communication pattern questionnaires, and relationship satisfaction scale were used to assess convergent and divergent validity. Table 3 shows the correlation coefficients of the DAS with the dimensions of dyadic coping (positive, common, and negative), communication practices (constructive communication, self-demand/partner withdrawal, and partner demand/self-withdrawal) and the unidimensional relationship satisfaction scale across couples (Husband = 0.11 ≤ r ≥.83; Wife = 0.11 ≤ r ≥.79). From the results, it is clear that two dimensions of dyadic coping (common and positive), constructive communication and relationship satisfaction, have a significant positive relationship with all the dimensions of dyadic adjustment indicating convergent validity. While, negative dyadic coping and negative communication patterns (self-demand/partner withdrawal and partner demand/self-withdrawal) have a significant negative relationship with all the dimensions of the DAS across husbands and wives indicating divergent validity. Furthermore, in terms of dyadic consensus, affectionate expression was not related to negative communication patterns for wives, while for husbands, it was not related to constructive communication.

Discussion

The primary objective of the study was to establish the psychometric properties of the translated Tamil version of the dyadic adjustment scale (DAS), a widely used scale to assess marital quality [11] and couple satisfaction [45], among couples. The translation and validation of the scale could be useful for researchers and clinicians to use this scale among married couples residing in India. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis indicated that both the first and hierarchical or second-order factor structures for the dyadic adjustment scale fit the data well. Both the models seemed to have an acceptable fit with references to the critical values of CFI, RMSEA and SRMR [60]. Although there are several criticisms on the factor structure that are consistently debated, our results are in accordance with a few studies that demonstrated a hierarchical factor structure [43, 47, 52, 62, 63] and first-order factor structure [45, 46, 50]. Although the overall factor structure of the Tamil version was similar and in accordance with previous validations of other languages—Chinese, Roman, and Persian—significant differences evolved concerning the number of items removed. Particularly from the subscale satisfaction, seven items—18, 19, 21, 22, 23, 31, and 32—were removed, while one item from consensus (item 3), one item from cohesion (item 24), and two dichotomous items from affectional expression (items 29 and 30) were removed due to weak loadings. Thus, the results provide empirical evidence that the Tamil version of the DAS is a valid measure for assessing marital quality /adjustment among married Indian couples.

Very few studies have established construct validity for the translated version of the DAS [35, 62–65]. In the current study, to establish construct validity, the correlations of the DAS and its subscales with communication patterns, relationship satisfaction, and dyadic coping were computed.

The DAS was positively associated with relationship satisfaction, constructive communication, supportive dyadic coping, and common dyadic coping, which is in accordance with prior literature [48, 52]. These results indicate and establish convergent validity. While the negative relationship between dyadic adjustment and negative dyadic coping and negative communication patterns among couples indicate divergent validity. Thus the dyadic adjustment scale indicated to have construct validity for the sample in the Indian context. The dyadic consensus refers to the degree of agreement between the couple and denotes a positive interaction among couples whereas self-demand etc. patterns are negative strategies., which explains lack of divergent validity between them.

For the DAS, multigroup analysis was computed at four different levels—factor structure (configural), factor loadings (metric), intercepts (scalar), and residuals (uniqueness). The analysis was employed across dyad (husbands and wives), marriage type (love and arranged), and family type (nuclear and joint). The results of the analyses indicated that the Tamil version of the DAS was similar across dyad, marriage type, and family type. This indicated that Indian husbands and wives, love and arranged marriage couples, and nuclear and joint families reported similar levels of consensus, cohesion, satisfaction, and affection expression. Overall, the results of the measurement invariance analysis will enable future researchers to freely use the scores of the DAS-T for comparison across groups (e.g., husbands vs. wives).

Implications and strengths

Psychometric assessment plays a crucial role in couple and family psychology research and practice. The current study is among the few to translate and validate a measurement instrument developed in Western cultural contexts for use in Indian couples. Thus, the validation of the DAS Tamil would help psychologists and mental health workers dealing with married couples obtain a better understanding of their quality of marital life. The DAS can also be useful for assessing the efficacy of marital education programs conducted to enhance marital functioning among couples. It can also be used to screen and identify areas of marital dysfunction among couples who seek marital therapy and counseling. The DAS scale and its validation serves as an example to understand and validate assessment tools in different cultural context for better reliable results. This scale may also help researchers in doing cross-cultural comparisions in marital relationships.

There are certain strengths of the study. First, although the DAS is widely used in the field of marital relationships, to our knowledge, this is the first study to translate and validate the DAS for the Indian population, despite the slowly developing research in the couple and family context [66]. Second, the use of a dyadic dataset is a unique contribution to this area of study. Third, the recruitment of a larger non-clinical sample makes the results more reliable and valid for providing empirical evidence. Finally, the construct validity of the DAS is well established through comparisons with three different measures that are also extensively used in couple and family research.

Limitations and future directions

Our study has a few limitations that cannot be overlooked. First, the study followed a cross-sectional research design, which limits testing of the scale reliability across time. Future studies might use a longitudinal design to evaluate the temporal validity of the DAS-T. Second, the present study involved Tamil-speaking couples in India only, Tamil speaking couples of Sri Lanka were not a part of this study. Although cultural contexts involving marriage and marital relationships are common across India, subcultural changes within the Indian cultural context cannot be overlooked. Hence, future research might focus on validating the DAS in other Indian languages as well. India is a diverse country, with more than 20 official languages spoken and recognized [67]. The scale have resulted differently across different cultures serving as an evidence for the need of cultural validations of the scale. Last, as the participants in the present study were couples from the general population, the generalization of the results to the clinical population needing therapy should be considered cautiously.
